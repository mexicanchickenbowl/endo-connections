# 25 — Outcome numbers worth memorizing (NSRCT, ReTx, apical surgery) — evidence brief

Research stage only (nothing built). Compiled 2026-11-22 from PubMed (abstracts read for every paper cited;
PMC full text read for Burns 2022 and Pinto 2020). "Verified from" says exactly what was read. Anything recalled
from memory but not found in an abstract/full text is marked **NOT VERIFIED** and must stay off screen.

**Fact-checked 2026-11-22 (independent second pass).** Every row in section (a) and every on-screen fact in
section (b) was re-read against the PubMed record (abstract) by a second reviewer; Burns 2022 (PMC9322405) and
Pinto 2020 (PMC7558840) were re-read in PMC full text. All eight headline numbers held. What changed: three
on-screen tags, three on-screen options withdrawn, one wrong claim ("the only RCT"), one wrong denominator
(Lazarski 2001) and several added caveats. Items edited by the fact-check are marked **[FC]**; the full list is in
the "Fact-check log" at the end of this file.

Attribution: bibliographic data and abstracts were retrieved from PubMed; DOIs are given for every source.

**The one rule that governs this reel:** every number is a different *kind* of number. Three outcome families are
in play and they must never be mixed in one column without a tag:

| Family | What it means | Typical source |
|---|---|---|
| **Success / healed — strict** | no apical radiolucency + no signs/symptoms (complete healing; Strindberg-type) | Ng 2007/2008, Burns 2022, Sabeti 2024 (all define "strict" in the abstract). **[FC]** Sjögren 1990 is usually described as strict, but its abstract does not state the criteria — do not print "STRICT" next to a Sjögren number. |
| **Success — loose** | lesion reduced in size ("healing") also counts | Ng 2007, Burns 2022, Sabeti 2024 |
| **Survival / retained / functional** | tooth still present (± symptom-free), regardless of the radiograph | Salehrabi & Rotstein 2004/2010, Ng 2010, Lazarski 2001, Chen 2007 |

Apical surgery uses its own radiographic scale (Rud 1972 / Molven 1987: complete, incomplete = scar, uncertain,
unsatisfactory); "success" in the surgery meta-analyses is **not** the same thing as "strict" in the NSRCT reviews.
So the column header "SUCCESS (strict)" is correct for the two nonsurgical rows only — see Corrections, item 1.

## (c) Corrections to the brief

1. **Column header.** "SUCCESS (strict)" fits primary RCT and retreatment (Burns 2022, Ng 2008). It does not fit the
   surgery row: Setzer 2010 reports "weighted pooled success"/"positive outcome"; in that literature incomplete
   (scar) healing is normally counted as success (Rud/Molven classes), and this was not confirmed in the Setzer full
   text. Use a header "SUCCESS" with a criteria tag per cell, or keep "(strict)" only on the two nonsurgical rows.
2. **Ng 2007 "about 75% strict / 85% loose".** The abstract says the pooled strict success "ranged between 68% and
   85%" (treatments completed at least 1 year before review). The 74.7% (40 studies) and 85.2% (36 studies) means
   are full-text figures, verified here only as quoted in Burns 2022 (which itself writes 85.2% in the introduction
   and 85.3% in the discussion). Fine for the caption as "about 75% / 85%"; on screen prefer Burns 2022.
3. **Burns 2022.** Confirmed: 82.0% strict (79.3–84.8), 92.6% loose (90.5–94.8); first author Lorel E. Burns; Int
   Endod J 2022;55:714-731. Inclusion was at least 12 months of follow-up. Most studies used periapical radiographs.
4. **Ng 2010 survival "86–93% over 2–10 years".** Correct as the abstract's summary, but it is not a decline curve:
   86% at 2–3 yr, 93% at 4–5 yr, 87% at 8–10 yr, each pooled from different studies. Never animate it as a
   time series.
5. **Salehrabi & Rotstein 2004.** Confirmed: 97% retained at 8 yr; 1,462,936 teeth in 1,126,288 patients. It is
   retention without a claimed untoward event (retreatment, surgery, extraction = 3% combined) in an insurance
   database — not healing. Tag it "retained", never "success".
6. **Sjögren 1990.** The abstract says success "exceeded 96%" for teeth without a periapical radiolucency (vital or
   nonvital) — write ">96%". 86% (necrotic with lesion) and 62% (retreatment of teeth with a lesion) confirmed;
   356 patients, 8–10 yr. The analysis unit (roots) is not stated in the abstract.
7. **Toronto Study.** de Chevigny 2008 (Phase 4, pooled Phases 1–4): 86% healed overall; 93% without vs 82% with a
   radiolucency; 4–6 yr. The brief's "healed/functional rates": the functional rate is not in the abstract (NOT
   VERIFIED). "Healed" in Toronto means PAI-based absence of apical periodontitis plus no signs/symptoms; recall was
   low (32% in Phase 4).
8. **Ng 2008 retreatment "pooled about 77%".** Confirmed: 76.7% by complete healing and 77.2% by incomplete healing.
   The abstract prints the strict CI as 73.6–89.6% (asymmetric; probably a typo) — leave CIs off.
9. **Gorni & Gagliani 2004.** Confirmed: 69.03% overall; 86.8% respected vs 47% altered; 452 teeth; 24 months only.
10. **Toronto retreatment.** Phases 1–4 pooled: 82% healed (187/229); 93% without vs 80% with radiolucency;
    perforation 56% vs 87% (de Chevigny 2008, J Endod 34:131-137). Phases 1–2: 81%; 97% vs 78% (Farzaneh 2004).
11. **Salehrabi & Rotstein 2010.** Confirmed: 89% retained at 5 yr; 4,744 teeth; retreatments by endodontists; 4% had
    apical surgery. (The abstract also says 11% were extracted.)
12. **Setzer 2010.** Confirmed: traditional 59% (12 studies, n = 925) vs microsurgery 94% (9 studies, n = 699),
    RR 1.58. These are pooled single-arm groups from different eras, with follow-up as short as 6 months (per Pinto
    2020), not randomized comparisons.
13. **Setzer 2012 ("magnification").** Confirmed: microinstruments with loupes/no magnification 88% vs
    microscope/endoscope 94%, RR 1.07; significant for molars only.
14. **Tsesis 2013.** Confirmed: 89.0% at 1-yr follow-up; microscope/endoscope better than loupes; MTA better. The
    2009 version reported 91.6% and no significant magnification effect.
15. **Kang 2015.** Confirmed: microsurgery 92% vs retreatment 80%; journal is Clin Oral Investig (2015;19:569-582),
    not J Endod; the difference was significant only under 4 years of follow-up.
16. **Torabinejad 2009.** Confirmed: 2–4 yr surgery 77.8% vs retreatment 70.9%; 4–6 yr retreatment 83.0% vs surgery
    71.8%; surgery 62.9% at 6+ yr. Largely pre-microsurgery studies.
17. **Rubinstein & Kim 1999/2002.** The 2002 paper (PMID 12026924) is verified: 91.5% (54/59 roots) remained healed
    5–7 yr later, and it restates the 1999 result (96.8% healed within 1 yr). The 1999 paper could not be located in
    PubMed with the tools used — cite the 2002 paper for both numbers.
18. **Not in the brief but better/more recent:** Sabeti 2024 (contemporary retreatment 79% strict / 88% loose);
    Pinto 2020 (long-term microsurgery); Kohli 2018 (Part 3 of the Setzer series); Bucchi 2023 and Del Fabbro 2016
    (no clear superiority of surgery vs retreatment); Patel 2012 (CBCT reads lower).

## (d) Controversies and caveats (what must not be overstated)

- **Success, survival and "functional" answer different questions.** A tooth can be retained and symptom-free with a
  persistent radiolucency. Survival figures (89–97% in the studies on the board) are typically higher than strict
  success (77–82% on the board; about 75% in Ng 2007).
- **Strict vs loose.** Loose criteria add roughly 10 points (Burns 2022: 82% vs 93%; Sabeti 2024: 79% vs 88%).
  Toronto's "healed" (PAI ≤ 2) is a third definition. **[FC]** The 10-point gap is not universal: in Ng 2008 the
  pooled rates were 76.7% (complete healing) and 77.2% (incomplete healing), because each is pooled from a
  different subset of studies. Do not state "loose is always about 10 points higher".
- **Radiograph vs CBCT.** All headline numbers are periapical-radiograph based. With CBCT the same teeth score much
  lower (Patel 2012: 87% vs 62.5% at 1 yr; Burns 2022: 29.8% in the single CBCT-only study).
- **Has primary RCT improved?** Ng 2007: "had not improved over the last four (or five) decades". Burns 2022:
  "reported success rates show improvement over time" (82% vs about 75%). Different study pools, criteria reporting
  and eras; 64% of Burns' included studies were graded low-quality evidence.
- **Pooled estimates hide heterogeneity.** Ng 2007 individual studies: 31–96% strict. Burns 2022: 29.8–97.3% strict.
- **Recall bias.** Toronto recall rates were about 32–50% (as reported in the abstracts); long-term surgery cohorts lose 12–40%. Missing teeth are not
  missing at random.
- **Insurance-claims survival** (Salehrabi & Rotstein, Lazarski, Chen): very large, but no radiographs, events only
  if claimed within the plan, and patients who leave the plan are invisible. The abstract describes an 8-yr assessment
  period; whether every tooth was followed for the full 8 years was not verified.
- **Is retreatment worse than primary treatment?** Not when stratified by periapical status (Ng 2011: 83% vs 80%;
  Friedman & Mor 2004: "no systematic difference"). It is worse when a lesion is present and anatomy was altered
  (Sjögren 62%; Gorni 47%).
- **Microsurgery 94% is a short-term pooled figure.** Long-term cohorts are lower (von Arx 2012: 84% → 76% from 1 to
  5 yr; Pinto 2020: 78% in prospective cohorts vs 91% in RCTs; von Arx 2019: 81.5% at 10 yr with MTA). Cochrane 2016
  found no RCT evidence that magnification type changes healing. **[FC]** Its minimum follow-up was 6 months (per
  Pinto 2020), and the Setzer abstract states neither follow-up nor success criteria. Other pooled microsurgery
  estimates are lower: 92% (Kang 2015), 89.0% at 1 yr (Tsesis 2013), 84.5% (Sabeti 2023, 19 studies). The 94% is
  reproduced only within the same Penn series (Kohli 2018: 94.42%). Pinto's 91% "RCT" arm is itself inflated by a
  successes-only follow-up (Song 2012), so 78% from the prospective cohorts is the more sober long-term figure.
- **59% vs 94% is not a trial.** It compares pooled case series from different decades, operators, case selection and
  root-end materials (amalgam era vs Super-EBA/IRM/MTA).
- **Surgery vs retreatment.** Surgery heals faster; by about 4 years the difference disappears or reverses in older
  data (Torabinejad 2009; Kvist & Reit 1999; Kang 2015). Cochrane 2016 and Bucchi 2023: no clear superiority.
  **[FC]** The randomized evidence is two small trials (Danin 1996, 38 patients, 1 yr; Kvist & Reit 1999, 95 teeth,
  4 yr, with 10-yr survival in Riis 2018), both with traditional surgical techniques and rated high risk of bias
  by Cochrane.
- **Unit of analysis.** Roots (Ng 2011, Rubinstein & Kim) vs teeth vs patients; root-level rates look better.
- **[FC] Apical surgery: no pooled survival figure could be verified here.** Pinto 2020's 79–100% is a range
  across ten studies, calculated by the reviewers. Torabinejad 2015 calculated a weighted survival rate for
  microsurgery, but its abstract prints no number. So the board should show nothing in that cell — and should not
  claim that no such data exist.
- **[FC] Follow-up tags are inclusion minimums, not typical follow-up.** "1 YR+" (Burns: studies ran from 12 months
  to 19 years) and "6 MO+" (Ng 2008; Setzer 2010) say how short a study could be and still be pooled.

- Do not present 59% vs 94% as a head-to-head trial, or 94% as a long-term result.
- Do not say microsurgery beats retreatment (or the reverse) long-term.
- Do not say success of primary RCT "has not improved" or "has improved" as settled fact.
- Do not show Ng 2010's 86 / 93 / 87% as a survival curve.

- Do not give a PMID or page numbers for Rubinstein & Kim 1999, Strindberg 1956, or any AAE document.

- Do not use Huang 2020's 95.2% as "the" survival of apical surgery.
- No implant comparisons in this reel (Torabinejad 2007 and Iqbal & Kim 2007 say survival is similar, but that is a

  spinning flaps show blanks, dashes or scrambled letters rather than plausible-looking percentages.
