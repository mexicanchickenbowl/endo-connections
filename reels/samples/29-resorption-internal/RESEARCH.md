Resorption series 1/5. Research stage only (nothing built). Prepared 2026-11-22.

## (c) Corrections to the brief

1. **"Wedenberg and Lindskog 1985/1987"** are different papers, and a third is usually meant as well:
   - Wedenberg & Lindskog **1985**, Endod Dent Traumatol 1:221-7 — experimental internal resorption in monkey teeth
     (transient vs progressive). No abstract.
   - Wedenberg & Lindskog **1987**, Scand J Dent Res 95:205-11 — "Evidence for a resorption inhibitor in dentin"
     (rat macrophages in vitro; predentin resists cell spreading).
   - Wedenberg & **Zetterqvist** 1987, J Endod 13:255-9 — human teeth, histology/SEM/enzyme histochemistry. No abstract.
   Cite each for what it is; only the 1987 Scand J Dent Res paper has a readable abstract.
2. **"Inflamed/infected coronal pulp as the stimulus"** — the sources say **necrotic and infected** pulp tissue coronal to
   the lesion (bacteria in the canal and dentinal tubules). The *inflamed but vital* tissue is at and apical to the lesion.
   Label the coronal layer "necrotic, infected", not "inflamed".
3. **"Vital apical pulp bringing the blood supply and clastic cells"** — "blood supply / nutrients" is what the sources
   state (Tronstad via Patel 2022; Abbott & Lin 2022). That the clastic cells arrive through that blood supply is
   plausible (monocyte lineage) and is stated outright by Nilsson 2013 ("the blood supply brings the clastic cells";
   "coming from the apical vital part of the pulp") **[FC]**, but their origin is debated (Patel 2022 mentions an
   alternative periodontal origin).
   On screen write "blood supply"; cells entering from the apical side is fine as schematic animation.
4. **"Damaged predentin/odontoblast layer as the entry"** — more exactly: loss of the unmineralized layer **exposes
   mineralized dentin**, to which clastic cells can attach. The experimental support is in vitro (rat cells).
5. **"Pink spot when in the crown"** — true only for coronal lesions, described as **rare** (Patel 2022 Table 1), and a pink
   spot is not specific: it is also a sign of external cervical resorption, which Heithersay considered the more common
   source — **[FC]** but he wrote that about "internal replacement (invasive) resorption", not about the inflammatory type.
   Do not make it a recognition point; at most a small aside. (Pink spot in 5.1% of 215 external cervical resorption
   lesions: Patel et al. Int Endod J 2023;56:1475-87, PMID 37801348 — verified from the abstract.)
6. **Location.** Patel 2010 defines IRR as affecting "the middle and apical thirds of the canal walls", and Gabor 2012 found
   28 of 29 microscopic lesions there (15 middle third, 13 apical third, 1 coronal third). Draw the lesion **mid-root**, not in the crown.
   **[FC]** This is a drawing choice, not a rule: Patel 2022 says "IRR may occur anywhere within the root canal system"
   (Table 1: "Anywhere"); Abbott & Lin 2022 also illustrate a mid-root lesion. Never write "always mid-root".
7. **"Uniform, symmetrical ballooning"** — correct as the classic picture, but "the resorptive defect can be any shape or
   size" (Abbott & Lin 2022), and internal vs external cervical resorption "can be indistinguishable" on 2-D films
   (Patel 2022). Keep "classic" or "typically" in the wording or in the caption.
8. **"Canal outline continuous with / lost into the lesion"** — supported by three narrative reviews (Aidos 2018,
   Thomas 2014, Abbott & Lin 2022). Attribution of this particular sign to Gartner 1976 could not be checked.
9. **"Lesion does not move with a shifted angle"** — supported: Patel 2022 citing Gartner 1976. "Stays centered" is the
   wording of Patel's own figure legend. Fine as written.
10. **"CBCT to assess perforation"** — supported (Bhuva 2011; Patel 2009; Patel 2022). The AAE/AAOMR statement was
    **updated in 2025** (J Endod 2026;52:4-13); do not quote the 2015 sentence as current guidance. **[FC]** The reviews
    disagree on how necessary CBCT is — "essential" (Patel 2022) vs "not usually required" (Abbott & Lin 2022). The small
    on-screen `CBCT: perforated?` is fine; "CBCT needed" or "CBCT confirms" is not.
11. **"Root canal treatment arrests it"** — supported as the treatment of choice (Patel 2010; Fuss 2003; Nilsson 2013),
    but the clinical outcome evidence is one series of 20 teeth (Çalişkan & Türkün 1997). Say "stops / arrests";
    never "heals", "reverses" or "regrows".
12. **"Clastic cells lose their supply → resorption stops"** — correct. Add the corollary (caption): the lesion also stops
    by itself once the entire pulp is necrotic, so a tooth with an old defect and a negative pulp test is no longer
    resorbing — it still needs root canal treatment, for the infection.
13. **"Prevalence data"** — there is no reliable prevalence. Historic radiographic figures range from 1/1000 teeth (Thoma
    1935) to 51.5% of autotransplanted canines (Ahlberg 1983); the frequently repeated "0.01-1%" traces to Haapasalo &
    Endal 2006, which could not be read and is quoted inconsistently. Use "rare", no percentage.
14. **Haapasalo & Endal 2006** — Endodontic Topics 14:60-79 is not PubMed-indexed (Crossref DOI ends 2008.00226.x). Cite it
    only as a review in the caption, never for a number.
15. **Tronstad 1988 and Gartner et al. 1976** have no PubMed abstracts: cite them as the classics, with the wording taken
    from Patel 2022 / Lin 2022.
16. **Patel et al. 2010**: J Endod 2010;36(7):1107-21. The third author is printed "Durak" in this paper (Conor Durack).
17. **Internal replacement (metaplastic) resorption** — exists, described as "very rare" (Abbott & Lin 2022). Radiographic
    hint: irregular enlargement with a mottled/cloudy look. One small mention at most is right.
18. **Obturation** — the brief did not mention it; if the treatment beat shows a filled defect, "warm gutta-percha" is the
    supported label (Goldberg 2000; Gencoglu 2008 — in vitro). **[FC]** Only for injected or compacted warm gutta-percha:
    carrier-based heated obturators (Thermafil, Soft-Core) were the worst performers in both studies. With a perforation
    the calcium silicate cement goes *into* the defect (hybrid fill, gutta-percha apical to it).
19. **[FC] On-screen source lines.** The first draft printed `Tronstad 1988 · Patel et al. 2010 J Endod` and
    `Gartner et al. 1976 J Endod`. None of these was read for the claim it sat under (two have no abstract; Patel 2010 was
    read as an abstract that does not contain the claim). Replaced by the reviews that were read in full text — see (b).
20. **[FC] "Smooth".** Not a verified criterion — replaced by "well-defined" on screen.
21. **[FC] Etiology quote.** Abbott & Lin 2022 do not say most cases had "caries only"; they say caries alone or

## (d) Controversies and caveats (do not overstate)

- **Evidence level.** Almost everything is case reports, narrative reviews, small histologic series and in vitro work.
  No systematic review of IRR treatment was found; no clinical trial. Patel 2022: "minimal evidence on the long-term outcome".
- **What starts it.** Trauma is the traditional answer (and again the most frequently reported factor in a 2026 narrative
  review of 62 articles, Mercante et al., PMID 42434214); Abbott & Lin 2022 point out that most reported cases had caries
  — alone or combined with trauma or periodontal disease **[FC: not "caries only"]**.
  Many cases are labelled idiopathic. Herpes zoster has been proposed (systematic review of case reports: Jakovljevic A
  et al. J Endod 2017;43:1230-6, PMID 28662875 — 8 teeth with internal resorption in 7 patients; evidence level 4-5).
- **Why predentin protects.** "Resorption inhibitor in dentin" is Wedenberg's interpretation of in vitro cell-spreading
  experiments with rat macrophages/osteoclasts. Safe wording: "clastic cells do not colonize intact, unmineralized
  predentin (in vitro)". **[FC]** In the macrophage experiments the cells attached but did not spread, and they did spread
  on *infected* predentin (Wedenberg & Lindskog 1986) — so "predentin cannot be resorbed" would be wrong.
- **Origin of the resorbing tissue.** Wedenberg & Zetterqvist found the pulp replaced by periodontal-like connective tissue
  and could not tell whether that preceded the resorption. Pulpal origin vs ingrowth from the periodontium is unsettled.
- **Transient internal resorption.** Abbott & Lin 2022 classify a separate "internal surface resorption" (self-limiting,
  undetectable clinically; activity for 2-3 weeks if the stimulus does not persist). Patel 2022 calls the same idea the
  "transient phase". The reel shows the progressive (inflammatory) type only.
- **Inflammatory vs replacement: one disease or two?** Patel 2022: "More research is needed to appreciate and confirm that
  these conditions ... are similar." Management advice also differs: Patel treats both with root canal treatment;
  Abbott & Lin say replacement resorption is managed by observation and eventual extraction unless caught very early.
- **Does it always progress? Must it always be treated at once?** Standard teaching: treat when diagnosed, because
  progression is unpredictable and perforation worsens the prognosis. But lesions burn out when the pulp necroses,
  Nilsson 2013 lists monitoring as an option in the absence of signs and symptoms, and Patel 2022 illustrates an
  untreated post-pulpotomy lesion that was only reviewed. Do not write "always progresses".
- **2-D radiographic rules are fallible.** The parallax and canal-outline signs are classic teaching (1976) without a
  modern accuracy study. Patel 2009 found periapical accuracy "acceptable" (Az 0.78) but below CBCT (1.00) in 15 teeth.
- **CBCT.** Better than periapicals in small in vivo and ex vivo studies; one ex vivo study found the difference not
  significant (Madani 2016); voxel size matters (Kamburoğlu 2010). CBCT is an adjunct after clinical and periapical
  examination, not a screening tool. **[FC]** How necessary it is, is itself disputed: Patel 2022 calls a CBCT scan
  "essential" to assess the extent; Abbott & Lin 2022 say CT scans "can help differentiate, but they are not usually
  required". Current AAE/AAOMR (2025) and ESE (2023) wording on resorption was not read.
- **Medicament.** Patel 2022: hypochlorite, activation, calcium hydroxide. Abbott & Lin 2022 prefer a
  corticosteroid-antibiotic paste first when the resorption is active, then calcium hydroxide. No comparative data.
- **Obturation technique.** In vitro only, simulated cavities; the "best" technique differs between studies (Obtura II in
  Goldberg 2000; Microseal in Gencoglu 2008, where cold lateral compaction filled 92% of the area, mostly with sealer).
  **[FC]** Carrier-based warm techniques (Thermafil, Soft-Core) were the worst in both studies, so "warm is better" is true
  only for injected/compacted gutta-percha.
- **Perforating lesions.** MTA/Biodentine repair, hybrid fillings, surgery and regenerative procedures are all supported
  only by case reports; the one older series (Çalişkan 1997) had 3 of 4 failures with calcium hydroxide alone.
- **Prevalence.** "Rare" refers to the radiographically visible lesion. Microscopic lacunae are common in inflamed and
  necrotic pulps (Gabor 2012: 14 of 21) — which is why "rare" and "frequent" both appear in the literature.
- **[FC] Pulp testing.** Patel 2022 Table 1: positive in (partially) vital cases; Thomas 2014: a negative response is
  expected because the coronal pulp is necrotic; Nilsson 2013: positive until the lesion is large. No data. Off screen.
- **[FC] When do patients present?** Early/active lesions are silent (all reviews), but Abbott & Lin 2022 state that most
  patients only present once the resorption has stopped and apical periodontitis has developed. "Often silent", not "always".
- **[FC] "Irreversible".** True for the lost dentin (Patel 2022). Case reports of regenerative procedures describe new hard
  tissue and a smaller defect, and replacement-type lesions fill with bone-like tissue. Never show dentin regrowing, but
  do not write "nothing can ever form again".
- **[FC] Location.** "Middle and apical thirds" (Patel 2010 abstract) vs "anywhere within the root canal system"
  (Patel 2022). Thomas 2014 contradicts itself (mid/apical root in one paragraph, coronal start in another).

## (e) Do not claim

- Any prevalence percentage for clinical IRR ("0.01-1%", "1 in 1000", "2%").
- A success rate for treatment, or "always saves the tooth". **[FC]** Not even "16 of 16 non-perforating teeth healed": the
  abstract of the 1997 series gives no "all", no follow-up time and no criteria (caption wording at most: "healing
  reported in a 1997 series of 20 treated teeth").
- That the lost dentin grows back, the canal "shrinks back", or the lesion heals. It stops; the defect is filled.
- That trauma is *the* cause, or that a cause is always identifiable.
- That a pink tooth means internal resorption, or that internal resorption usually shows a pink spot.
- That a periapical radiograph (even with a cone shift) reliably separates internal from external cervical resorption.
- That CBCT is mandatory or "the standard of care" for every case, or any quoted AAE/AAOMR/ESE recommendation text.
- That the pulp is vital throughout (the coronal part is necrotic), or that the tooth always responds to pulp tests.
- That the coronal pulp is merely "inflamed" — it is necrotic and infected.
- That odontoclasts are a different cell from osteoclasts, or giant numbers of nuclei (they are small, typically <= 10 nuclei).
- That internal resorption is always a smooth oval (it "can be any shape or size"), or always in the crown/coronal third.
- That MTA/Biodentine repair of a perforating lesion has proven long-term success (case reports only).
- A growth rate or a time to perforation.
- Anything "according to Gartner / Tronstad / Wedenberg & Lindskog 1985 / Haapasalo & Endal" as a direct quotation.

- **[FC]** That the pulp test is positive (or negative) in internal resorption — the reviews contradict each other.
- **[FC]** That a pink spot "more often means external cervical resorption" as a finding about internal *inflammatory*
