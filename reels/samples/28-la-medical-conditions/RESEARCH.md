# 28 — Medical conditions and drugs that change your local anesthetic choice
Evidence brief (research stage). Prepared 2026-11-22. American spelling throughout.

**How this was verified.** Bibliographic data and abstracts come from PubMed (PMIDs and DOIs given). Where
"full text" is stated, the article or label was read through a web-fetch tool that returns short verbatim
quotes (<= 125 characters) plus paraphrase, or as a PDF read page by page (ACOG CO 569; Gomez-Moreno 2009).
Short quotes below are as returned; re-open the source before reproducing any sentence verbatim.
Malamed's *Handbook of Local Anesthesia* could **not** be opened here: every statement attributed to it is
marked "textbook, not read directly" and is corroborated by a source that was read wherever possible.
FDA labels were read on DailyMed. The FDA 2018 benzocaine communication page itself returned 404; its
content is taken from an AAFP news report of it plus search-result excerpts (flagged below).

**Evidence character of the whole topic.** Almost everything here is pharmacology + expert convention +
case reports + small hemodynamic studies. There are no outcome trials for most items. The reel must read
as a *conventional precautions* study card, not as trial-proven rules.

## (c) Corrections to the brief

1. **"0.04 mg per appointment (about 2 cartridges)"** — the number is the right *convention* (Becker & Reed: "most
   conservative and frequently cited"; Malamed textbook), but (i) it is expert convention, not an evidence-derived
   ceiling; (ii) Becker & Reed say it "more accurately reflects 30-minute time periods, not appointments" and call a
   rigid 2-cartridge limit "naive"; (iii) 2 cartridges of 1:100,000 are 0.034-0.036 mg, so write "about 2", never
   "= 2". Systematic reviews: 1-2 cartridges safe in controlled hypertension / coronary disease (Seminario-Amez 2021);
   up to 4 "relatively safe" (Godzieba 2014); GRADE low / very low (Guimaraes 2021).
2. **Bader 2002** is a systematic review restricted to *hypertensive* patients (6 studies), not cardiovascular
   disease in general. Correct citation: Oral Surg Oral Med Oral Pathol Oral Radiol Endod 2002;93:647-53.
3. **Brown & Rhodus 2005** (OOOOE 100:401-8, PMID 16182160) has no PubMed abstract and was not read: do not attach
   specific claims to it.
4. **Beta-blockers** — applies to *non-selective* agents only; cardioselective beta-1 blockers "do not generally
   introduce a risk". The interaction is "apparently rare in the dental setting" and dose-related; vasoconstrictors
   are not contraindicated. A red prohibition sign here would be wrong.
5. **Tricyclics** — "avoid levonordefrin / norepinephrine" is supported (animal: more than sixfold; human: 4-8x for
   norepinephrine, 2-4x for epinephrine). Norepinephrine is not in US dental cartridges, so on screen say
   levonordefrin. The popular numeric epinephrine cap (about 0.05 mg) could not be traced to a primary source read
   here: no number on screen.
6. **Cocaine / methamphetamine time window** — there is no single evidence-based figure. Read sources give 6-24 h
   (Brand 2008), "> 12 h" (Little & Falace via Nassar 2020) and ">= 48 h" (Gomez-Moreno 2009); the familiar "24 h"
   could not be verified to a paper. Yagiela's abstract speaks of cocaine *intoxication*. On screen: "recent use".
7. **Hyperthyroidism / pheochromocytoma** — applies to *uncontrolled / untreated* disease. Controlled patients and
   patients on levothyroxine are treated normally; Yagiela 1999 rates the thyroid-hormone interaction evidence as
   "much less compelling".
8. **Methemoglobinemia** — correct. Add: prilocaine is label-contraindicated in congenital or idiopathic
   methemoglobinemia; other high-risk groups are G6PD deficiency, infants < 6 months, cardiac / pulmonary compromise
   and concurrent oxidizing drugs. Since 2018 all prescription local anesthetic labels carry a methemoglobinemia
   warning, so "avoid prilocaine and benzocaine" names the highest-risk agents, not the only ones. Prilocaine also
   hides in topical lidocaine/prilocaine products.
9. **"Atypical plasma cholinesterase — avoid esters (note articaine)"** — no injectable ester is sold in US dental
   cartridges, so this is mainly about ester topicals (benzocaine, tetracaine) and is low-yield for the screen.
   Articaine's ester side chain is hydrolyzed in plasma (label: "plasma carboxyesterase"; Becker & Reed: "plasma
   cholinesterases"), but no source read shows a clinical problem in cholinesterase deficiency and the label has no
   such precaution. Recommend caption only.
10. **Severe liver disease** — the direction ("less amide, longer intervals") is supported by labels and
    pharmacokinetics, but there is no dental-specific numeric adjustment. Articaine is 90-95% plasma-hydrolyzed,
    yet its label still says caution in severe hepatic disease (not studied).
11. **Sulfite** — "bisulfite" is loosely right: cartridges contain sodium or potassium *metabisulfite*, 0.5-1.2 mg/mL
    depending on brand. Sulfite sensitivity is not sulfa (sulfonamide) allergy. An inhaler pictogram must not imply
    that asthma itself forbids epinephrine: under 3.9% of asthmatics reacted to ingested sulfite (Bush 1986).
12. **Pregnancy "verify current labelling language"** — FDA letter categories were removed by the PLLR (effective
    June 30, 2015). Guidance to cite instead: ACOG CO 569 (2013, reaffirmed 2017) "local anesthesia (lidocaine with or
    without epinephrine) ... safe during pregnancy"; ADA (page updated July 14, 2025). Some dental labels still print
    legacy letters (prilocaine B; articaine and mepivacaine C). Do not put a letter category on screen.
13. **Malignant hyperthermia "amides considered safe"** — MHAUS is broader: "All Local Anesthetics are Safe".
14. **MAOIs** (not in the brief but a classic board trap): "no credible evidence of a significant interaction with
    epinephrine or levonordefrin" (Yagiela 1999; Boakes 1973), although FDA labels still list MAOIs.
15. Citations confirmed as given: Becker & Reed, Anesth Prog 2012;59:90-101; Yagiela, JADA 1999;130:701-9 (Part V of
    a series); Hersh & Giannakopoulos, Dent Clin North Am 2010;54:687-96; Guay, Anesth Analg 2009;108:837-45
    (242 episodes).

## (d) Controversies and caveats (what must not be overstated)

- **The epinephrine cap is a convention.** No trial defines 0.04 mg. Available RCTs are small and at high risk of
  bias, excluded unstable patients, and none reported death, MI or stroke. Unstable cardiac patients (unstable
  angina, recent MI, uncontrolled arrhythmia / hypertension / heart failure) should not be having elective care at
  all — that is a different message from "2 cartridges".
- **Withholding the vasoconstrictor has a cost.** Poorer, shorter anesthesia means pain and endogenous catecholamine
  release; Guimaraes 2021 found more pain and bleeding without vasoconstrictor. For endodontics, profound pulpal
  anesthesia matters. Frame as "limit and monitor", not "avoid".
- **Levonordefrin is not the gentle alternative** (norepinephrine-like; raises blood pressure).
- **Labels vs evidence.** Labels warn about MAOIs and still say MH risk of amides is "not known"; reviews and MHAUS
  say otherwise. Labels allow prilocaine 8 mg/kg; Guay recommends 2.5 mg/kg.
- **Cocaine window** ranges from 6 to 48 h across sources; none is evidence-based.
- **Thyroid**: classic contraindication (Perusse 1992; Little 2006) versus weak interaction evidence (Yagiela 1999).
- **Articaine half-life** is reported as about 20 min (Oertel 1997), 20-40 min (Becker & Reed 2012) and 43.8-44.4 min
  (label): do not put a half-life on screen.
- **Pregnancy**: ACOG says treatment may be given "at any time during pregnancy"; the second-trimester preference
  for elective care is tradition. Cohort data are reassuring but underpowered for rare anomalies.
- **Sulfite**: true reactions to cartridge sulfite are rare case-report-level events; most "epinephrine allergy"
  histories are palpitations.
- **Pictogram risk**: a heart with a red prohibition sign over a cartridge would teach the wrong thing. Keep the
  amber / red / green distinction strict.

## (e) Do not claim

- "Maximum 2 cartridges in cardiac patients" as a rule, "per appointment" as settled, or that exceeding 0.04 mg is
  proven harmful.
- "Epinephrine is contraindicated" with beta-blockers, tricyclics, controlled hypertension, controlled thyroid
  disease, asthma, or in pregnancy.
- Any numeric epinephrine cap for tricyclic patients (0.05 / 0.054 mg) or a 1/2-cartridge test dose as verified.
- "Wait 24 hours after cocaine" attributed to a specific paper.
- "MAOIs interact with epinephrine."
- "Articaine is contraindicated / unsafe in atypical cholinesterase", or "articaine is safe in liver failure".
- A numeric dose reduction for liver disease (the "2-3 fold" figure is for antiarrhythmic lidocaine).
- An FDA pregnancy letter ("Category B") as current labeling, or that any local anesthetic is "proven safe" in
  pregnancy (wording: "considered safe" / ACOG).
- "Sulfa allergy" in place of sulfite; a single mg figure for sulfite content.
- "Only prilocaine and benzocaine cause methemoglobinemia", or any half-life number for articaine.
- "Amides trigger malignant hyperthermia."
- Cartridge band colors as ADA color codes — the ADA color table could not be read here (image only). Use neutral
  cartridges labelled with text ("EPI 1:100,000", "LEVO", "PRILOCAINE", "PLAIN").
