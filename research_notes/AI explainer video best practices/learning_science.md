# Learning-Science Evidence for Short Educational Explainer Videos (Medical/Dental Emphasis)

Scope note: These notes cover research through late 2026. They are meant for a 90-second, vertical (9:16), narrated, animated explainer on placebo and nocebo in endodontics, for endodontists and residents. Effect sizes are Cohen's d or Hedges' g unless stated. Read "Mayer median d" values as medians from Mayer's own lab experiments. They run larger than independent meta-analyses (see Q1).

---

## Q1. What do Mayer's multimedia principles say, and how strong is the evidence for each? (Includes Multimedia Learning 3rd ed., 2020/2021, and the Cambridge Handbook 3rd ed., 2022)

### Takeaway
Mayer's 2021 synthesis of his own experiments gives large median effects for most principles: temporal contiguity 1.31, modality 1.00, personalization 1.00, coherence 0.86, spatial contiguity 0.82, pre-training 0.78, voice 0.74, generative activity 0.71, signaling 0.70, segmenting 0.67, embodiment 0.58. Redundancy is near zero (0.10) and the image principle is weak (0.19). Independent meta-analyses give much smaller averages: g ≈ 0.38 across principles (Noetel et al. 2022) and g = 0.37 across Mayer's corpus (Cromley & Chen 2025). Design matters most for complex material and for system-paced media, which describes an autoplay social video.

### Cited Findings
**Mayer (2021) meta-analysis of his own lab's experiments.** Reproduced in Mayer's 2023 APA Division 2 chapter, "Note. Based on Mayer (2021)". Format: median d (positive tests / total tests).
- *Reducing extraneous processing*
  - Coherence: 0.86 (18/19).
  - Signaling: 0.70 (26/28). Verbal signaling 0.69 (15/16); visual signaling 0.71 (11/12).
  - Redundancy: 0.10 (8/12), "Don't add onscreen captions to narrated graphics".
  - Spatial contiguity: 0.82 (9/9).
  - Temporal contiguity: 1.31 (8/8).
  - Source: [Mayer 2023, *Research-Based Principles for Designing Multimedia Instruction*, APA Div. 2 e-book](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- *Managing essential processing*
  - Segmenting: 0.67 (7/7).
  - Pre-training: 0.78 (10/10).
  - Modality: 1.00 (18/19).
  - Source: [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- *Fostering generative processing*
  - Personalization: 1.00 (13/15).
  - Voice (human rather than machine voice): 0.74 (6/7).
  - Embodiment (human-like gesture, movement, eye contact): 0.58 (16/17).
  - Image (static image of speaker on screen): 0.19 (4/7, with 3 negative).
  - Generative activity: 0.71 (37/44).
  - Source: [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- *Multimedia principle* (words + graphics vs. words alone): d = 1.35 across 13 comparisons — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- *Generative activity subtypes* (Fiorella & Mayer 2015):
  - Summarizing d = 0.50 (30 tests); mapping 0.62 (39); drawing 0.40 (28); imagining 0.65 (22).
  - Self-testing 0.57 (76); self-explaining 0.61 (54); teaching 0.77 (19); enacting 0.51 (49).
  - Source: [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Mayer treats d > 0.40 as educationally important (following Hattie). He notes that "most of the supporting research involves short-term laboratory studies." — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)

**Boundary conditions Mayer lists (2021/2023)**, all from [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf):
- Coherence is strongest for learners with low working memory, for system-paced lessons, and when the extraneous material is highly interesting.
- Signaling is strongest for low-knowledge learners, for complex material, and when cues are used sparingly.
- The redundancy effect is "diminished or even reversed" when:
  - the lesson is slow-paced or learner-controlled;
  - the on-screen text is short or reworded;
  - the words are in the learner's second language;
  - the material lacks graphics.
- Spatial contiguity is strongest for low prior knowledge, non-redundant text, complex lessons, and interactive formats.
- Temporal contiguity is strongest for long lessons and system-paced lessons.
- Pre-training "may not apply to high prior knowledge learners", nor when the material is simple or the lesson is slow or learner-paced.
- Printed words can beat spoken words (a modality boundary) when the verbal material contains technical terms, is in the learner's second language, or the lesson is learner-paced.
- Personalization "may not apply for experienced learners or long lessons."
- The voice and embodiment principles can fail when paired with negative social cues: a machine voice, or an agent with low embodiment.
- Generative activities work best when scaffolded so they are not too demanding.

**Independent meta-meta-analysis: Noetel et al. 2022**, *Review of Educational Research* 92(3):413–454.
- Scope: 29 reviews, 1,189 studies, 78,177 participants. Eleven principles had significant positive effects on learning. The largest were:
  - captioning second-language videos;
  - temporal/spatial contiguity;
  - signaling.
- Robust but smaller effects: modality, animation, coherence (removing seductive details), anthropomorphics, segmentation, personalization, pedagogical agents, and verbal redundancy.
- Source: [Noetel et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329)
- Average effect on learning g = 0.38 (95% CI 0.27–0.49; k = 808). Retention g = 0.42 and transfer g = 0.43 were nearly identical. Effect on cognitive-load management g = 0.22 (0.04–0.40) — [Noetel et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329)
- Principle-level estimates from the largest review per principle — [Noetel et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329):
  - Contiguity overall g = 0.74 [0.67–0.82] (Ginns 2006). Spatial d = 0.72 and temporal d = 0.78 in Ginns. Schroeder & Cenkci (2018) spatial contiguity g = 0.63.
  - Signaling g = 0.43 [0.35–0.50], k = 209 (S. Schneider et al. 2018). Transfer g = 0.53, retention g = 0.33, cognitive load reduced g = 0.25.
  - Signaling in a second review (row attributed by position to Alpizar et al. 2020): d = 0.38. Larger for multimedia (d = 0.63), for low prior knowledge (d = 0.47), and with dynamic/animated signals (d = 0.43).
  - Modality d = 0.72 (Ginns 2005) and g = 0.38 [0.33–0.43], k = 86 (Reinwein 2012). Reinwein's estimate drops to g = 0.20 after correcting for publication bias. The effect is "eliminated when students can self-pace"; in system-paced presentations d = 0.93.
  - Removing seductive details g = 0.33, and g = 0.52 for learners without prior experience (Sundararajan & Adesope 2020).
  - Personalization: retention g = 0.30, transfer g = 0.54 (Ginns et al. 2013).
  - Segmenting (Rey et al. 2019): retention g = 0.32, transfer g = 0.36, cognitive load g = 0.23.
  - Verbal redundancy g = 0.15 [0.08–0.22] (Adesope & Nesbit 2012).
  - Pedagogical agents g ≈ 0.19–0.20.
  - Emotional design (anthropomorphism/pleasant colors): retention d = 0.39, transfer d = 0.33.
- Moderators — [Noetel et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329):
  - Design principles mattered more for complex material (high element interactivity) g = 0.70 [0.59–0.81] than for simple material g = 0.20 [0.02–0.39].
  - They mattered more for system-paced media g = 0.41 [0.33–0.49] than learner-paced media g = 0.27 [0.19–0.35].
  - Prior knowledge did not significantly moderate the pooled effects (p = 0.14).
  - Education level and subject domain also did not moderate.
- Quality caveat: only 1 of 29 reviews did a formal quality assessment, and only 2 reported duplicate screening — [Noetel et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329)

**Independent meta-analysis of Mayer's own corpus: Cromley & Chen 2025**, *Educational Research Review* 49.
- Overall g = 0.37 across 591 effects from 92 articles.
- Largest effects: removing seductive details, modality, personalization, multimedia, sentence-level coherence, and self-explanation.
- Medium effects: testing, scaffolding, cueing, and embodiment.
- Animation, games and simulations showed smaller effects on factual learning and larger effects on inference and transfer. VR effects were non-significant.
- Source: [Cromley & Chen 2025 (NSF PAR record)](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10637927)
- Secondary summary of the same paper: effects declined slightly by publication year, and university and medical trainees showed the largest benefits — [Hendrick summary (secondary)](https://carlhendrick.substack.com/p/which-multimedia-strategies-actually)

**Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning, 3rd ed.** (Mayer & Fiorella, eds., Cambridge University Press, 2022; 46 chapters). Principles and chapters that are new or more prominent than in 2014:
- Expertise Reversal (ch. 13, Kalyuga)
- Transient Information (ch. 21, Jiang & Sweller)
- Immersion (ch. 24, Makransky)
- Animation Composition (ch. 26)
- Emotional Design (ch. 27)
- Generative Activity (ch. 28, Fiorella & Mayer), plus Drawing, Imagination, Self-Explanation and Learner Control chapters
- Multimedia Learning with Instructional Video (ch. 41, Fiorella), which includes a "dynamic drawings" lesson figure
- Source: [Cambridge Handbook 3rd ed. front matter](https://assets.cambridge.org/97811088/41580/frontmatter/9781108841580_frontmatter.pdf)

**Mayer 2024 *Educational Psychology Review*** ("Past, present, and future of CTML") summarizes 15 evidence-based principles — [Springer record](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-023-09842-1). I could not open its table (see Gaps).

**Video-specific principles: Mayer, Fiorella & Stull 2020**, *ETR&D* 68:837–852, "Five ways to increase the effectiveness of instructional video".
- Dynamic drawing: the instructor draws while explaining, rather than pointing at pre-drawn graphics.
- Gaze/attention guidance.
- Generative activity: prompted summaries or explanations, especially for lower prior knowledge.
- Perspective: first-person filming of procedures.
- Subtitles for second-language learners.
- "One way not to use video": seductive details, including a small talking head beside slides.
- Sources: [DOI](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S11423-020-09749-6); [Univ. of Sydney summary](https://educational-innovation.sydney.edu.au/teaching@sydney/five-ways-to-make-online-videos-better-for-student-learning/)

**Dynamic drawing evidence**
- Fiorella & Mayer (2016): students did better on transfer after watching an instructor draw on a board while lecturing than after watching her gesture at already-drawn graphics. Mayer classifies this under embodiment — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Fiorella, Stull, Kuhlmann & Mayer 2019 (*J Educ Psychol* 111:1162–1171): learning improved from instructor drawing and eye contact. Instructor visibility had no added effect when drawing was present — [ECR Hub record](https://ecrhub.org/publications/instructor-presence-video-lectures-role-dynamic-drawings-eye-contact-and-instructor)

### Inferences
- **Treat Mayer's medians as upper bounds.** They come from short lab studies with novice undergraduates learning causal systems (lightning, brakes, pumps). Independent pooled estimates are about half as large (g ≈ 0.3–0.4). For an expert audience, expect smaller gains still.
- **The most robust levers for a 90-second narrated animation:**
  - Temporal and spatial contiguity (strong in both Mayer and Noetel).
  - Signaling.
  - Coherence (cut seductive details).
  - Modality: narration plus graphics, which matters most because autoplay video is system-paced.
  - Conversational wording, with the caution that it may matter less for experts.
- **Redundancy is the weakest and most conditional principle** (0.10 in Mayer 2021; g = 0.15 *favoring* some redundancy in Adesope & Nesbit). Do not treat "no on-screen text" as a hard rule (see Q3).
- **The image principle argues against a static talking-head avatar** (d = 0.19). If a presenter appears, it should *do* something — draw, gesture or point (embodiment / dynamic drawing).

### Gaps
- I could not open the full Mayer (2024, *EPR*) table of 15 principles or the 3rd-edition book table. Numbers here come from Mayer's 2023 chapter, which states they are "Based on Mayer (2021)".
- I did not find a published median effect size for a stand-alone "dynamic drawing principle". Mayer's 2021 JARMAC article ([DOI 10.1016/j.jarmac.2021.03.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jarmac.2021.03.007)) reportedly lists 14 video principles, but I could not open the text.
- Pre-training, voice and image have few or no independent meta-analyses; their evidence rests mainly on Mayer's lab (7–10 experiments each).

## Q2. How do expertise-reversal effects change these principles for expert learners (specialists and residents)?

### Takeaway
Classic cognitive-load studies show that supports which help novices can become redundant and harmful for more knowledgeable learners:
- integrated text in diagrams;
- narration added to diagrams;
- worked examples;
- static step-by-step depictions.

Mayer explicitly says pre-training and personalization may not apply to experienced learners. But the meta-meta-analysis found that prior knowledge did *not* significantly moderate pooled effects. So expertise reversal is real but principle-specific and task-specific, not a blanket license to drop good design. Endodontists are experts in endodontics, not necessarily in placebo/nocebo neurobiology or statistics.

### Cited Findings
- The expertise reversal effect is a form of redundancy. Information that helps novices becomes redundant for more knowledgeable learners, and processing it "may overload working memory." It is tied to "task-specific expertise" — [Kalyuga 2007, *Educ Psychol Rev* 19:509–539](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/EDC608/Kalyuga2007_Article_ExpertiseReversalEffectAndItsI.pdf)
- Kalyuga et al. (1998), electrical wiring: novices learned best from explanations embedded in diagrams. After extensive training, diagram-alone materials were easier and gave higher test performance — [Kalyuga 2007](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/EDC608/Kalyuga2007_Article_ExpertiseReversalEffectAndItsI.pdf)
- Kalyuga et al. (2000), mechanical engineering: detailed *narrated* explanations with animated diagrams helped novices (a modality effect). As learners gained knowledge, the narration became redundant. After intensive training, the diagram-only group outperformed the diagram-plus-narration group — [Kalyuga 2007](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/EDC608/Kalyuga2007_Article_ExpertiseReversalEffectAndItsI.pdf)
- Novice-friendly load reducers "became ineffective and often resulted in negative rather than positive or neutral effects for more knowledgeable learners." Examples: integration, dual-modality, and worked examples — [Kalyuga 2007](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/EDC608/Kalyuga2007_Article_ExpertiseReversalEffectAndItsI.pdf)
- Animation reverses in the *opposite* direction:
  - In Kalyuga (2007/2008), low-knowledge learners did better with static diagrams and high-knowledge learners did better with animated instructions.
  - In Schnotz & Rasch (2005), high-prerequisite learners did equally well with animations or statics, while low-prerequisite learners did better with statics.
  - Source: [Kalyuga 2007](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/EDC608/Kalyuga2007_Article_ExpertiseReversalEffectAndItsI.pdf)
  - Noetel's summary of an animation review reports the same pattern: low-prior-knowledge learners did not benefit from animation (d = −0.16) and high-prior-knowledge learners did (d = 0.49) — [Noetel et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329)
- Mayer's principle-level boundary conditions, all from [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf):
  - Pre-training may not apply to high prior knowledge learners.
  - Personalization may not apply for experienced learners.
  - Signaling and spatial contiguity are strongest for low-knowledge learners.
  - Coherence is strongest for low working memory.
- The meta-meta-analysis found that "the pooled effects of multimedia design principles were, on average, equivalent for high and low prior-knowledge learners." The expertise-reversal hypothesis "was not supported by our analyses." Some reviews found larger benefits for low prior knowledge and others the reverse — [Noetel et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329)
  - Internal inconsistency in that paper: its discussion cites Richter et al. (2016) as finding a reverse pattern. Its own summary table reports Richter's signaling effect as larger for low prior knowledge (r = 0.19) than high (r = −0.08).
- Noetel et al. give a clinical example: "junior doctors may find anatomy clearer with labels directly on the image… but senior doctors may be so familiar with anatomy that the labels become distracting" — [Noetel et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329)
- The signaling meta-analysis by Schneider et al. (2018) found effects "relatively consistent… in both high- and low-prior knowledge of learners," and within both system- and learner-paced presentations — [Noetel et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329)
- Learner control improves performance for high-prior-knowledge learners but has no or negative effects for low-prior-knowledge learners (Carolan et al. 2014) — [Cavanagh & Kiersch 2022, *ETR&D*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9762622)
- Video-based coaching for surgical residents: the benefit "may be most substantial for trainees with lower baseline levels of objective skill" — [Surg Endosc 2023 meta-analysis, PMID 35739431](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35739431/)
- The 3rd-edition Cambridge Handbook gives expertise reversal its own principle chapter (ch. 13, Kalyuga) — [front matter](https://assets.cambridge.org/97811088/41580/frontmatter/9781108841580_frontmatter.pdf)
- Brame recommends tailoring "weeding" to expertise, since information extraneous for novices may help experts — [Brame 2016, CBE-LSE 15:es6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5132380/)

### Inferences
- **Expertise is task-specific.** Endodontists know pulp biology, pain diagnosis and RCT outcomes. Many will be relative novices on placebo/nocebo mechanisms (expectancy, conditioning, open-label placebo) and on interpreting pooled placebo-arm data.
  - Strip explanations of endodontic basics: what irreversible pulpitis is, what a placebo arm is.
  - Keep brief scaffolds for the less familiar psychology and statistics: a one-line operational definition of nocebo, and natural-frequency framing.
- **Pre-training:** skip a separate glossary segment. At most, flash a 2–3 second "key term" card for one unfamiliar construct (e.g., "nocebo = harm from negative expectation"). Experts lose more from a pre-training detour than they gain.
- **Narration plus labels:** experts may find exhaustive verbal walk-throughs of a diagram redundant (Kalyuga 2000). Narrate the *claim and its implication*, not a description of every visual element. Let the visual carry detail an expert can parse unaided.
- **Animation may suit experts *better* than novices**, because experts can track transient change. Animated build-ups of mechanisms or data are reasonable for this audience, provided they are representational (see Q5).
- **Keep signaling and contiguity.** They held up across prior-knowledge levels in meta-analyses and cost little.
- **Personalization:** conversational wording is fine, but avoid a patronizing, consumer tone. The evidence for personalization benefits in experts is weak.

### Gaps
- I found no experimental expertise-reversal study using practicing dental or medical specialists watching narrated explainer videos. The evidence comes from engineering, mathematics and undergraduates, plus surgical-coaching data on baseline skill.
- No study quantifies how much "domain-adjacent" expertise (dentistry → placebo science) reduces the need for scaffolds.

## Q3. Do on-screen captions or subtitles conflict with the redundancy principle? (Non-native speakers, silent autoplay, keyword vs. verbatim captions)

### Takeaway
The redundancy penalty for verbatim on-screen text is small and conditional (Mayer 2021: d = 0.10). The broader meta-analysis finds that adding written key words to spoken presentations *helps* slightly (g ≈ 0.29). Captions strongly help second-language viewers (g ≈ 0.99 comprehension). Short keyword captions placed next to the relevant graphic are the best-supported compromise. Full verbatim captions are justified for accessibility, non-native viewers, and the large share of mobile viewing done with sound off; that last point rests on industry survey data, not peer-reviewed research.

### Cited Findings
- **Redundancy principle (Mayer 2021):** supported in only 8 of 12 tests, median d = 0.10 favoring no on-screen text. The effect is "diminished or even reversed" when the lesson is slow or learner-paced, the text is short or reworded, the words are in the learner's second language, or graphics are absent — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- **Adesope & Nesbit 2012 meta-analysis (k = 57):** verbal redundancy modestly *improved* learning (g = 0.15, 95% CI 0.08–0.22).
  - It helped "only when adding visual presentation of key words to audio presentations (g = 0.29), not when adding audio to text presentations (g = −0.04)."
  - Effects were consistent across learner- and system-paced formats, settings, and "participants of various levels of education and language fluency."
  - Source: [Noetel et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329)
- **Mayer & Johnson 2008** (*J Educ Psychol* 100:380), "Revising the redundancy principle":
  - Short redundant on-screen phrases placed *within* the diagram improved retention, not transfer.
  - Revised rule: redundancy is avoided "except when the onscreen text is short, highlights the key action described in the narration, and is placed next to the portion of the graphic that it describes."
  - Source: [DOI 10.1037/0022-0663.100.2.380](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.100.2.380)
  - Brame (2016) operationalizes this as "two or three on-screen key words" — [Brame 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5132380/)
- **Moreno & Mayer (2002):** the classic redundancy result used verbatim on-screen text "inserted at the bottom of the screen" — that is, a caption strip far from the graphic — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
  - Moreno & Mayer (1999): words placed next to the relevant part of the animation beat captions at the bottom of the screen (spatial contiguity) — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- **Second-language learners**
  - Captioning L2 videos is the single largest effect in the meta-meta-analysis: comprehension g = 0.99 [0.60–1.38], k = 15; vocabulary g = 0.87 (Montero Perez et al. 2013). Kang (2019) found multimedia captioned for L2 listening d = 1.69 — [Noetel et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329)
  - Lee & Mayer 2018 (*Appl Cogn Psychol* 32:648–654), with college students learning in a second language: adding on-screen text to narrated video *improved* comprehension (video + narration + text > video + narration). This is framed as a boundary condition on both the redundancy and modality principles — [Lee & Mayer 2018 record](https://pure.uos.ac.kr/en/publications/fostering-learning-from-instructional-video-in-a-second-language/)
  - Mayer, Fiorella & Stull (2020) list a "subtitle principle": subtitles in the instruction language are especially important for second-language learners — [Univ. of Sydney summary](https://educational-innovation.sydney.edu.au/teaching@sydney/five-ways-to-make-online-videos-better-for-student-learning/)
- **Technical terms and pacing.** Printed words may be preferable to spoken words when the content has technical terms or is in the learner's L2. Spoken words are preferable when the lesson is fast-paced — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- **Practical caption guidance** (review, no effect sizes): captions help hearing-impaired learners, non-native speakers, and learners unfamiliar with jargon. Recommendations are to give learners control over captions and make sure captions do not cover key visuals — [Cavanagh & Kiersch 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9762622)
- **Silent/sound-off viewing (industry survey, not peer-reviewed).** Verizon Media/Publicis Media, April 2019, online survey of 5,616 US adults aged 18–54:
  - 69% watch video with sound off in public places; 25% even in private.
  - Respondents reported being more likely to watch to the end with captions.
  - 37% said captions encourage them to turn sound on.
  - Sources: [NextTV coverage](https://nexttv.com/news/mobile-videos-often-watched-without-audio-study-finds); [3Play Media summary](https://www.3playmedia.com/blog/verizon-media-and-publicis-media-find-viewers-want-captions/)
  - Secondary summaries also cite 92% sound-off on mobile and an "80% more likely to finish" figure. These are loosely sourced and inconsistent across summaries — [3Play Media](https://www.3playmedia.com/blog/verizon-media-and-publicis-media-find-viewers-want-captions/)

### Inferences
- **For a vertical social/autoplay explainer, captions are not optional.**
  - Many viewers start muted, so captions are their only verbal channel. The modality principle cannot operate without sound.
  - Many endodontic residents and specialists worldwide are L2 English speakers, which is the strongest-evidence caption benefit.
  - The redundancy penalty is small (d ≈ 0.10), and it rests mostly on verbatim captions placed far from the graphic.
- **Recommended two-layer approach:**
  1. **Burned-in, keyword-style captions** (2–6 words per beat) placed *adjacent to* the relevant visual. These signal and label (Mayer & Johnson 2008; spatial contiguity; signaling) and keep the video intelligible muted.
  2. **Full verbatim closed captions** as a toggle (platform CC/SRT) for accessibility and L2 viewers. Where the platform cannot do this, use condensed, chunked full captions synced word-for-word to narration (temporal contiguity), in a band that does not occlude key graphics or the platform UI.
- **Do not put full verbatim sentences on screen *and* separate keyword labels at the same time.** That stacks three verbal streams (audio, caption sentence, labels) against the animation. If full captions are burned in, keep in-graphic text to minimal labels and numbers.
- **Technical terms** ("nocebo", "hyperalgesia", "open-label placebo") should appear in print when first spoken. The modality boundary for technical terms supports this.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed experiment directly testing keyword-only vs. full verbatim captions in *muted autoplay* vertical video, or with clinicians. Yue, Bjork & Bjork (2013) on partially redundant text is relevant, but I could not verify its findings here.
- Sound-off viewing prevalence comes from a 2019 advertiser survey. I found no 2023–2026 peer-reviewed estimate for professional or educational short-form viewing.

## Q4. Does the voice principle hold for synthetic/AI voices? (Craig & Schroeder 2017/2019; neural TTS 2022–2026)

### Takeaway
The original voice effect (human > machine voice; Mayer median d = 0.74 over 7 tests) was built on older concatenative/robotic TTS. With modern TTS, Craig & Schroeder found no learning difference vs. human recordings, and later trials agree: the 2025 *Computers & Education* RCT (N = 447) of AI-generated videos found equal exam scores. Human voices are still consistently rated higher on social presence, trust, humanness and "learning experience". Studies that find a human-voice learning advantage mostly used noticeably less natural synthetic voices or small samples. Voice quality, naturalness and enthusiasm matter more than whether the voice is human or synthetic.

### Cited Findings
- **Voice principle:** human voice > machine voice, 6 of 7 tests, median d = 0.74 (e.g., Mayer, Sobko & Mautone 2003). Mayer adds that "Craig and Schroder (2017, 2018) found that advances in modern text-to-speech engines can allow them to be more human-like." Boundary: the voice principle may not apply with negative social cues such as low embodiment — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- **Craig & Schroeder 2019** (*J Educ Comput Res* 57(6):1534–1548; online 2018):
  - Randomized pretest–posttest design with an instructional animation narrated by an older TTS engine, a modern TTS engine, or a recorded human voice.
  - Modern TTS learners did not differ statistically from human-voice learners in perceptions, learning outcomes, or cognitive efficiency.
  - The authors suggest voice may matter less now than it did in the past.
  - Sources: [DOI 10.1177/0735633118802877](https://doi.org/10.1177/0735633118802877); [secondary description](https://journals.phil.muni.cz/bse/article/view/41144)
- **Craig & Schroeder 2017** (*Computers & Education*): tested the same voices with a virtual human. A 2026 review describes it as finding that modern synthesized voices outperformed both classic synthetic and human voices — [Ma et al. 2026, Frontiers in Psychology (literature review section)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1863163/full)
  - A follow-up reports that the human voice produced higher agent-persona ratings, especially humanness and engagement, with no differences in learning or cognitive efficiency — [summary via search of Craig & Schroeder follow-up](https://journals.phil.muni.cz/bse/article/view/41144)
- **Chiou, Schroeder & Craig 2020** (*Computers & Education* 146): learners trusted human-voiced agents more and perceived them more favorably. Voice quality (low- vs. high-quality synthetic vs. human) had no significant effect on learning — [DOI 10.1016/j.compedu.2019.103756](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2019.103756); summarized in [Ma et al. 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1863163/full)
- **Netland et al. 2025** (*Computers & Education* 224:105164), RCT:
  - 447 participants; four AI-generated vs. human-made teaching videos in a management course.
  - No statistically significant difference in exam results.
  - Human-made videos had a small but significant edge in learning experience; learners still preferred human teachers.
  - AI videos took hours to produce vs. days.
  - Sources: [author write-up](https://media-and-learning.eu/subject/artificial-intelligence/when-ai-took-over-my-teaching-videos-students-enjoyed-them-less-but-learned-the-same/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105164)
- **2025 study of AI-generated short videos for L2 learning** (*Educ Inf Technol*): AI voice and AI avatar can each reduce extraneous load, but the reduction was significant only when both were AI-generated. Traditional videos gave stronger social presence; AI videos were rated moderately to highly engaging — [Springer s10639-025-13654-x](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-025-13654-x)
- **2026 eye-tracking study of ~60-second micro-learning videos** (*Int J Hum–Comput Interact*): human-avatar conditions produced higher objective learning than AI-avatar conditions. Human voices improved *perceived* comprehension without significantly improving objective scores ("visual authenticity outweighs vocal naturalness") — [T&F abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/10447318.2026.2688925)
  - Abstract-level only; full text was paywalled (403).
- **Ma et al. 2026** (*Frontiers in Psychology*, fNIRS):
  - N = 30, within-subjects 2×2 (human vs. animated agent × human vs. machine voice), ~2-minute videos.
  - Human voice improved transfer: F(1,29) = 14.5, ηp² = 0.33. But transfer was a single item per condition, which the authors call exploratory, and the paper has table/text inconsistencies.
  - The machine voice was rated far less natural (2.13 vs. 4.73 on 5 points), so it was not a state-of-the-art neural voice.
  - Source: [Ma et al. 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1863163/full)
- **Other studies, via the Ma et al. 2026 literature review:**
  - Liew et al. (2023): human voices promoted learning more than machine voices in a programming course.
  - Jing et al. (2024): human voices improved attention and performance vs. "cute" or formal synthesized voices.
  - Source: [Ma et al. 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1863163/full)
- **Enthusiasm matters for TTS.** A 2022 study (n = 244) found enthusiastic Alexa voices produced stronger effects than a neutral voice and lowered intrinsic/extraneous load ratings. The authors argue a mechanical-sounding TTS may not prime social presence — [Frontiers in Psychology 2021/2022, 10.3389/fpsyg.2021.746857](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.746857/pdf)
- **Dinçer 2022** (*Educ Inf Technol*) re-tested whether the voice effect still holds; cognitive-load results were consistent with learning outcomes — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10639-022-11255-6)
- **Engagement with human speakers:** fast, enthusiastic speakers were most engaging in MOOC data. Speaking rate was a surface proxy for enthusiasm — [Guo, Kim & Rubin 2014](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf)

### Inferences
- **A high-quality modern neural TTS voice is defensible for knowledge outcomes.** The best-powered study (Netland 2025, N = 447) and Craig & Schroeder found parity on learning. The main cost is social-affective: slightly lower perceived presence, trust and "experience". That cost may matter more for expert clinicians judging credibility.
- **Mitigations:**
  - Choose a natural, warm, moderately enthusiastic neural voice. Avoid flat or "announcer" prosody.
  - Use a pronunciation dictionary for technical terms (nocebo, periapical, hyperalgesia); mispronunciation is a negative social cue.
  - Avoid pairing the TTS voice with a low-embodiment static avatar. Mayer's boundary conditions say voice and embodiment effects fail together with negative social cues, and the 2026 IJHCI data suggest visual authenticity matters more than vocal naturalness.
  - Consider disclosing AI narration and/or crediting the clinician-author on screen to protect credibility. This is an inference; I found no study testing it.
- Studies showing a human-voice advantage used weaker synthetic voices (low rated naturalness) and small samples. They should not be generalized to current neural TTS without caution.

### Gaps
- I found no study testing neural TTS vs. human narration with *clinicians or health-professions learners*.
- I found no large preregistered 2023–2026 RCT that isolates voice alone (not voice plus avatar) with current neural TTS (e.g., ElevenLabs-class) and measures learning, cognitive load and social presence together.
- Whether credibility judgments by *experts* (vs. students) are more sensitive to synthetic voices is untested.

## Q5. Video engagement and design research (Guo et al. 2014; Brame 2016; Fiorella & Mayer on drawing and instructor visibility; animation vs. static)

### Takeaway
Shorter is more engaging. In 6.9M MOOC sessions, median engagement capped at ~6 minutes and the 0–3 minute videos held attention best. Drawing and motion beat static slides. Fast, enthusiastic delivery is more engaging. Engagement is not learning, though. For learning:
- Instructor *visibility* adds little (small retention benefit, no transfer benefit).
- Instructor *drawing* and eye contact help.
- Animation beats static graphics modestly overall (g ≈ 0.23–0.37), and only when representational — decorative animation ≈ 0.

### Cited Findings
**Guo, Kim & Rubin 2014** (ACM Learning@Scale; 6.9 million sessions, 4 edX courses). Engagement was measured as watch time and post-video problem attempts, which the authors call "a necessary (but not sufficient) prerequisite for learning." All findings below are from [Guo et al. 2014](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf).
- Median engagement time was at most 6 minutes regardless of video length. Students often made it less than halfway through videos longer than 9 minutes.
- In 0–3 minute videos, 75% of sessions lasted over three-quarters of the video.
- Problem attempts after videos fell with length across five length buckets: 56%, 48%, 43%, 41%, 31%.
- Videos that interspersed the instructor's talking head with slides were more engaging than slides alone. A personal, informal feel was more engaging than high-fidelity studio recordings.
- Khan-style tablet-drawing tutorials engaged students 1.5–2× longer than PowerPoint slides or code screencasts. Recommendation: "Introduce motion and continuous visual flow."
- Speaking rates ranged from 48 to 254 wpm (mean 156, SD 31). Within a length bucket, engagement rose up to 2× with speaking rate.
  - The 145–165 wpm instructors were "the least energetic."
  - Even 254 wpm was understandable because the information was also shown visually.
  - Speaking rate "is merely a surface feature that correlates with enthusiasm."

**Brame 2016** (*CBE—Life Sciences Education* 15(4):es6), all from [Brame 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5132380/):
- Signaling: 2–3 on-screen key words, color/contrast changes, arrows.
- Segmenting: ≤6 minutes or chapters.
- Weeding: remove music, complex backgrounds and extra animation, tailored to expertise.
- Matching modality: narrate the animation; Khan-style sketches over a talking head.
- Conversational "you/I" language.
- Speak relatively quickly with enthusiasm. Her 185–254 wpm range is uncited.
- Interpolated questions (Szpunar et al. 2013) and guiding questions.
- Her inference: making videos longer than 6–9 minutes is "likely to be wasted effort."

**Instructor visibility and presence**
- Henderson & Schroeder 2021 systematic review (12 studies): "no consistent, compelling evidence that an instructor should be included on-screen" — [Computers & Education Open (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/8153054b2828456987b7c3be15d2895d)
- Alemdag 2022 meta-analysis (20 experiments): no significant effect of instructor presence on learning or social presence, but increased cognitive load and motivation — [METU record](https://avesis.metu.edu.tr/yayin/8df0f0a3-6154-4f4b-9333-5108f1e4b1f1/effects-of-instructor-present-videos-on-learning-cognitive-load-motivation-and-social-presence-a-meta-analysis)
- Beege, Schroeder et al. 2024 series of meta-analyses (*ETR&D*; 35 studies, 46 comparisons, 6,339 participants): small significant positive effect on retention, no significant effect on transfer. Higher social presence and affective/motivational ratings. Moderators include prior knowledge, domain and instructor size — [Springer s11423-024-10391-9](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11423-024-10391-9)
- Image principle: a static image of the speaker gives d = 0.19 (4/7 tests, 3 negative) — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- A small "talking head" beside slides is flagged as a seductive detail ("one way not to use video") — [Mayer, Fiorella & Stull 2020 summary](https://educational-innovation.sydney.edu.au/teaching@sydney/five-ways-to-make-online-videos-better-for-student-learning/)
- Drawing: Fiorella & Mayer 2016 (instructor drawing > pointing at pre-drawn graphics) and Fiorella et al. 2019 (drawing and eye contact helped; visibility added nothing when drawing was present) — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf); [ECR Hub](https://ecrhub.org/publications/instructor-presence-video-lectures-role-dynamic-drawings-eye-contact-and-instructor)
- Pointing/gesturing agents: transfer g = 0.39, retention g = 0.28 (Davis 2018) — [Noetel et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329)

**Animation vs. static graphics**
- Höffler & Leutner 2007 (*Learning & Instruction*):
  - Non-interactive animations beat static images, d = 0.37.
  - Representational animations d = 0.40.
  - Decorational animations d = 0.05 (no significant benefit).
  - Larger effects for procedural-motor knowledge.
  - Source: [Noetel et al. 2022 summary table](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329)
- Berney & Bétrancourt 2016 (*Computers & Education* 101:150–167; 140 comparisons):
  - Overall g = 0.226 (95% CI 0.12–0.33).
  - Larger for factual knowledge (g = 0.336) than conceptual knowledge (g = 0.162).
  - Worked better with audio commentary or when learners had little control over pacing (Noetel summary).
  - Search summaries also report subgroup values: system-paced g = 0.309, auditory commentary g = 0.336. I did not verify these against the paper.
  - Sources: [UNIGE archive](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:92234); [Noetel et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329)
- Castro-Alonso et al. 2019: dynamic > static, g+ = 0.23 — [search-reported; Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09469-1)
- Ploetzner et al.'s re-analysis: animation is "considerably more effective" than static pictures *when the specifics of the displayed changes need to be learned* — [Springer s11251-021-09541-w](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-021-09541-w)
- Cromley & Chen 2025: animation, games and simulations had smaller effects on factual learning and larger effects on inferential and transfer outcomes — [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10637927)
- Signaling within animation: dynamic (moving/animated) signals d = 0.43 — [Noetel et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329)

**Short-form video (emerging, weaker evidence)**
- INTED 2026 (N = 68, randomized): four 1-minute videos vs. one 8-minute video. Short-form produced higher engagement and satisfaction; retention differences were reportedly not significant (abstract snippet truncated) — [IATED](https://library.iated.org/view/MCKEOWN2026DES)
- A *Communications Psychology* study, reported secondhand by PsyPost (two experiments, N = 180 and 185): students who *intentionally* studied short clips forgot more overnight than those who watched a continuous video — [PsyPost (secondary)](https://psypost.org/the-illusion-of-learning-from-short-videos)
- Medical students (N = 156; Frontiers in Medicine 2025): the microlearning pre-class video group did better on essay assessments; nanolearning (single concept) improved concept completion — [Frontiers Med 2025 via PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12538294/)

### Inferences
- A 90-second video sits well inside the high-engagement zone (Guo's 0–3 minute bucket). Length is not the risk; density and pacing are (see Q6).
- **Skip a talking-head clinician inset.** A small static face is a seductive detail with d ≈ 0.19 for learning. If a human or avatar appears, use it briefly and *functionally*: a hand drawing, pointing or gazing at the key element, for 3–5 seconds at the hook or close for credibility.
- **Use representational, process-revealing animation.**
  - Good uses: expectation → descending pain modulation → perceived pain; a placebo-arm response building up over time; nocebo wording → increased pain report.
  - Avoid decorative motion (floating molars, particle backgrounds, kinetic typography for its own sake).
  - A "drawn-on" build (lines appearing as narrated) approximates the dynamic-drawing benefit without an on-screen person.
- **Use motion for signaling:** animated highlights and arrows synced to narration (dynamic signals d = 0.43).

### Gaps
- Guo et al. measured engagement in MOOCs (2012–2013 data), not learning. I found no peer-reviewed study comparing 9:16 vertical vs. 16:9 horizontal formats on learning outcomes.
- Short-form learning studies are few, small and mixed. The key negative result (*Communications Psychology*) was verified only through secondary coverage.

## Q6. Cognitive load theory applied to video: element interactivity, transient information, pacing/learner control, and sensible information density for 90 seconds

### Takeaway
Video is transient and, in autoplay feeds, system-paced. These are exactly the conditions where design principles matter most: system-paced g = 0.41 vs. learner-paced g = 0.27, and complex g = 0.70 vs. simple g = 0.20. Long spoken segments can even reverse the modality advantage (the transient information effect). For 90 seconds, aim for:
- roughly 180–230 spoken words (≈ 130–160 wpm effective rate, leaving pauses);
- one core claim plus 3–4 supporting idea units;
- each idea in its own 10–20 second visual "segment" with a clear boundary.

### Cited Findings
- **Pacing and complexity:**
  - Design principles were more important in system-paced media (g = 0.41) than learner-paced (g = 0.27), and in complex, high-element-interactivity materials (g = 0.70) than simple ones (g = 0.20) — [Noetel et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329)
  - The modality effect is "eliminated when students can self-pace." It is strongest in system-paced presentations (d = 0.93), for short passages, and for dynamic visualizations — [Noetel et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329)
- **Transient information effect:**
  - Long and complex transient information disappears before it can be processed, adding working-memory load (Leahy & Sweller 2011, *Appl Cogn Psychol* 25:943–951; Wong, Leahy, Marcus & Sweller 2012, *Learn Instr* 22:449–457). Wong et al. showed transience can weaken the superiority of dynamic over static visualizations — [summary in Lead/UBE paper](https://lead.ube.fr/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/001246-reducing-the-transience-effect-of-animations-does-not-always-lead-to-better-performance-in-children-learning-a-complex-hand-procedure.pdf); [Macquarie record](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/cognitive-load-theory-and-the-effects-of-transient-information-on/)
  - Leahy & Sweller (2016): longer *audio* text was inferior to equivalent visual text, reversing the modality effect because of transience. Segmentation with pauses is the usual remedy — [same sources](https://lead.ube.fr/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/001246-reducing-the-transience-effect-of-animations-does-not-always-lead-to-better-performance-in-children-learning-a-complex-hand-procedure.pdf)
  - The transient information principle has a dedicated chapter in the 2022 Cambridge Handbook (Jiang & Sweller, ch. 21) — [Cambridge](https://resolve-he.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/transient-information-principle-in-multimedia-learning/670D96C3B9520320CE558AA855A49EE9)
  - Mayer's modality boundary: spoken words work when segments are not "too large to be held in the learner's working memory" — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- **Segmenting:**
  - Mayer & Chandler (2001) split a lightning narrated animation into 16 ten-second segments with a "continue" button, which improved transfer. The segmenting principle has median d = 0.67 (7/7) — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
  - Rey et al. 2019 meta-analysis (56 studies, 7,713 participants): retention g = 0.32, transfer 0.36, cognitive load 0.23. Time on task rose with segmenting (d = −0.92 in the review's sign convention). The optimal segment length is unresolved and depends on content, prior knowledge and working memory — [Cavanagh & Kiersch 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9762622); [Noetel et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329)
- **Learner control:** helps high-prior-knowledge learners; no or negative effects for low prior knowledge — [Cavanagh & Kiersch 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9762622). The Cambridge Handbook has a dedicated Learner Control chapter (Scheiter, ch. 35) — [front matter](https://assets.cambridge.org/97811088/41580/frontmatter/9781108841580_frontmatter.pdf)
- **Speech rate:**
  - MOOC speaking rates averaged 156 wpm (range 48–254). Faster, more enthusiastic speakers were more engaging, and 254 wpm was still understandable with visual support — [Guo et al. 2014](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf)
  - Murphy et al. 2022 (*Appl Cogn Psychol* 36:69–82): lecture videos at up to 2× speed did not significantly impair immediate or delayed comprehension; 2.5× did. Speeding up may not suit especially complex material — [Murphy et al. 2022 PDF](https://castel.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/111/2022/03/Applied-Cognitive-Psychology-2021-Murphy-Learning-in-double-time-The-effect-of-lecture-video-speed-on-immediate-and.pdf)
- **Coherence/extraneous load:** seductive details hurt most when "persistently on-screen" (g = 0.43 benefit of removal) — [Noetel et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329). Brame recommends removing background music and complex backgrounds — [Brame 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5132380/)
- **Mechanism:** limited working memory plays a bigger role (a) when materials are complex, (b) for newer learners, and (c) when the format is "less forgiving" — you cannot pause or reread — [Noetel et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329)

### Inferences (information-density rules for 90 s)
- **Word budget.**
  - 90 s × ~150 wpm ≈ 225 words is the ceiling. Neural TTS at 150–165 wpm with deliberate 0.5–1.0 s pauses at segment boundaries and after each number gives ~180–220 usable words.
  - Experts tolerate faster delivery (Guo; Murphy), but statistics and novel constructs (nocebo) are high element interactivity. Slow down *around numbers*, not uniformly.
- **Idea units.**
  - One take-home claim, e.g., "Expectations measurably change endodontic pain: placebo arms improve, and negative framing worsens pain."
  - Plus about 3–4 supporting beats: the mechanism, the size of the placebo response, the nocebo/communication risk, and the clinical action.
  - Treat each beat as a 10–20 second "segment" with a visual reset (new scene or title card) and a micro-pause. This is system-paced segmenting, because an autoplay feed gives no continue button.
- **One number on screen at a time.** Hold each statistic visually for at least ~3–4 s past the end of its narration (counteracts transience). Never narrate one number while showing a different one.
- **No background music under narration** (or extremely low and simple), no decorative B-roll, and no persistent logos or animated borders (coherence; persistent seductive details are the worst kind).
- **Front-load the conclusion.** In a feed, the first 2–3 seconds decide whether anyone watches. Stating the claim first also acts as an advance organizer (verbal signaling: outline and headings, d ≈ 0.69 in Mayer). This is an inference; the hook-timing figure is platform folklore, not a cited finding.
- **Loopability/rewatch.** Since learner control helps high-prior-knowledge learners, design so a second loop is useful: end on the summary card that matches the opening claim.

### Gaps
- I found no empirical study defining an optimal number of idea units or words for a 60–90 second educational video. The density rules above are extrapolations from segmenting, transient-information and speaking-rate evidence.
- I found no study of how autoplay looping (vs. single play) affects learning.
- Working-memory capacity limits (e.g., ~4 chunks) are commonly cited, but I did not retrieve a primary source in this session.

## Q7. Medical and dental education video research (systematic reviews; dental video studies; animated explainers vs. lectures for clinicians)

### Takeaway
Across health professions and higher education, video is rarely worse than conventional teaching and usually helps:
- Replacing teaching with video gives a small gain (g = 0.28); adding video to teaching gives a large one (g = 0.80).
- Video does best for skills.
- Medical-student knowledge effects of offline digital education are near zero (SMD 0.11).
- Dental "video vs. live demonstration" meta-analyses show point estimates favoring video, with CIs crossing zero and high heterogeneity.

Direct evidence on animated explainers for *practicing specialists* is essentially absent. The best transferable evidence is a medical-school lecture redesigned with Mayer's principles (transfer d = 0.76 immediate, 1.17 delayed).

### Cited Findings
- **Higher education overall.** Noetel et al. 2021, *Review of Educational Research* 91(2), 105 randomized trials, 7,776 students:
  - Video replacing existing teaching: g = 0.28.
  - Video supplementing existing teaching: g = 0.80.
  - More effective for skills than knowledge.
  - "Unlikely to be detrimental."
  - Source: [AERA news release](https://www.aera.net/Newsroom/Video-Improves-Learning-in-Higher-Education-A-Systematic-Review)
- **Medical school lecture redesigned with multimedia principles** (Issa et al. 2013): improved immediate transfer (d = 0.76) and delayed transfer (d = 1.17) — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- **Offline digital education for medical students** (Digital Health Education Collaboration, *JMIR* 2019; 36 RCTs/cRCTs, 3,325 students; included computer-based videos):
  - Knowledge SMD = 0.11 (95% CI −0.11 to 0.32; low-quality evidence).
  - Skills SMD = 1.05 (0.15–1.95; 4 studies; low quality).
  - Source: [PubMed 30907731](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30907731/)
- **Online video for basic surgical skills** (*J Surg Educ* 2022; 11 studies, 715 participants): no difference vs. conventional teaching (SMD −0.02, 95% CI −0.34 to 0.30) — [PubMed 35933308](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35933308/)
- **Video-based coaching in surgery**
  - *World J Surg* 2025 (15 studies, 382 participants): performance SMD 0.71 (0.37–1.04) — [PubMed 39938917](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39938917/)
  - *Surg Endosc* 2023 (11 RCTs, residents): post-coaching SMD 0.53 (0.00–1.01, p = 0.05). Benefit greatest for lower-baseline trainees — [PubMed 35739431](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35739431/)
- **Flipped classroom in health professions** (28 studies): SMD 0.33 (0.21–0.46). More effective with quizzes at the start of class — [PubMed 29544495](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29544495/)
- **Dental: video vs. live demonstrations** (*BMC Med Educ* 2025; 10 studies, 8 meta-analyzed):
  - Psychomotor skills SMD 0.68 (95% CI −0.62 to 1.98; I² = 96%).
  - Knowledge SMD 0.28 (−0.83 to 1.39; I² = 67%).
  - Students valued video for repeatability and clarity.
  - The paper's "significantly enhanced" conclusion is not supported by its own CIs, and it has internal inconsistencies. No GRADE assessment.
  - Source: [PMC11756184](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11756184/)
- **Prosthodontic technology-enhanced learning** (*Eur J Dent Educ* 2024; 14 RCTs, all high risk of bias or some concerns): video demonstrations improved *self-reported* understanding and confidence. Practical skills were mostly comparable; knowledge results were contradictory — [PubMed 39223737](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39223737/)
- **Endodontics-specific**
  - Undergraduate endodontic practice (*Iran Endod J* 2017): video-assisted conventional instruction gave the highest knowledge scores vs. live demonstration or video teaching alone; it beat video alone significantly (p = 0.011) — [PubMed 28512486](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28512486/)
  - Endodontic e-learning app pilot (*BMC Med Educ* 2022): better first real-patient RCT outcomes and fewer errors vs. controls — [PubMed 35606729](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35606729/)
  - Video-based decision aid for asymptomatic persistent endodontic lesions (*Int Endod J* 2024; patients, n = 50): median knowledge 58.3 → 87.5; decisional conflict 25.0 → 3.1 — [PubMed 38251804](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38251804/). This shows video works for endodontic communication, though in patients.
- **YouTube endodontic content quality**
  - Minimally invasive endodontics videos: lower-quality videos had *higher* view counts — [PubMed 40411276](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40411276/)
  - Pre-endodontic build-up videos: most were low quality, even though 56% were uploaded by endodontists — [PubMed 40904257](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40904257/)
  - Mean duration of endodontic access-cavity videos ≈ 686 s — [PubMed 35947634](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35947634/)
- **Moderators in Mayer's corpus:** a secondary summary reports that university and medical trainees showed the largest benefits — [Hendrick summary of Cromley & Chen 2025 (secondary)](https://carlhendrick.substack.com/p/which-multimedia-strategies-actually)

### Inferences
- The realistic benefit of a single 90-second explainer is as a *supplement*: a hook and a conceptual anchor pointing to the paper or longer CE content. Supplementing gives larger effects than replacing (g = 0.80 vs. 0.28).
- Clinician audiences reward credible, high-quality content, but platforms reward views regardless of quality (YouTube endo data). Explicit citation of the underlying evidence on screen (author, journal, year) is a cheap credibility signal. This is an inference, not tested.
- Expect knowledge gains to be modest; video's advantage is strongest for skills and procedures. The placebo/nocebo explainer aims to shift *conceptual understanding and communication behavior*. Pair it with a concrete, actionable "say this, not that" script, which converts declarative knowledge into a performable behavior.

### Gaps
- I found no RCT of short animated explainers vs. lectures or text for *practicing* endodontists or dental specialists.
- I found no study of social-media or vertical-video continuing education for dentists measuring learning outcomes.
- Dental video meta-analyses are small, heterogeneous and at high risk of bias. The 2025 BMC Med Educ pooled effects should be cited as "inconclusive, point estimates favor video."
- I did not retrieve Cook et al.'s health-professions internet-learning meta-analyses (JAMA 2008; Acad Med 2010) in this session.

## Q8. Data visualization in instructional video: presenting statistics (percentages, odds ratios) so viewers understand them

### Takeaway
Health professionals misread conditional probabilities and relative risks as often as lay people do. Better formats:
- natural frequencies ("X out of 100") over percentages or conditional probabilities;
- absolute over relative risks;
- simple static, grouped icon arrays.

Animating icon arrays *hurt* or did not help in the one direct test. Animated trend displays are fast for presentation but less accurate for analysis than static small multiples. Animate transitions *into* a static, readable final frame, then hold it.

### Cited Findings
- **Natural frequencies vs. conditional probabilities** (McDowell & Jacobs 2017 meta-analysis): in the simplest design, 4% correct with probabilities vs. 24% with natural frequencies. A "non-trivial amount" of people still fail with natural frequencies — [Weber et al. 2018, Frontiers in Psychology (citing McDowell & Jacobs)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6194348/); [SMDM abstract](https://smdm.confex.com/smdm/16BEC/webprogram/Paper9915.html)
- **Gigerenzer et al. 2007** (*Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 8(2):53–96): 160 gynecologists' most frequent answer to a mammography positive-predictive-value problem was 90%, when the correct value was about 10%. With natural frequencies, variability dropped sharply and the modal answer was correct. The authors recommend absolute over relative risks because relative risks "are generally large numbers and thus are easily used to capture people's attention" — [PSPI PDF](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~aldous/157/Papers/health_stats.pdf); [PubMed 26161749](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26161749/)
- **Akl et al. 2011 Cochrane review** (35 studies, 83 comparisons):
  - Natural frequencies were better understood than percentages (SMD 0.69).
  - Relative risk reduction was understood about as well as absolute risk reduction but was perceived as larger and more persuasive.
  - ARR was better understood than NNT.
  - **No differences between health professionals and consumers.**
  - Evidence quality: moderate.
  - Source: [Cochrane plain-language summary](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD006776_using-different-statistical-formats-presenting-health-information)
- **Galesic, Garcia-Retamero & Gigerenzer 2009** (*Health Psychology*): icon arrays are "a promising way of communicating medical risks to a wide range of patient groups, including older adults with lower numeracy skills" — [MPG PDF](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_2099767_5/component/file_2562291/MG_Using_2009.pdf) (accessed via search snippet; full text returned 403)
- **Garcia-Retamero & Cokely 2017** (*Human Factors* 59(4):582; systematic review): visual aids benefit people across numeracy and graph-literacy levels *when transparent* (promoting unbiased understanding). Simple training in reading icon arrays extends benefits to low graph-literacy groups — [EurekAlert release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/897052)
- **Zikmund-Fisher et al. 2012** (*JMIR* 14(4):e106), animated icon arrays: compared with static grouped-icon arrays, "no animations significantly improved any outcomes, and most showed significant performance degradations." The authors concluded static risk graphics appear optimal for comparing two risks — [JMIR](https://jmir.org/2012/4/e106)
- **Zikmund-Fisher et al. 2014** (*Med Decis Making* 34(4):443–453): icon type affects risk perception and recall. "Restroom" person icons gave the best outcomes; the optimal type may depend on numeracy and graph literacy — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/medema/v34y2014i4p443-453.html)
- **Robertson et al. 2008** (IEEE TVCG 14(6), InfoVis): static trend displays were significantly faster for analysis than animation, and small multiples were more accurate. Animation was the fastest for *presentation*. A 2019 mobile-phone replication (Brehmer et al.) found small multiples faster and more accurate than animation for trend comparison — [Robertson et al. PDF](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~stasko/papers/infovis08-anim.pdf); [Brehmer et al. arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.03919)
- Spatial contiguity: labels *on* the graphic rather than in a legend (d = 0.82 Mayer; g = 0.63 independent) — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf); [Noetel et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329)

### Inferences (rules for placebo/nocebo statistics)
- **Convert pooled results to natural frequencies on a common denominator.** For example: "In placebo arms, about X of every 10 patients reported meaningful pain relief." Keep one denominator (10 or 100) throughout the video.
- **Avoid odds ratios and SMDs as the on-screen headline.** If the source reports an OR or SMD, translate it to absolute numbers or a plain-language magnitude ("about as large as…"). Put the original statistic as a small citation footnote for expert credibility. Akl found professionals are no better than consumers at formats, so experts benefit from translation too.
- **Pair relative with absolute.** Never show a relative change alone. Relative effects are perceived as larger and more persuasive, which can come across as hype to skeptical experts.
- **Icon arrays.** In a vertical frame, use a 10×10 grid (or 5×10/10×10 stacked for two arms) with grouped (not scattered) filled icons and simple person icons. Show the placebo and nocebo arms as two adjacent arrays with labels directly beside them (spatial contiguity).
  - Animate only the *reveal*: icons filling in group order, synced to narration (temporal contiguity, dynamic signaling).
  - End on a **static frame held 3–4 s**, since animated arrays degraded comprehension in Zikmund-Fisher 2012.
- **Bar/trend charts:** at most one chart per segment, direct labels, no legend, no gridline clutter. Animate the build, then hold. Use small multiples (vertically stacked in 9:16) rather than morphing animation when viewers must compare.
- **Narration–number alignment:** say the number exactly as written on screen ("3 in 10," not "thirty percent" while "3/10" is displayed).

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve a peer-reviewed study on clinicians' misreading of odds ratios as risk ratios. This is a well-known issue, but no source was verified here, so cite cautiously.
- I found no study testing icon arrays *inside narrated instructional video* (vs. static print or web). The static-over-animated recommendation comes from web-based risk-graphic studies.
- McDowell & Jacobs (2017) and Galesic et al. (2009) were verified via citing papers and search snippets, not full text.

## Q9 (synthesis required by constraints). Concrete design rules for a 90-second narrated, animated, vertical (9:16) explainer on placebo/nocebo in endodontics for expert clinicians

### Takeaway
Build a tightly segmented, system-paced narrated animation:
- one claim and 3–4 beats;
- ~180–220 words;
- representational and "drawn-on" visuals only;
- keyword labels next to graphics, plus burned-in synced captions;
- statistics as natural frequencies in static, grouped icon arrays;
- a natural, enthusiastic neural voice with a pronunciation dictionary;
- no decorative motion, music bed or talking-head inset.

Trim novice scaffolds such as pre-training and over-narration, but keep signaling and contiguity, which held up across expertise levels.

### Cited Findings
- System-paced and complex media benefit most from good design (g = 0.41 vs. 0.27; g = 0.70 vs. 0.20) — [Noetel et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329)
- Contiguity (g = 0.74), signaling, modality, and coherence are among the most robust principles — [Noetel et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329)
- Short, adjacent key-word text helps (Mayer & Johnson 2008; key words added to audio g = 0.29) — [Mayer & Johnson DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.100.2.380); [Noetel et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329)
- Pre-training and personalization may not apply to experienced learners — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Modern TTS gives learning parity with human narration; humans retain a social-presence edge — [Netland et al. 2025](https://media-and-learning.eu/subject/artificial-intelligence/when-ai-took-over-my-teaching-videos-students-enjoyed-them-less-but-learned-the-same/); [Craig & Schroeder 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/0735633118802877)
- Natural frequencies, absolute risks, and static icon arrays aid comprehension; animated icon arrays do not — [Akl et al. 2011](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD006776_using-different-statistical-formats-presenting-health-information); [Zikmund-Fisher et al. 2012](https://jmir.org/2012/4/e106)

### Inferences (actionable rule set)
1. **Structure.** Five segments of about 15–20 s each, with a visual reset and a ~0.5 s pause at each boundary:
   - (a) Hook plus claim, stated up front as an advance organizer.
   - (b) Mechanism: expectation → descending modulation → reported pain, as a drawn-on schematic.
   - (c) Placebo-response magnitude in endodontic pain trials, shown as an icon array.
   - (d) Nocebo risk from clinician wording, shown as a contrast array or side-by-side phrasing.
   - (e) Clinical action: "say this / not that," then a summary card that matches the hook.
2. **Word budget.** About 180–220 narrated words; one idea per sentence; slow down and pause after every number.
3. **Visual style.** Representational animation only. Use a "drawn-on" line build (dynamic-drawing analogue) and animated arrows or highlights synced to narration (dynamic signals). No decorative motion, stock B-roll, persistent logos or background music under speech (coherence).
4. **Text.** Two to six word keyword labels placed *on or next to* the graphic element they name, plus burned-in captions synced to narration in a band that does not cover key graphics or the platform UI. Offer full CC/SRT for accessibility and L2 viewers. Print technical terms the first time they are spoken.
5. **Expert calibration.**
   - No glossary or pre-training segment; at most a 2–3 s inline definition of "nocebo".
   - Do not narrate what the visual already shows. Narrate the claim, magnitude and implication.
   - Keep signaling and contiguity.
   - Use a collegial rather than consumer tone.
6. **Statistics.**
   - Natural frequencies with one constant denominator.
   - Absolute numbers first; any relative or OR/SMD values as small citation text.
   - Grouped 10×10 person-icon arrays, two arms stacked vertically and labeled directly.
   - Animate the fill, then hold a static frame for 3–4 s.
   - Spoken and written numbers must match exactly.
7. **Voice.** A natural, warm, moderately enthusiastic neural TTS voice (~150–165 wpm base rate) with a custom pronunciation dictionary. Avoid pairing it with a static or low-embodiment avatar. If any human or avatar appears, it should gesture, point or draw. Consider an on-screen author or source credit for credibility.
8. **Sources on screen.** Small, persistent-but-unobtrusive citation lines (author, journal, year) on data frames. Expert viewers judge credibility, and low-quality endodontic videos are common on platforms.
9. **Generative hook.** Close with one reflective question (e.g., "How do you describe post-op pain to patients?") or a link to a short quiz. Generative activity has d = 0.71, and adding video to existing teaching beats replacing it (g = 0.80 vs. 0.28).
10. **Make the loop useful.** End frame equals the summary of the claim, so a second loop reinforces rather than repeats an intro.

### Gaps
- None of these rules has been tested as a package with endodontists. They are evidence-informed extrapolations, mainly from undergraduate lab studies, independent meta-analyses, and health-risk-communication research. If feasible, a small A/B test would be worthwhile, e.g., keyword-only vs. full burned-in captions, or human vs. neural voice, with residents measuring immediate comprehension of the natural-frequency claims.
