## 1. Injection pain and needle fear: prevalence, and evidence for techniques that make the injection painless

### Takeaway
Fear of the dental injection is common (>25% of adults report at least one clinically significant injection fear; ~1 in 20 avoid care because of it) and is the single strongest correlate of patients self-labelling as "difficult to anesthetize". No single device abolishes injection pain: the best-supported, cheapest measures are slow deposition (≥60 s per cartridge), warming to body temperature, topical anesthetic where it works (anterior maxilla; long contact time), and vibration/CCLAD as adjuncts with small-to-moderate effects (≈9–20 mm on a 100-mm VAS); buffering and needle gauge have little or inconsistent effect on injection pain in adults.

### Cited Findings

**Prevalence and consequences of injection/needle fear**
- University of Washington survey (students and staff, 1995): ">25 percent of adults surveyed expressed at least one clinically significant fear of injections"; "almost one in 20" reported avoiding, cancelling or not appearing for dental appointments because of injection fear. Four dimensions: general fear (pain of injection), fear of bodily injury, fear of acquired disease, and fears about the local anesthetic itself (side effects, inadequate anesthesia). Milgrom P, Coldwell SE, Getz T, Weinstein P, Ramsay DS. J Am Dent Assoc 1997;128(6):756-66. PMID 9188235 **[A]** — [Milgrom 1997](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.1997.0301)
- MDAS validation (n = 1,392): 13% expressed extreme anxiety about injections but were only "fairly" or less anxious about drilling, i.e., an injection-specific subgroup that the original Corah DAS (no injection item) misses. Humphris GM, Morrison T, Lindsay SJ. Community Dent Health 1995;12(3):143-50. PMID 7584581 **[A]** — [Humphris 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7584581/)
- General (non-dental) needle fear, systematic review/meta-analysis (119 studies; 35 in meta-analysis): prevalence 20–50% in adolescents and 20–30% in young adults, falling with age; more prevalent in females; 16% of adult patients avoided influenza vaccination because of needle fear. McLenon J, Rogers MAM. J Adv Nurs 2019;75(1):30-42. PMID 30109720 **[A]** — [McLenon 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/jan.13818)
- EHR cross-sectional study, University of Iowa (n = 12,400 patient records): 11.4% (n = 1,411) self-reported being difficult to anesthetize; adjusted odds ratios: needle anxiety AOR 29.03 (95% CI 23.80–35.52), dental fear AOR 3.60 (3.01–4.31), female gender AOR 1.61 (1.32–1.96), mental-health disorders AOR 1.21, neurological/nerve disorders AOR 1.30, problems with general anesthesia AOR 1.46; prior root canal treatment AOR 0.82 (0.68–0.99). Bowers RD et al. J Dent Anesth Pain Med 2025;25(2):97-108. PMID 40201231 **[A]** — [Bowers 2025](https://doi.org/10.17245/jdapm.2025.25.2.97)
- Australian survey-based clinician guide on fear of injections and of numbness (results not in abstract). Armfield JM, Milgrom P. SAAD Dig 2011;27:33-9. PMID 21323034 **[A – abstract contains background only]** — [Armfield & Milgrom 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21323034/)
- Cardiovascular reactivity peaks before the injection: review reports significant heart-rate/blood-pressure changes "before administration of a local anaesthetic", and that pain "plays an important role in the cardiovascular response". Brand HS. Int Dent J 1999;49(1):60-6. PMID 10887475 **[A]** — [Brand 1999](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1875-595x.1999.tb00509.x)

**Topical anesthetic**
- Retrospective analysis, Ohio State (1,080 patients; 2,336 injections; 27-gauge needle; 20% benzocaine applied before 720 injections): no difference in needle-insertion pain for IANB or posterior maxillary infiltration; significant reduction only for maxillary anterior injections (P = .0041). Nusstein JM, Beck M. Anesth Prog 2003;50(4):159-63. PMID 14959903 **[A]** — [Nusstein & Beck 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14959903/)
- Randomized double-blind split-mouth trial in patients awaiting apical surgery (lidocaine–prilocaine gauze 2 min vs water): topical significantly reduced both insertion and injection pain; injection pain > insertion pain; high-MDAS patients reported higher pain, but "topical anesthetics reduced the effect of anxiety on increasing pain". Cho SY et al. J Endod 2017;43(3):364-369. PMID 28110919 **[A]** — [Cho 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2016.10.036)
- Narrative review: topical anesthetic "does not guarantee pain-free dental local anaesthesia"; efficacy depends on duration of application and needle gauge. Meechan JG. J Dent 2000;28(1):3-14. PMID 10666956 **[A]** — [Meechan 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0300-5712(99)00041-x)
- Precooling vs topical anesthetic in adults, systematic review/meta-analysis (8 RCTs, n = 415): 6 of 8 trials favoured precooling; pooled pain after anesthesia significantly lower with precooling; no difference at needle insertion. de Andrade ABL et al. J Clin Exp Dent 2025;17(3):e341-e349. PMID 40231138 **[A]** — [de Andrade 2025](https://doi.org/10.4317/jced.62351)
- ADA 2024 guideline (toothache, temporary management): low-certainty evidence that 10% (2 RCTs) or 20% (3 RCTs) topical benzocaine increases the proportion with substantial pain reduction at 20–30 min vs placebo. Carrasco-Labra A et al. J Am Dent Assoc 2024;155(2):102-117.e9. PMID 38325969 **[FT]** — [ADA 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.adaj.2023.10.009)

**Slow injection**
- Double-blind randomized crossover, 38 healthy adult volunteers, IANB 2.0 mL 2% lidocaine 1:80,000: slow (60 s) vs rapid (15 s) injection gave more episodes of no response to maximal pulp stimulation in molars (220 vs 159, P < .001), premolars (253 vs 216, P = .003) and lateral incisors (119 vs 99, P = .049), and slow IANB "was more comfortable" (P = .021). Kanaa MD, Meechan JG, Corbett IP, Whitworth JM. J Endod 2006;32(10):919-23. PMID 16982264 **[A]** — [Kanaa 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2006.04.004)
- AAE endorses this: "A slow inferior alveolar nerve block injection (60 seconds) results in a higher success rate of pulpal anesthesia than a rapid injection (15 seconds)… There is also less pain with the slow injection." AAE, ENDODONTICS: Colleagues for Excellence, Winter 2009 (author Al Reader) **[FT]** — [AAE Winter 2009](https://www.aae.org/specialty/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2017/07/winter09ecfe.pdf)

**Buffered local anesthetic (sodium bicarbonate)**
- Systematic review/meta-analysis (14 RCTs, intraoral injections): buffered lidocaine "did not result in less pain during intraoral injections": mean difference −6.4 (95% CI −12.81 to 0.01) on a 0–100 scale; onset faster by 1.26 min for IANB and 1.37 min in inflamed tissues, judged not clinically relevant given preparation time. Aulestia-Viera PV, Braga MM, Borsatti MA. Int Endod J 2018;51(8):862-876. PMID 29377171 **[A]** — [Aulestia-Viera 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.12899)
- Meta-analysis restricted to IANB: buffered lidocaine 5.0 units lower injection pain on 0–100 VAS (95% CI −9.13 to −0.77; P = .02) and 48 s faster onset; no difference in proportion with painless injection (P = .059) or success (P = .290); evidence low–moderate. Guo J, Yin K, Roges R, Enciso R. J Dent Anesth Pain Med 2018;18(3):129-142. PMID 29984317 **[A]** — [Guo 2018](https://doi.org/10.17245/jdapm.2018.18.3.129)
- In SIP specifically (RCT, 4% buffered lidocaine IANB): injection pain not different (P = .9080); success 32% buffered vs 40% non-buffered (P = .4047). Schellenberg J et al. J Endod 2015;41(6):791-6. PMID 25841959 **[A]** — [Schellenberg 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2015.02.022)
- Paediatric meta-analysis for context only (5 RCTs, ages 4–12): subjective injection pain SMD −0.59 (95% CI −1.00 to −0.17). Bhatnagar A, Gupta D. J Dent Anesth Pain Med 2026;26(1):19-28. PMID 41640719 **[A]** — [Bhatnagar 2026](https://doi.org/10.17245/jdapm.2026.26.1.19)

**Warming the cartridge**
- Split-mouth RCT, 72 dental-student volunteers, maxillary buccal infiltration 0.9 mL lidocaine 2%: VAS 35.3 ± 16.71 mm at room temperature (21 °C) vs 15 ± 14.67 mm at 42 °C (P < .001). Aravena PC et al. Local Reg Anesth 2018;11:9-13. PMID 29503582 **[A]** — [Aravena 2018](https://doi.org/10.2147/LRA.S147288)
- Systematic review (4 studies): 3 of 4 found significantly lower self-reported pain with solution warmed to ~37 °C; both studies that measured observed pain favoured warming; authors call for higher-quality trials. Tirupathi SP, Rajasekhar S. J Dent Anesth Pain Med 2020;20(4):187-194. PMID 32934984 **[A]** — [Tirupathi 2020](https://doi.org/10.17245/jdapm.2020.20.4.187)
- In SIP (RCT, IANB 2% lignocaine): pre-heated and buffered solutions both produced less intraoperative pain than conventional; "Preheated local anesthetics caused the least pain, followed by buffered". Gandhi N et al. PeerJ 2022;10:e14187. PMID 36281366 **[A]** — [Gandhi 2022](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.14187)

**Needle gauge / design**
- 810 adult patients, 930 injections, patient-blinded, 25/27/30-gauge: "no statistically significant difference in perceived injection pain based on needle gauge" for any site. Flanagan T, Wahl MJ, Schmitt MM, Wahl JA. Gen Dent 2007;55(3):216-7. PMID 17511363 **[A]** — [Flanagan 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17511363/)
- Double-blind split-mouth RCT (n = 20): larger-bore vs standard-bore 27-gauge needle, IANB VAS 38.9 ± 22.7 vs 37.1 ± 22.4 mm (P = .70) — no benefit. McPherson JS et al. Anesth Prog 2015;62(1):2-7. PMID 25849467 **[A]** — [McPherson 2015](https://doi.org/10.2344/0003-3006-62.1.2)
- Exception — the intrapulpal injection: RCT in SIP, 31-gauge vs 27-gauge with/without topical lignocaine–prilocaine: mean pain 3.7 and 2.3 (31G without/with topical) vs 5.6 and 5.7 (27G); 52% and 80% vs 12% and 8% of patients "comfortable" (VAS < 4); intrapulpal success 100% in all groups. Suresh N et al. Eur Endod J 2020;5(3):191-198. PMID 33353913 **[A]** — [Suresh 2020](https://doi.org/10.14744/eej.2020.38358). Similar direction (31G < 26G for intrapulpal pain; n = 80): Rairam S et al. J Conserv Dent Endod 2024;27(8):849-852. PMID 39372572 **[A]** — [Rairam 2024](https://doi.org/10.4103/JCDE.JCDE_357_24)

**Vibration / counter-stimulation (DentalVibe and similar)**
- Split-mouth RCT, 166 adults, 332 injections for exodontia: vibration (no topical) vs topical anesthetic — pain at penetration and during injection and heart-rate change all significantly lower with vibration (P < .001), "regardless of the gender, injection technique (infiltration or block) or anxiety level". Salma RG et al. Int J Oral Maxillofac Surg 2021;50(3):407-415. PMID 32723512 **[A]** — [Salma 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijom.2020.07.010)
- Split-mouth RCT, 20 adults, bilateral IANB, both with topical: VAS 21.2 ± 18.6 mm with vibratory device vs 38.7 ± 23.3 mm without (P = .006). DiFelice MG et al. Compend Contin Educ Dent 2014;35(4):246-. PMID 24773250 **[A]** — [DiFelice 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24773250/)
- Open-label split-mouth, 50 adults, IANB: VAS range 4–10 with 2% lidocaine gel vs 0–6 with DentalVibe (P < .001). Joshi S et al. J Dent Anesth Pain Med 2021;21(1):41-47. PMID 33585683 **[A]** — [Joshi 2021](https://doi.org/10.17245/jdapm.2021.21.1.41)
- Pilot (n = 60, mostly dental/medical students): DentalVibe DV3 significantly reduced pain/unpleasantness for long buccal and IANB injections (P < .05). Shaefer JR, Lee SJ, Anderson NK. Compend Contin Educ Dent 2017;38(6):e5-e8. PMID 28586233 **[A]** — [Shaefer 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28586233/)
- Negative trial: crossover, 32 subjects, maxillary infiltration — "no significant differences" in VAS or FACES; preference evenly split. Erdogan O et al. Anesth Prog 2018;65(2):95-99. PMID 29952639 **[A]** — [Erdogan 2018](https://doi.org/10.2344/anpr-65-02-01)

**Computer-controlled local anesthetic delivery (CCLAD: The Wand/STA etc.)**
- Systematic review/meta-analysis of crossover split-mouth studies (20 studies, n = 973; children and adults): pain "over 9 points lower" with CCLAD on a 0–100 scale (95% CI −12.90 to −5.53; P < .001; 8 studies pooled); no difference in physiological pain parameters; "data about anxiety are inconsistent"; conclusion: "significantly slightly less pain perception". Libonati A et al. Eur J Paediatr Dent 2018;19(4):324-332. PMID 30567452 **[A]** — [Libonati 2018](https://doi.org/10.23804/ejpd.2018.19.04.14)
- Systematic review of RCTs (14 trials, children and adults; no pooled estimate in abstract): CCLAD gave significantly lower pain-perception scores and more cooperative behaviour. Altuhafy M, Sodhi GS, Khan J. J Dent Anesth Pain Med 2024;24(4):245-264. PMID 39118810 **[A]** — [Altuhafy 2024](https://doi.org/10.17245/jdapm.2024.24.4.245)
- Intraligamentary injection (which CCLAD facilitates) vs IANB, meta-analysis in adults (7 studies): less injection pain with intraligamentary (OR 0.32, 95% CI 0.1–1), fewer cardiovascular disturbances (OR 0.12, 0.02–0.69), no difference in failure rate. Shabazfar N et al. Clin Oral Investig 2014;18(2):351-8. PMID 24077785 **[A]** — [Shabazfar 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00784-013-1113-1)

**Choice of solution and injection pain**
- Cochrane review: moderate-quality evidence of "no difference in pain on injection" between 4% articaine 1:100,000 and 2% lidocaine 1:100,000 (lidocaine slightly less post-injection pain). St George G et al. Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2018;7:CD006487. PMID 29990391 **[A]** — [St George 2018](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD006487.pub2)
- Buccal infiltration can hurt more than a block in an inflamed mandible: RCT in mandibular second premolars with irreversible pulpitis found equal success but more administration discomfort and greater heart-rate rise with buccal infiltration than IANB. Yilmaz K, Tunga U, Ozyurek T. Niger J Clin Pract 2018;21(4):473-477. PMID 29607860 **[A]** — [Yilmaz 2018](https://doi.org/10.4103/njcp.njcp_135_17)

### Inferences
- Effect sizes for every injection-comfort adjunct are modest (≈5–20 mm/100) and none is consistently effective across sites; the largest and most reproducible signals in adults are for slow deposition, warming, and vibration in block injections. A bundle (topical with adequate contact time or precooling + warmed cartridge + slow 60-s deposition ± vibration/CCLAD) is more defensible than reliance on any one device.
- Because anxiety amplifies reported injection pain (Cho 2017) and needle anxiety is the dominant predictor of "difficult to anesthetize" self-labels (Bowers 2025), asking a single screening question about injection fear and about previous anesthetic failure identifies the patients in whom the bundle matters most.
- Buffering should not be sold to patients as a comfort measure for IANB in adults: pooled benefit is ≤6 mm and absent in SIP trials.

### Gaps
- No adult-only meta-analysis of CCLAD with anxiety (rather than pain) as the primary outcome was found; Libonati 2018 explicitly calls anxiety data inconsistent.
- No trial was found that tests whether a painless injection in one endodontic visit reduces dental-anxiety scores at later visits (the hypothesised "interruption of the fear–pain cycle" is inferred from conditioning literature in Section 4, not directly tested).
- DentalVibe studies are small, often unblinded, and mixed (Erdogan 2018 negative); manufacturer involvement was not assessed here.
- "Two-stage" injection and stretching/distraction-of-mucosa techniques: no adult RCT evidence was located in this search.
- Sample size for Cho 2017 was not captured from the abstract excerpt read.

## 2. Profound anesthesia in the "hot tooth" (SIP): IANB success, supplemental techniques, premedication — and how intraoperative pain feeds fear

### Takeaway
A conventional IANB alone gives pain-free access in only roughly 19–56% of mandibular posterior teeth with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis (lip numbness is not a surrogate), so a resident should plan supplemental anesthesia from the outset: articaine buccal infiltration (adds modestly), then intraosseous (≈86–91%) or intraligamentary (≈75%, ≈95% with re-injection), with intrapulpal as last resort; oral NSAID (ibuprofen ≥600 mg, ketorolac, diclofenac) or dexamethasone premedication roughly doubles IANB success (RR ≈1.8–2.1). High dental anxiety is associated with more intraoperative pain (OR ≈4) and lower block success, and painful/helpless treatment experiences are the strongest known precipitants of pathological dental fear — which is the mechanistic argument for treating anesthesia as an anxiety intervention.

### Cited Findings

**Baseline IANB success in SIP**
- AAE: "Clinical studies in endodontics in patients with irreversible pulpitis have found success (mild or no pain upon endodontic access or initial instrumentation) with the inferior alveolar nerve block alone between 19% and 56% of the time." Also: lip numbness "does not guarantee pulpal anesthesia"; repeating the IANB when the lip is numb "does not help"; two cartridges are not better than one in healthy volunteers; test with cold refrigerant or EPT before access (but in irreversible pulpitis a negative cold test "may not always indicate pulpal anesthesia"); "Patients who report a history of previous difficulty with anesthesia are more likely to experience unsuccessful anesthesia… ask the patient"; and "patients in pain are often apprehensive, which lowers their pain threshold." AAE Colleagues for Excellence, Winter 2009 **[FT]** — [AAE Winter 2009](https://www.aae.org/specialty/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2017/07/winter09ecfe.pdf)
- AAE Winter 2015: "some studies report an eight-fold increase in local anesthesia failure after inferior alveolar nerve block injection in pain patients versus normal controls." **[FT]** — [AAE Winter 2015, "A 3D Approach for Treating Acute Pain"](https://www.aae.org/specialty/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2017/07/ecfeacutedentalpain.pdf)
- Retrospective Ohio State data: IANB success 28% (first molars), 25% (second molars), 39% (premolars); supplemental articaine buccal infiltration after failed IANB succeeded in 42%, 48% and 73% respectively — "would not be high enough to ensure profound pulpal anesthesia". Fowler S, Drum M, Reader A, Beck M. J Endod 2016;42(3):390-2. PMID 26831048 **[A]** — [Fowler 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2015.12.025)
- RCT (n = 72 emergency patients): IANB success 24% articaine vs 23% lidocaine (P = .89). Claffey E et al. J Endod 2004;30(8):568-71. PMID 15273637 **[A]** — [Claffey 2004](https://doi.org/10.1097/01.don.0000125317.21892.8f)
- 2025 diagnostic study: IANB failure in 56% of mandibular first molars with SIP despite lip numbness; a positive cold test strongly indicated failure, and a negative cold test should be confirmed with EPT. Afkhami F, Ghabraei S, Hashemi N, Peters OA. J Endod 2025;51(1):28-34. PMID 39521338 **[A]** — [Afkhami 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2024.10.013)
- Systematic review (Pak & White): across root canal treatment studies "supplemental injections were frequently required" (60% ± 24%). Pak JG, White SN. J Endod 2011;37(4):429-38. PMID 21419285 **[A]** — [Pak & White 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2010.12.016)
- Review of treatment recommendations for SIP anesthesia (MeSH-indexed under anti-anxiety agents; abstract gives no numbers). Drum M, Reader A, Nusstein J, Fowler S. J Am Dent Assoc 2017;148(4):267-271. PMID 28190451 **[A]** — [Drum 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.adaj.2017.01.002)

**Articaine vs lidocaine**
- SR/MA of double-blind RCTs in SIP: articaine more likely to achieve success overall (OR 2.21, 95% CI 1.41–3.47); no difference for mandibular block alone (OR 1.44, 0.87–2.38) or maxillary infiltration (OR 3.99, 0.50–31.62); clear advantage for supplemental infiltration after block (OR 3.55, 1.97–6.39). Kung J, McDonagh M, Sedgley CM. J Endod 2015;41(11):1784-94. PMID 26293174 **[A]** — [Kung 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2015.07.001)
- SR/MA (20 RCTs, irreversible pulpitis): articaine RR 1.15 (95% CI 1.10–1.20) per person; lower VAS during injection (MD −0.67) and treatment (MD −3.35); faster onset. Su N et al. Aust Endod J 2016;42(1):4-15. PMID 27062318 **[A]** — [Su 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/aej.12125)
- Umbrella review (5 SR/MAs, all AMSTAR "high"): "Articaine is more effective than lidocaine for local anaesthesia of teeth with irreversible pulpitis"; only limited evidence that it is less painful to inject or faster. Nagendrababu V et al. Int Endod J 2020;53(2):200-213. PMID 31491042 **[A]** — [Nagendrababu 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.13215)
- Earlier JADA meta-analysis (adults, healthy + symptomatic): articaine OR 2.44 overall, 3.81 for infiltration, 1.57 for block, "no difference when the authors considered only symptomatic teeth". Brandt RG et al. J Am Dent Assoc 2011;142(5):493-504. PMID 21531931 **[A]** — [Brandt 2011](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.2011.0219)
- Contrary/neutral syntheses for the block itself: Corbella S et al. Quintessence Int 2017;48(1):69-82 (RR 1.05, 95% CI 0.91–1.21 articaine vs lidocaine IANB) PMID 27834417 **[A]** — [Corbella 2017](https://doi.org/10.3290/j.qi.a37131); de Carvalho LF et al. J Dent 2025;163:106175, SR/MA with cost-effectiveness (12 studies, 1,262 participants): no significant efficacy differences among lidocaine 2%, articaine 4%, bupivacaine 0.5%, mepivacaine 2% for IANB; lidocaine most cost-effective. PMID 41075922 **[A]** — [de Carvalho 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdent.2025.106175)
- Articaine buccal infiltration as the *primary* injection for mandibular first molars with SIP vs lidocaine IANB (5 RCTs, 405 teeth): success 72.2% vs 68.1%, RR 1.06 (95% CI 0.93–1.20), moderate certainty; trial-sequential analysis says evidence still inconclusive. Saatchi M et al. Clin Oral Investig 2025;29(3):146. PMID 39982504 **[A]** — [Saatchi 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00784-025-06229-0); commentary Majid OW. Evid Based Dent 2025;26(2):89-90. PMID 40328981 — [Majid 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41432-025-01153-4)

**Supplemental techniques**
- Articaine buccal infiltration after failed IANB in SIP: 58% success (n = 55). Matthews R et al. J Endod 2009;35(3):343-6. PMID 19249592 **[A]** — [Matthews 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2008.11.025)
- Buccal + lingual supplemental infiltration (RCT, n = 84): success 33% (IANB alone) → 47% (lidocaine) → 67% (articaine). Aggarwal V, Jain A, Kabi D. J Endod 2009;35(7):925-9. PMID 19567309 **[A]** — [Aggarwal 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2009.04.012)
- Intraosseous: 88% success when conventional techniques failed (n = 51); 81% of mandibular teeth needed it. Nusstein J et al. J Endod 1998;24(7):487-91. PMID 9693577 **[A]** — [Nusstein 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0099-2399(98)80053-8). AAE summary: Stabident/X-tip success "86% to 91%", immediate onset; vasoconstrictor-containing intraosseous solution may cause transient tachycardia. **[FT]** — [AAE Winter 2009](https://www.aae.org/specialty/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2017/07/winter09ecfe.pdf), [AAE Winter 2015](https://www.aae.org/specialty/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2017/07/ecfeacutedentalpain.pdf)
- Intraligamentary: "about 75% successful. Re-injection will increase success to approximately 95%"; shorter duration than intraosseous. AAE Winter 2009 **[FT]**. Meta-analysis of supplemental intraligamentary injection in irreversible pulpitis (4 studies): RR 3.56 (95% CI 2.86–4.44), I² = 0%. Gupta A et al. J Dent Anesth Pain Med 2022;22(1):1-10. PMID 35169615 **[A]** — [Gupta 2022](https://doi.org/10.17245/jdapm.2022.22.1.1)
- Intrapulpal: "In approximately 5-10% of mandibular posterior teeth with irreversible pulpitis, supplemental injections, even when repeated, do not produce profound anesthesia… The disadvantage is that the injection is painful"; works if given under back-pressure. AAE Winter 2009 **[FT]**. Pain of the intrapulpal injection is reduced with a 31-gauge needle (see Section 1, Suresh 2020).
- Network meta-analysis (mandibular molars with SIP): highest-ranked strategies were supplemental intraosseous injection (2% lidocaine or 4% articaine; OR ≈20 vs IANB alone; SUCRA ≈86–87%) followed by supplemental buccal + lingual articaine infiltration + preoperative NSAIDs (OR 18, 95% CrI 6–56); very low to moderate confidence; no major safety concerns. Zanjir M et al. J Endod 2019;45(12):1435-1464.e10. PMID 31601433 **[A]** — [Zanjir 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2019.09.002)
- SR/MA (lower molars, irreversible pulpitis): increasing anesthetic volume (P = .007), changing anesthetic agent (P = .03) and NSAID premedication (P = .001) improved success; changing block technique did not. Tupyota P et al. Aust Endod J 2018;44(1):14-25. PMID 28736921 **[A]** — [Tupyota 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/aej.12212)
- Buffering for success in pulpally involved teeth: JADA SR of 5 double-blind RCTs found OR 2.29 (95% CI 1.11–4.71; I² = 66%) — [Kattan 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.adaj.2018.11.007) (PMID 30803488 **[A]**) — but the two largest SIP IANB trials were null: 62.5% vs 47.5%, P = .381 — [Saatchi 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2014.09.011) (PMID 25442722 **[A]**); 32% vs 40%, P = .40 — [Schellenberg 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2015.02.022).
- Cryotherapy adjunct after IANB (6 RCTs, n = 506): ice pack improved success OR 1.44 (95% CI 1.04–2.00). Alenezi AT et al. Cureus 2025;17(5):e84046. PMID 40364862 **[A]** — [Alenezi 2025](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.84046)

**Oral premedication to raise IANB success**
- Network meta-analysis, 19 RCTs (n = 1,654): vs placebo, dexamethasone RR 2.92 (95% CI 1.74–4.91; SUCRA 0.96), NSAIDs RR 1.92 (1.63–2.27), tramadol RR 2.03 (1.18–3.49); NSAID + acetaminophen no better than NSAID alone (RR 1.06, 0.79–1.43); effective doses included ketorolac 10 mg, piroxicam 20 mg, ibuprofen 400/600/800 mg (ibuprofen 300 mg ineffective), dexamethasone 0.5 mg. Pulikkotil SJ et al. Int Endod J 2018;51(9):989-1004. PMID 29480930 **[A]** — [Pulikkotil 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.12912)
- SR/MA with trial-sequential analysis, 13 RCTs (n = 1,034): any NSAID RR 1.92 (95% CI 1.55–2.38); ibuprofen 1.83, diclofenac 2.56, ketorolac 2.07; ibuprofen >400 mg RR 1.85 (1.39–2.45) vs ≤400 mg RR 1.78 (0.90–3.55, NS); TSA "conclusive". Nagendrababu V et al. J Endod 2018;44(6):914-922.e2. PMID 29709297 **[A]** — [Nagendrababu 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2018.02.017)
- Ibuprofen-only meta-analysis (7 studies): RR 1.79 (1.32–2.42); in SIP RR 1.55 (1.05–2.29). de Geus JL et al. Aust Endod J 2019;45(2):246-258. PMID 30295006 **[A]** — [de Geus 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/aej.12306)
- Umbrella review (12 SRs, AMSTAR-2 very low to high): NSAID premedication increases IANB success. Só GB et al. Clin Oral Investig 2023;27(5):1885-1897. PMID 36988825 **[A]** — [Só 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00784-023-04979-3)
- Most recent (2026) SR/MA, adults with SIP, 11 trials (n = 737): pain-free IANB 64.6% vs 28.4%, RR 2.06 (95% CI 1.48–2.86), I² = 63%, prediction interval 0.83–5.10, low certainty; diclofenac and ketorolac consistent, ibuprofen imprecise; "clinicians should still anticipate supplemental anesthesia". Sabeti MA et al. J Dent Anesth Pain Med 2026;26(5):373-389. PMID 42889447 **[A]** — [Sabeti 2026](https://doi.org/10.17245/jdapm.2026.26.122)
- Corticosteroid SR/MA with TSA (8 trials): oral dexamethasone RR 1.80 (95% CI 1.35–2.41) vs placebo; 0.5 mg and 4 mg both effective; equivalent to NSAIDs; GRADE moderate (vs placebo). Suresh N et al. Int Endod J 2026;59(9):1766-1777. PMID 41981812 **[A]** — [Suresh 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.70162)
- Caveat on self-medication: AAE notes 800 mg ibuprofen reduced palpation pain by 40% and percussion/cold pain by 25% in SIP/SAP teeth, so "clinicians should ask which analgesics were taken in the 4-6 hours prior to clinical evaluation." **[FT]** — [AAE Winter 2015](https://www.aae.org/specialty/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2017/07/ecfeacutedentalpain.pdf)

**Anxiety ↔ anesthetic failure ↔ intraoperative pain**
- Cross-sectional, Seville (n = 180): mean intraoperative RCT pain 2.2 ± 2.1 (0–10 VAS); anxiety correlated with intraoperative pain (R = 0.406); multivariate OR 4.0 (95% CI 1.7–9.3; P = .001) for moderate/intense pain in anxious patients. Murillo-Benítez M et al. Int Endod J 2020;53(4):447-454. PMID 31691312 **[A]** — [Murillo-Benítez 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.13245)
- Systematic review of observational studies (4 studies, n = 471): dental anxiety associated with pre- and intraoperative endodontic pain; certainty low/very low. Farias ZBBM et al. Clin Oral Investig 2023;27(10):5709-5718. PMID 37526740 **[A]** — [Farias 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00784-023-05181-1)
- Prospective study in SIP (n = 52; articaine IANB + buccal/lingual infiltration): anesthetic success 84.2% in low-anxiety (MDAS ≤10) vs 15.8% in moderate/high-anxiety (MDAS ≥11) patients (P < .001); salivary cortisol the only independent predictor of failure. Mumcu AK et al. J Dent Sci 2026;21(2):799-806. PMID 42688684 **[A]** — [Mumcu 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jds.2025.10.019)
- Double-blind RCT of ibuprofen premedication in SIP: IANB success 62% (ibuprofen) and 78% (ibuprofen-arginine) vs 34% placebo; patients with successful blocks had lower median anxiety scores (8 vs 15) and lower preoperative pain. de Oliveira JP et al. Clin Oral Investig 2024;28(7):366. PMID 38850383 **[A]** — [de Oliveira 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00784-024-05765-5)
- Conflicting: comparative study (articaine vs bupivacaine, SIP molars) found high anxiety associated with higher pain scores but concluded anesthetic efficacy "did not appear to be influenced by the degree of anxiety". Manteca-Fernández L et al. Dent J (Basel) 2025;13(5):199. PMID 40422619 **[A]** — [Manteca-Fernández 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/dj13050199)
- Anxiolysis does not replace anesthesia: double-blind RCT (n = 58 emergency SIP patients), sublingual triazolam 0.25 mg vs placebo — IANB success 43% vs 57% (P = .43); "profound local anesthesia is still required". Lindemann M et al. J Endod 2008;34(10):1167-70. PMID 18793913 **[A]** — [Lindemann 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2008.07.013). SR of pharmacological anxiety control and intraoperative RCT pain (4 RCTs): "insufficient evidence". Silva IA et al. Eur Endod J 2023;8(2):105-113. PMID 37010201 **[A]** — [Silva 2023](https://doi.org/10.14744/eej.2022.83097)
- With a planned supplemental protocol the experience is good: Iowa prospective cohort (n = 77 mandibular molar RCTs; lidocaine IANB + supplemental buccal articaine): 73% no procedural pain, 95% felt adequately numb, supplemental anesthesia needed in 14%, and 65% "rated the experience better than expected". Kopec E et al. Aust Endod J 2026. PMID 42458676 **[A]** — [Kopec 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/aej.70107)

**How painful treatment feeds subsequent fear (acquisition evidence)**
- Population survey: 71% reported painful, 23% frightening, 9% embarrassing dental experiences; those reporting all three were 22.4 times more likely to be dentally anxious; onset of such experiences was in adulthood for 40%. Locker D, Shapiro D, Liddell A. Community Dent Health 1996;13(2):86-92. PMID 8763138 **[A]** — [Locker 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8763138/)
- Dental patients (n = 1,462): experiences involving helplessness during treatment most strongly associated with high dental anxiety (OR 8.2) and positive dental-phobia screen (OR 16.2); general (non-dental) trauma was not. Oosterink FM, de Jongh A, Aartman IH. J Anxiety Disord 2009;23(4):451-7. PMID 18990543 **[A]** — [Oosterink 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.janxdis.2008.09.002)
- UK students (n = 1,024): 11.2% MDAS ≥19; high anxiety clustered with "extreme helplessness during dental treatment", "lack of understanding of the dentist", "extreme embarrassment". Humphris G, King K. J Anxiety Disord 2011;25(2):232-6. PMID 20952156 **[A]** — [Humphris & King 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.janxdis.2010.09.007)
- Adults with irreversible pulpitis (China): 83.1% had moderate or high dental anxiety and 16.2% met specific-phobia criteria; pain at the most recent dental visit (P < .01) and before the present visit correlated with anxiety; "Effective pain control in endodontics is beneficial to manage the anxiety." Dou L et al. BMC Oral Health 2018;18(1):101. PMID 29879974 **[A]** — [Dou 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-018-0563-x)
- Vicious-cycle data (Australia, n = 6,112 and n = 1,036): 29.2% of very fearful people showed the full pattern of delayed visiting + poor oral health + symptom-driven attendance vs 11.6% of non-fearful; 38.5% vs 0.9% in the later study. Armfield JM et al. BMC Oral Health 2007;7:1. PMID 17222356 **[A]** — [Armfield 2007](https://doi.org/10.1186/1472-6831-7-1); Armfield JM. Community Dent Oral Epidemiol 2013;41(3):279-87. PMID 23004917 **[A]** — [Armfield 2013](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdoe.12005)

### Inferences
- The defensible chairside sequence for a mandibular molar with SIP is: (1) NSAID (or dexamethasone) premedication ~1 h pre-op when not contraindicated; (2) slow IANB; (3) wait ≥15 min, confirm lip sign, then cold ± EPT test; (4) articaine buccal (± lingual) infiltration routinely or on positive test; (5) intraosseous or intraligamentary (repeat once) if access is painful; (6) intrapulpal with thin needle and warning only as last resort. This ordering combines AAE guidance with the NMA rankings; no single trial tests the whole pathway.
- Because the block is expected to fail in roughly half of hot mandibular molars, telling the patient in advance that "extra numbing is routine" converts a supplemental injection from an alarming failure into an expected step — consistent with the helplessness/unpredictability findings (Oosterink 2009; Lin 2013 in Section 7).
- The anxiety–anesthesia relationship is bidirectional and confounded (anxious patients present later, with more inflammation and pre-op pain); Mumcu 2026's very large effect (84% vs 16%) is from a single small study and should not be treated as an effect size.
- Sedation/anxiolysis lowers distress but does not improve block success (Lindemann 2008): anesthesia and anxiolysis are complementary, not substitutes.

### Gaps
- No formal AAE *position statement* or clinical practice guideline specific to anesthesia in SIP was located; the AAE material found is the educational newsletter series (2009, 2015). The 2009 issue pre-dates most articaine-infiltration and premedication meta-analyses (it says articaine is "not really" better for blocks, which remains consistent with later block-only data).
- Longitudinal evidence that intraoperative endodontic pain *causes* a later rise in dental anxiety scores in the same adult patients was not found; the link rests on retrospective/cross-sectional association studies.
- Reader/Nusstein/Drum textbook algorithm ("Successful Local Anesthesia for Restorative Dentistry and Endodontics") was not accessible for direct citation.
- Maxillary hot-tooth data and Gow-Gates/Vazirani-Akinosi alternatives were not reviewed in depth.

## 3. Postoperative pain expectations and management as anxiety mitigation

### Takeaway
Post-endodontic pain is usually mild, peaks in the first 24 h and falls to minimal levels by 7 days; NSAIDs alone or with acetaminophen are first-line (ADA 2024; endodontic network meta-analyses), opioids add nothing, and a long-acting anesthetic (0.5% bupivacaine 1:200,000) reduces early pain and analgesic use. Evidence that telling patients this beforehand lowers fear exists (one large analogue RCT; observational data on expectation/optimism), but trials of written or video information show small or null effects on anxiety.

### Cited Findings

**What to tell patients to expect**
- Systematic review of RCT-associated pain: pain prevalence 81% (SD 28) pre-treatment, 40% (24) at 24 h, 11% (14) at 1 week; severity on 100-point scale 54 (24), 24 (12), 5 (5). Pak JG, White SN. J Endod 2011;37(4):429-38. PMID 21419285 **[A]** — [Pak & White 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2010.12.016)
- Persistent pain is uncommon but real: meta-analysis 5.3% (95% CI 3.5–7.2%) all-cause persistent tooth pain after endodontic procedures (7.6% in prospective studies). Nixdorf DR et al. J Endod 2010;36(2):224-30. PMID 20113779 **[A]** — [Nixdorf 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2009.11.007)
- In a National Dental PBRN prospective cohort (708 enrolled, 651 followed), 10.0% had pain at 6 months; each extra day of pain in the week before RCT raised risk (OR 1.19, 95% CI 1.07–1.33) while "optimism about the procedure reduced the risk (odds ratio = 0.39, 95% CI 0.22–0.67)". Nixdorf DR et al. Pain 2016;157(1):159-165. PMID 26335907 **[A]** — [Nixdorf 2016](https://doi.org/10.1097/j.pain.0000000000000343)
- Emergency endodontic patients with SIP: expected pain relief before treatment correlated with post-treatment relief (rho = 0.76) and relief recalled at 6 weeks (rho = 0.69), and predicted lower post-treatment pain and anxiety. Wu LT, Lin CS, Yang SF. Clin Oral Investig 2022;26(1):275-285. PMID 34080062 **[A]** — [Wu 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00784-021-03997-3)

**Analgesic strategy**
- ADA/University of Pittsburgh/Penn guideline 2024 (adolescents ≥12, adults, older adults). Scope: post-extraction pain and *temporary* management of toothache (symptomatic pulpitis ± symptomatic apical periodontitis; necrosis with SAP or acute apical abscess) when definitive care is not immediately available — it does not issue post-root-canal recommendations. Toothache recommendations (all conditional): (1) short-acting local anesthetic for immediate relief (very low certainty); (2) non-opioid analgesics first-line rather than opioids (low); (2.1) start with an NSAID alone "(eg, 400 mg of ibuprofen or 440 mg of naproxen sodium) or in combination with acetaminophen (eg, 500 mg)"; (2.2–2.3) only "in the rare instances" of inadequate control add acetaminophen 325 mg + an acetaminophen 325 mg/opioid combination (hydrocodone 5–7.5 mg or oxycodone 5 mg) at lowest dose, fewest tablets, "rarely exceeds 3 days"; (2.4) if NSAIDs contraindicated, acetaminophen 1,000 mg; (3) for extended temporary management, supplemental "0.5% bupivacaine plus 1:200,000 epinephrine by block or infiltration" or 4% articaine infiltration (very low certainty); topical benzocaine 10–20% as a bridge. Carrasco-Labra A et al. J Am Dent Assoc 2024;155(2):102-117.e9. PMID 38325969; PMC10919895 **[FT]** — [ADA 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.adaj.2023.10.009). The ADA maintains this as a living guideline (page reports "Last evidence search: July 2026") **[FT of web page]** — [ADA living guideline: toothache](https://www.ada.org/resources/research/science/evidence-based-dental-research/living-guideline-program/adult-adolescent-pain-management-toothache)
- ADA 2024 bupivacaine evidence base is indirect (extraction RCTs): very low certainty that bupivacaine before discharge reduces rescue analgesia at 8–48 h; moderate certainty of negligible difference in adverse effects vs lidocaine/mepivacaine. **[FT]** — [ADA 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.adaj.2023.10.009)
- Endodontic network meta-analysis (11 RCTs, post-treatment medication; irreversible pulpitis or necrosis): vs placebo at 6–8 h, NSAID + acetaminophen MD −22 (95% CrI −38 to −7.2; moderate confidence) and NSAID alone MD −21 (−34 to −7.6; very low confidence) on a 100-point scale; at 12 and 24 h only NSAIDs effective; nothing effective at 48 h; "Corticosteroids and opioids did not significantly decrease pain." Zanjir M et al. J Endod 2020;46(10):1387-1402.e4. PMID 32668310 **[A]** — [Zanjir 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2020.07.002)
- SR/MA in patients with preoperative pain (15 studies): ibuprofen 600 mg > placebo at 6 h; ibuprofen 600 mg + acetaminophen 1,000 mg > placebo but not significantly different from ibuprofen alone. Smith EA et al. J Endod 2017;43(1):7-15. PMID 27939729 **[A]** — [Smith 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2016.09.010)
- Index RCT (n = 57, moderate–severe pain, after pulpectomy): ibuprofen 600 mg + acetaminophen 1,000 mg superior to ibuprofen alone and to placebo over 8 h. Menhinick KA et al. Int Endod J 2004;37(8):531-41. PMID 15230906 **[A]** — [Menhinick 2004](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2591.2004.00836.x)
- SR/MA (27 studies, 2,188 patients): non-narcotic analgesics vs placebo SMD −0.50 immediately, −0.76 at 6 h, −1.15 at 12 h, −0.65 at 24 h. Shirvani A et al. J Oral Rehabil 2017;44(9):709-721. PMID 28449307 **[A]** — [Shirvani 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/joor.12519)
- Pre-emptive oral medication NMA (11 RCTs): prednisolone 30–40 mg ranked best for post-RCT pain at 6/12/24 h (MD ≈ −18 to −22), evidence very low–moderate. Nagendrababu V et al. J Endod 2019;45(4):364-371. PMID 30737050 **[A]** — [Nagendrababu 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2018.10.016)
- 2026 SR/MA of single-dose post-RCT analgesics (10 RCTs; 5 pooled, n = 347): diclofenac + acetaminophen best at 6–8 h; naproxen-containing regimens best at 12–24 h. Kelidari K, Samani D. BMC Oral Health 2026;26(1):339. PMID 41588508 **[A]** — [Kelidari 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-025-07458-1)
- AAE "3D" playbook (Diagnosis → Dental treatment → Drugs): definitive treatment is itself analgesic; ibuprofen 400–600 mg; ibuprofen 200 mg + acetaminophen 500 mg up to ibuprofen 400 mg + acetaminophen 1,000 mg give "greater peak analgesia and more consistent analgesia… without increasing adverse side effects"; antibiotics have no analgesic effect. **[FT]** — [AAE Winter 2015](https://www.aae.org/specialty/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2017/07/ecfeacutedentalpain.pdf)

**Long-acting anesthetic**
- RCT (n = 60; mandibular molars, irreversible pulpitis, single-visit RCT): 0.5% bupivacaine 1:200,000 vs 2% lidocaine 1:80,000 IANB — lower pain at 6 and 12 h and fewer analgesics with bupivacaine (P < .05). Parirokh M et al. J Endod 2012;38(8):1035-9. PMID 22794202 **[A]** — [Parirokh 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2012.04.012)
- RCT (n = 40): same direction at 6 and 12 h. Al-Kahtani A. Saudi Pharm J 2014;22(1):39-42. PMID 24493972 **[A]** — [Al-Kahtani 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsps.2013.01.004). RCT (n = 30, anterior maxilla): bupivacaine reduced post-op pain vs lidocaine. Moradi S, Naghavi N. Iran Endod J 2010;5(1):31-5. PMID 24778680 **[A]** — [Moradi 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24778680/)
- Meta-analysis (16 RCTs, dental): 0.5% bupivacaine vs 2% lidocaine — higher success in inflamed pulp (P = .03) but lower in vital pulp, fewer patients using post-op analgesics (P < .00001), slower onset, longer duration. Su N et al. Int Dent J 2014;64(1):34-45. PMID 24117122 **[A]** — [Su 2014](https://doi.org/10.1111/idj.12060)
- Liposomal bupivacaine no better than plain bupivacaine (RCT, n = 100, symptomatic necrosis): success 29% vs 22% (P = .47). Glenn B et al. J Endod 2016;42(9):1301-6. PMID 27461787 **[A]** — [Glenn 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2016.05.018)

**Preoperative counselling / information**
- RCT (n = 437, analogue design; five information paragraphs): subjects given positive, accurate information about pain during endodontic treatment reported less fear of pain associated with it; authors: "patients should be accurately informed about pain associated with ET." van Wijk AJ, Hoogstraten J. Int Endod J 2006;39(5):384-8. PMID 16640637 **[A]** — [van Wijk & Hoogstraten 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2591.2006.01090.x)
- RCT (n = 60 endodontic patients): video information reduced anxiety pre→post in the intervention group but between-group differences in anxiety scores and galvanic skin response were not significant. Anatürk Ş et al. J Oral Rehabil 2025;52(11):1933-1944. PMID 40506422 **[A]** — [Anatürk 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/joor.14054)
- RCT (RCT/retreatment patients): informative preoperative video lowered post-op VAS pain (P = .003) but music, not video, reduced physiological anxiety measures. Abdulhameed SA et al. Quintessence Int 2024;55(8):670-680. PMID 39078172 **[A]** — [Abdulhameed 2024](https://doi.org/10.3290/j.qi.b5640066)
- RCT of paper information leaflets (general dental patients, 82 vs 85): no significant effect on pre-procedure anxiety or post-procedure well-being; "verbal communication alone may be sufficient". Plachý R et al. J Int Soc Prev Community Dent 2025;15(1):69-79. PMID 40151554 **[A]** — [Plachý 2025](https://doi.org/10.4103/jispcd.jispcd_171_24)

### Inferences
- A practical "expectation script" is supported by the numbers above: most people have some soreness for 1–2 days (≈40% at 24 h, mean ≈24/100), ≈1 in 10 at a week, usually controlled by ibuprofen ± acetaminophen; severe or rising pain is not expected and should prompt a call. Framing with accurate positive information (van Wijk 2006) and fostering realistic optimism (Nixdorf 2016; Wu 2022) is low-cost and plausibly beneficial, but the causal evidence in real endodontic patients is thin.
- Using bupivacaine for the block (or as a closing injection) in a patient with high pre-op pain is a reasonable anxiety-mitigation tactic because the first 6–12 h are when pain peaks; trade-offs are slower onset and prolonged numbness (which some injection-fearful patients specifically dislike — Armfield & Milgrom 2011 studied this but results were not retrieved).
- The ADA 2024 guideline is being applied by extension to post-endodontic pain; its formal scope is extractions and temporary toothache management.

### Gaps
- No RCT was found testing structured pre-operative pain counselling (verbal, clinician-delivered) against usual care with dental-anxiety or post-op pain as outcomes in endodontic patients.
- No study linked post-operative pain control quality to *subsequent* dental anxiety or attendance in endodontic patients.
- Patient-reported experience (satisfaction, anxiety) was not an outcome in the bupivacaine endodontic RCTs.
- Whether there is an AAE-specific post-operative analgesic statement newer than the 2015 newsletter was not established.

## 4. Root canal treatment is less painful than expected; positive experience and the (fragile) extinction of fear

### Takeaway
Across endodontic cohorts, patients — especially anxious, younger and female patients — predict more pain than they then report (mean intra-operative pain ≈1–2/10; half or more feel none), and people who have had a root canal are far more likely to call it painless. But memory of dental pain drifts back toward what was expected, most strongly in anxious patients, so a single good experience does not durably disconfirm fear; repeated, closely spaced, pain-free and low-anxiety visits (and explicit post-visit reinforcement) are what conditioning data suggest is needed.

### Cited Findings

**Expected vs experienced pain in endodontics**
- JADA cohort of endodontic patients: "Pain experienced during ET often is less than anticipated"; younger people anticipate and experience more; women anticipate but do not necessarily experience more; "patients perceive each ET experience as new, implying that dentists should manage patients who have had ET in the past as carefully as those receiving ET for the first time." Watkins CA, Logan HL, Kirchner HL. J Am Dent Assoc 2002;133(1):45-54. PMID 11811742 **[A]** — [Watkins 2002](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.2002.0020)
- Seville cohort (n = 176): mean pain during RCT 1.2 ± 0.8 on 0–10 VAS; 54% felt no pain; more pain in mandibular and posterior teeth, irreversible pulpitis/acute apical periodontitis, and appointments ≥45 min. Segura-Egea JJ et al. Int Endod J 2009;42(7):614-20. PMID 19467050 **[A]** — [Segura-Egea 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2591.2009.01562.x)
- Croatian endodontic patients: expected pain exceeded actual pain (t = 3.540, P = .001); anxiety correlated with expected pain (rho = 0.401) but not with actual pain (rho = 0.080, P = .524); authors propose breaking the "vicious cycle" with positive information before and during treatment. Perković I et al. Acta Stomatol Croat 2014;48(4):258-67. PMID 27688374 **[A]** — [Perković 2014](https://doi.org/10.15644/asc47/4/3)
- AAE surveys (not peer-reviewed): Feb 2015 online survey — root canal treatment is the procedure Americans are most apprehensive about (56%; women 62% vs men 48%); 2008 consumer survey — "patients who have experienced root canal treatment are six times more likely to describe it as 'painless' than patients who have not." **[FT]** — [AAE Winter 2015](https://www.aae.org/specialty/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2017/07/ecfeacutedentalpain.pdf)
- 2026 Iowa cohort: 65% rated mandibular-molar RCT "better than expected"; 73% no procedural pain. Kopec E et al. Aust Endod J 2026. PMID 42458676 **[A]** — [Kopec 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/aej.70107)
- Personal experience reduces fear of specific dental pains — except in the highly anxious: fear-of-dental-pain items were rated lower by those who had experienced the pain personally in periodontal patients (n = 56) and students (n = 262) but not in highly anxious dental patients (n = 48). van Wijk AJ, Hoogstraten J. J Dent Res 2005;84(10):947-50. PMID 16183796 **[A]** — [van Wijk & Hoogstraten 2005](https://doi.org/10.1177/154405910508401014)
- Narrative review of patient-centred endodontic outcomes: fear of pain before RCT is "fair" to "very much"; pulpal disease generally produces moderate not severe pain; RCT produces a dramatic fall over the following week. Hamedy R et al. Iran Endod J 2013;8(4):197-204. PMID 24171029 **[A]** — [Hamedy 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24171029/)

**Memory reconstruction and maintenance of fear**
- Kent 1985: remembered pain 3 months after a dental visit was more closely associated with *expected* than with *experienced* pain, "particularly for those patients who scored high on the Dental Anxiety Scale… dental anxiety may be slow to extinguish because the discrepancy between expected and experienced pain felt at one appointment may not be recalled accurately by anxious patients at their next appointment." Kent G. Pain 1985;21(2):187-194. PMID 3982842 **[A]** — [Kent 1985](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-3959(85)90288-X)
- Kent & Warren 1985: DAS scores were stable over 3 months, but some change was associated with the expected–experienced pain discrepancy. J Dent Res 1985;64(11):1316-8. PMID 3867692 **[A]** — [Kent & Warren 1985](https://doi.org/10.1177/00220345850640111301)
- Arntz 1990 (n = 40, two extensive treatments): anxious subjects expected more pain and anxiety than they experienced and "appeared to need more experiences before their predictions became accurate. In the course of time, the expectations (and memories) of anxious subjects returned to their original more inaccurate level… the old schema is ultimately reinstated if disconfirmations are few and far between. Anxious subjects did not experience more pain, but they did experience more anxiety"; anxiety experienced during treatment maintained inaccurate expectations. Arntz A, van Eck M, Heijmans M. Behav Res Ther 1990;28(1):29-41. PMID 2302147 **[A]** — [Arntz 1990](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-7967(90)90052-k)
- McNeil 2011 (n = 79 emergency extraction patients; ratings before, during and 2 weeks after): "All patients exaggerated their recall of procedure pain, but only those high in trait dental anxiety exaggerated their recall of anxiety"; highly anxious patients expected more pain; pain and anxiety ratings assimilated over time. McNeil DW et al. J Dent Res 2011;90(2):220-4. PMID 21149859 **[A]** — [McNeil 2011](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022034510385689)
- Kyle 2016 (oral-surgery cohort): patients "reported less pain during extraction than they predicted or recalled"; recalled pain was driven by pain reported during extraction (β = .53) and recalled state anxiety (β = .52). Kyle BN et al. J Dent Res 2016;95(6):629-34. PMID 26908629 **[A]** — [Kyle 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022034516631977)
- Amnestic sedation alters the memory: patients given moderate sedation + LA (n = 27) recalled less procedural pain and anxiety at 1 month than LA alone (n = 27). Wilson TD et al. Oral Surg Oral Med Oral Pathol Oral Radiol 2014;117(3):277-82. PMID 24528789 **[A]** — [Wilson 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.oooo.2013.11.489)

**Latent inhibition and remission of fear**
- Davey 1989: people who never developed dental anxiety were less likely to have had a painful treatment; those who had a painful experience without acquiring anxiety "reported a history of dental treatment favourable to the operation of latent inhibition" (many uneventful visits first); a *very* painful experience could override latent inhibition; non-remitters reported "significantly more painful and traumatic dental experiences" than remitters. Davey GC. Behav Res Ther 1989;27(1):51-8. PMID 2914005 **[A]** — [Davey 1989](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-7967(89)90119-8)
- Liddell & Locker 2000: former avoiders whose fear had remitted had experienced less invasive and more non-invasive treatment, feared pain less, and reported fewer frightening/embarrassing experiences than still-anxious avoiders. Behav Modif 2000;24(1):57-68. PMID 10641367 **[A]** — [Liddell & Locker 2000](https://doi.org/10.1177/0145445500241003)
- Conceptual model: person-level dispositional and situational factors, more than objective stimulus intensity, determine distress in dental procedures. Litt MD. Behav Res Ther 1996;34(5-6):459-76. PMID 8687368 **[A]** — [Litt 1996](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-7967(96)00015-0)

### Inferences
- The clinical corollary of Kent/Arntz/McNeil is that the *end-of-visit debrief* matters: asking the patient to rate the pain they actually felt against what they predicted, saying it aloud, and (optionally) writing it on the recall card gives the anxious patient a retrievable disconfirmation before memory reconstructs it. This is a theory-driven suggestion; no trial of such a debrief was found.
- Since anxious patients do not necessarily feel more pain but do feel more anxiety (Arntz 1990; Perković 2014), pain-free treatment is necessary but not sufficient — in-chair anxiety must also be low for the visit to count as corrective. Conversely, Murillo-Benítez 2020 did find more pain in anxious endodontic patients; the two findings conflict and probably reflect setting (hot teeth vs mixed general treatment).
- Multi-visit endodontic treatment can be reframed as an asset for the fearful patient: several closely spaced, predictable, pain-free visits approximate graded exposure, provided the first (often emergency) visit is not traumatic.
- A single very painful event can undo years of benign experience (Davey 1989), which argues for a low threshold to stop, re-anesthetize or defer in a hot tooth rather than "push through".

### Gaps
- No prospective endodontic study measures dental-anxiety change over successive root-canal visits or tests whether confirmed pain-free treatment lowers MDAS at follow-up.
- Kent 1985, Arntz 1990 and Davey 1989 are small, old, and from general dentistry/self-report retrospection; sample sizes for Kent 1985 and Watkins 2002 were not captured from abstracts.
- AAE survey figures are from association consumer surveys with undisclosed methodology.

## 5. Stepwise, severity-stratified frameworks for managing the anxious dental patient

### Takeaway
The published frameworks converge on the same architecture: (i) screen every adult (MDAS; ≥19 = probable phobia) and ask what specifically is feared; (ii) give everyone "universal" low-level measures — rapport, information, control/stop signal, tell-show-do, attention to physiological arousal, positive feedback; (iii) add adjuncts for moderate anxiety (longer/staged appointments, relaxation, distraction, anxiolysis); (iv) reserve CBT referral and conscious sedation/GA for severe or phobic patients, with sedation justified by an explicit need assessment (IOSN: anxiety + medical/behavioural factors + treatment complexity) and never substituting for adequate local anesthesia. Trauma-informed care adds that the base layer should be applied universally because trauma history is common and usually undisclosed.

### Cited Findings

**Newton et al. 2012 — "proportionate" management**
- Premise: "anxiety manifests at different levels and that consequently management of dental anxiety involves both assessment and proportionate intervention"; methods for assessment and management are outlined. Newton T, Asimakopoulou K, Daly B, Scambler S, Scott S. Br Dent J 2012;213(6):271-4. PMID 22996472 **[A]** — [Newton 2012](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.bdj.2012.830)
- Publisher key points and search-engine extracts **[S]**: MDAS described as a five-item scale that is reliable, quick and acceptable to patients and dental teams; simple techniques for low anxiety; uncertainty is anxiety-provoking and is reduced by preparatory information and enhancing sense of control; preparatory information suggested for moderate anxiety; separate strategies for high anxiety/phobia; Figure 1 includes assessment of anxiety level, presence of urgent treatment need, CBT, relative analgesia, conscious sedation and general anaesthesia; IOSN referenced for when sedation is appropriate. — [Newton 2012](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.bdj.2012.830)

**NHS England "Clinical standards for dental anxiety management" (operationalises the proportionate model)**
- Read via a text-extraction proxy of the NHS England page; wording should be re-checked against the original **[FT via extract]**: scope is anxiety management in community and primary care (behavioural therapies, conscious sedation, GA referral); behavioural techniques and sedation are complementary; MDAS 5–25 with bands reported as mild 5–10, moderate 10–18, and "scores of >19 or more are considered to be severely anxious/phobic"; clinicians should also ask about specific triggers (environment, examination, X-rays, injections, drill, scaling, extraction), treatment urgency and ASA status; IOSN has three elements (MDAS, medical and behavioural indicators, treatment complexity). **Low-level techniques for all patients**: rapport building, environmental change, enhancing control, managing physiological arousal, retrospective control, feedback on positive coping (minimum training = GDC registration). **Advanced techniques** for moderate/phobic anxiety, highly invasive treatment or co-morbidity: letter to the dentist, treatment staging, longer appointments, CBT (requires post-registration training and supervised practice). Principle: "All patients should be managed with the simplest and safest anxiety management technique"; CBT typically 6–10 one-hour sessions; GA only when behavioural and sedation techniques are insufficient. — [NHS England clinical standards](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/clinical-guide-for-dental-anxiety-management/)
- NHS England "Clinical guide for dentistry" v2 (28 Oct 2022): services "should integrate the use of behavioural management techniques including Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) with conscious sedation and, if necessary, General Anaesthesia." **[FT]** — [NHS England Clinical guide for dentistry](https://www.england.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/B1639-cinical-guide-to-dentistry-september-2023.pdf)

**IOSN — Indicator of Sedation Need**
- Tool rationale: sedation need can be estimated "by assessing and ranking a combination of information on patient anxiety, medical history and the complexity of the anticipated clinical treatment", as a decision aid and referral/commissioning tool. Coulthard P et al. Br Dent J 2011;211(5):E10. PMID 21904334 **[A]** — [Coulthard 2011](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.bdj.2011.725)
- Application (n = 607 dental attenders, NW England): three domains — treatment complexity, medical and behavioural indicators, MDAS; about 5% of attenders likely to need a course of treatment under sedation at some time; females 3.8 times more likely to be in the high-need group; age, deprivation and practice location not associated. Pretty IA et al. Br Dent J 2011;211(5):E11. PMID 21904335 **[A]** — [Pretty 2011](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.bdj.2011.726)

**Armfield & Heaton 2013 (Aust Dent J)**
- Review of non-pharmacological techniques for the dental clinic: about "one in seven" of the population is highly dentally anxious; stresses "firstly identifying dental fear and then understanding its aetiology, nature and associated components"; techniques "range from good communication and establishing rapport to the use of systematic desensitization and hypnosis"; "many… could usefully be adopted for all dental patients, regardless of their known level of dental anxiety"; successful management "requires a greater level of understanding, good communication and a phased treatment approach"; "acceptable evidence base for several non-pharmacological anxiety management practices". Armfield JM, Heaton LJ. Aust Dent J 2013;58(4):390-407. PMID 24320894 **[A]** — [Armfield & Heaton 2013](https://doi.org/10.1111/adj.12118)

**Appukuttan 2016 (Clin Cosmet Investig Dent) — full text read**
- Identification: observe behaviour, then use a questionnaire; CDAS (range 4–20; >15 = high/possibly phobic; lacks an injection item) and MDAS (5 items, 5–25; "a cutoff point for high dental fear has been suggested at a score of 19"); patients can be "categorized as mildly anxious, moderately anxious, and extremely anxious or dental-phobic"; only ~20% of UK dentists reported using questionnaires. **[FT]** — [Appukuttan 2016](https://doi.org/10.2147/CCIDE.S63626)
- Stratified rule: "Mildly and moderately anxious patients can be frequently managed using psychological interventions, and occasionally anxiolytic drugs or conscious sedation may be necessary. Extremely anxious or phobic patients most frequently require combined management approaches." Sedation ladder: minimal sedation (oral sedative and/or nitrous oxide) for mild-to-moderate anxiety; moderate/conscious sedation for moderate-to-severe; GA for phobic patients and those with severe disability/comorbidity in special care. **[FT]** — [Appukuttan 2016](https://doi.org/10.2147/CCIDE.S63626)
- Communication base ("iatrosedative technique"): introduce yourself, converse before treatment, listen calmly and non-judgementally, ask about fears, give honest answers, "avoid false reassurances", normalise anxiety, avoid negative phrasing, keep checking for discomfort. Behavioural control: agree a stop signal (e.g., raised hand) before starting. Techniques listed: relaxation breathing, progressive muscle relaxation, guided imagery, biofeedback, hypnosis, acupuncture, distraction, positive reinforcement, stop-signalling, tell-show-do, modelling, systematic desensitisation, cognitive restructuring/CBT. **[FT]** — [Appukuttan 2016](https://doi.org/10.2147/CCIDE.S63626)
- The review also notes a strained, anxiety-dominated dentist–patient relationship has been linked to misdiagnosis during vitality testing for endodontic therapy (citing Eli). **[FT]** — [Appukuttan 2016](https://doi.org/10.2147/CCIDE.S63626)

**Milgrom, Weinstein & Heaton — the "Seattle system" (aetiology-based typing)**
- Four diagnostic groups by source of fear, developed at the University of Washington: (1) fear of specific dental stimuli, (2) distrust of dental personnel, (3) generalized anxiety, (4) fear of catastrophe (e.g., medical emergency). As summarised in Appukuttan 2016 **[FT of secondary source]** — [Appukuttan 2016](https://doi.org/10.2147/CCIDE.S63626). The injection-fear typology from the same group (pain, bodily injury, acquired disease, anesthetic side effects/inadequacy) is in [Milgrom 1997](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.1997.0301) **[A]**.

**Evidence syntheses underpinning the tiers**
- Systematic review of management strategies for adult dental anxiety in the clinic: psychotherapeutic behavioural strategies (relaxation, guided imagery, biofeedback, hypnosis, acupuncture, distraction, desensitisation) are minimally invasive with negligible side effects; pharmacological options (nitrous oxide, IV or oral sedation) carry side effects, contraindications and cost. Hoffmann B et al. Aust Dent J 2022;67 Suppl 1:S3-S13. PMID 35735746 **[A]** — [Hoffmann 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/adj.12926)
- Systematic review (10 RCTs, adults with dental anxiety/phobia): CBT/behaviour therapy reduced dental anxiety (mean difference −2.7 on DAS; low-quality evidence) and improved acceptance of dental treatment more than GA. Wide Boman U et al. Eur J Oral Sci 2013;121(3 Pt 2):225-34. PMID 23659254 **[A]** — [Wide Boman 2013](https://doi.org/10.1111/eos.12032)
- Endodontic-specific older view (for contrast): "Mild anxiety can be managed with oral sedatives and/or nitrous oxide; however, moderate to severe anxiety and phobia is best treated with deep sedation or general anesthesia." Saxen MA, Newton CW. J Indiana Dent Assoc 1999;78(4):21-3. PMID 10863457 **[A]** — [Saxen & Newton 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10863457/)

**Communication models**
- Four Habits Model (Kaiser Permanente: invest in the beginning; elicit the patient's perspective; demonstrate empathy; invest in the end): used as a unifying clinician–patient communication framework for 16 years with "sustained improvement in patient satisfaction scores". Stein T, Frankel RM, Krupat E. Patient Educ Couns 2005;58(1):4-12. PMID 15950831 **[A]** — [Stein 2005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pec.2005.01.014)
- Dental adaptation — "Four + One Habits Model for Dental Visits" (Torper et al., Eur J Dent Educ 2019) adds a fifth element, *facilitating perceived control*, because dental visits alternate dialogue and clinical phases; authors say validation research is still needed **[S]** — [Springer chapter "Communication in Dentistry: The Four Habits Model" (2022)](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/978-3-031-04248-5_6)
- Ask-tell-ask, "tell me more", and responding empathically are named as fundamental skills; pitfalls include blocking, lecturing and "premature reassurance". Back AL et al. CA Cancer J Clin 2005;55(3):164-77. PMID 15890639 **[A]** — [Back 2005](https://doi.org/10.3322/canjclin.55.3.164)

**Trauma-informed care (TIC)**
- Raja 2014 TIC pyramid (JADA), base to apex: (1) patient-centred communication skills; (2) understanding the health effects of trauma; (3) interprofessional collaboration and understanding one's professional role; (4) understanding one's own history; (5) screening. Prevalence cited: 22% of women and 4% of men sexually assaulted as adults; 17% of women and 8% of men sexually abused as children. "Universal trauma precautions" (no screening or disclosure required): ask what would make the patient more comfortable; ask what helped in past stressful situations; overview the whole appointment; tell-show-do throughout; agree a hand-raise stop signal "if it is medically safe"; warn before sensations (e.g., suction, percussion test demonstrated on the hand); offer blanket/pillow or adjusted position for those uneasy supine; build treatment gradually on systematic-desensitisation principles; "reassurance is not the same as assessment of anxiety". Routine trauma screening by dentists is *not* recommended (except assessment for abuse with acute orofacial injuries). Triggers named: supine position, inability to talk, being touched, things in the mouth. Raja S, Hoersch M, Rajagopalan CF, Chang P. J Am Dent Assoc 2014;145(3):238-45. PMID 24583888 **[FT]** — [Raja 2014](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.2013.30)
- Childhood sexual abuse (CSA): women with CSA vs matched controls (85 vs 170) — major psychological strain during dental treatment 36.5% vs 18.8%; fewer preventive visits (72.9% vs 89.4%); preference for a female dentist 29.4% vs 8.2%; 28% had memories of the abuse during dental treatment. Leeners B et al. J Psychosom Res 2007;62(5):581-8. PMID 17467413 **[A]** — [Leeners 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychores.2006.11.009)
- Women with dental fear + CSA (n = 58) vs dental fear alone (n = 25): similar Dental Fear Scale scores but higher Dental Belief Scale scores — communication, trust, fear of negative information and lack of control are the more fear-evoking elements. Willumsen T. Community Dent Oral Epidemiol 2004;32(1):73-9. PMID 14961843 **[A]** — [Willumsen 2004](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0528.2004.00120.x)
- Qualitative study (16 adult CSA survivors): what makes treatment possible is "Being considerate every step of the way" — offering a good start, being competent, awareness of staff behaviour, building a safe relationship, arranging a secure treatment situation, exploring individual triggers. Kranstad V et al. Eur J Oral Sci 2019;127(6):539-546. PMID 31731327 **[A]** — [Kranstad 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/eos.12661)
- Tromsø 7 population study: painful or frightening dental treatment showed the largest difference between high- and low-anxiety adults; among non-dental traumas only sexual abuse independently predicted dental anxiety after adjustment for mental-health symptoms. Nermo H et al. BMC Oral Health 2021;21(1):600. PMID 34814891 **[A]** — [Nermo 2021](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-021-01968-4)
- "Posttraumatic dental-care anxiety": review arguing most "dental phobia" stems from aversive dental experiences and resembles PTSD-spectrum conditioning; cites that painful treatment plus perceived lack of control conferred ~14-fold odds of higher dental fear. Bracha HS, Vega EM, Vega CB. Hawaii Dent J 2006;37(5):17-9. PMID 17152624 **[A]** — [Bracha 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17152624/)

### Inferences
**Synthesised chairside protocol for an endodontic resident (my integration of the above; not a published algorithm):**
1. *Screen (≤2 min, every new patient):* MDAS + three questions — "Have you ever had trouble getting numb?", "Is it the injection, the numbness, pain, or losing control that worries you most?" (maps to Seattle/Milgrom types), "What has helped before?". Flag MDAS ≥19, injection item 4–5, or previous anesthetic failure.
2. *Tier 0 — everyone (universal/trauma-informed precautions):* sit eye-level and invest in the beginning; ask-tell-ask the plan; overview of the visit; agreed stop signal (and honour it); tell-show-do for cold test, clamp, dam; explicit statement that you will not start until the tooth tests numb (Corah 1988: the dentist's "explicit dedication to prevent pain" is the top anxiety-reducing behaviour — Section 8); injection-comfort bundle (Section 1); test anesthesia before access.
3. *Tier 1 — mild (MDAS ≈5–10):* Tier 0 only; accurate expectation-setting for intra- and post-operative pain.
4. *Tier 2 — moderate (MDAS ≈11–18) or situational risk (hot mandibular molar, emergency, prior anesthetic failure, high catastrophizing):* add longer/staged appointments, scheduled breaks, paced breathing/relaxation or distraction of the patient's choice, NSAID premedication and planned supplemental anesthesia, bupivacaine for the tail; consider nitrous oxide/oral anxiolysis per local sedation rules (other researchers cover agents).
5. *Tier 3 — severe/phobic (MDAS ≥19) or failed Tier 2:* if urgent, stabilise (pulpotomy/pulpectomy under best achievable anesthesia ± sedation) and defer definitive care; document IOSN-type justification before sedation; offer/refer for CBT with the goal of treatment under LA; GA only when behavioural and sedation routes are insufficient.
6. *Close the loop every visit:* predicted-vs-actual pain debrief; positive, specific feedback on coping ("retrospective control"); written post-op plan; next visit soon.
- The UK (NHS England/SDCEP) position is "simplest and safest effective technique first, but choose the right one at the outset rather than escalate through failures" — this is compatible with, but not identical to, a strict stepped-care ladder.
- Seattle typing changes the *content* of Tier 0: a "distrust" patient needs control and information; a "catastrophe" patient (fear of fainting, allergy, choking) needs medical reassurance and physiological monitoring/positioning; a "specific stimulus" patient (needle) needs the injection bundle and graded exposure; a "generalised anxiety" patient is the likeliest to need Tier 2–3.

### Gaps
- Newton 2012 full text (including Figure 1 and exact assignment of techniques to low/moderate/high levels and any MDAS bands) could not be accessed; details above marked [S] come from publisher key points and search extracts. From background knowledge (unverified this session): the paper pairs low anxiety with rapport/control techniques, moderate anxiety with additional relaxation/distraction/information, and high anxiety with CBT ± sedation.
- IOSN scoring rules and thresholds could not be verified from the primary paper (paywalled). From background knowledge (unverified): each of three domains is ranked 1–4, total 3–12, with 3–4 minimal, 5–6 moderate, 7–9 high and 10–12 very high need; a search-engine summary of an NHS document instead stated "over 7 (out of 15)". These conflict — check the original before use.
- Armfield & Heaton 2013 full text was blocked (HTTP 403); the technique-by-technique evidence table and injection-specific advice could not be extracted.
- Milgrom, Weinstein & Heaton, *Treating Fearful Dental Patients* (book) was not directly consulted; the Seattle typology is cited second-hand. Whether the typology predicts response to specific management in adults was not established.
- The NHS England standard was read through a third-party text extractor and summarised by a model; the reported MDAS bands overlap at 10 and leave 18–19 ambiguous. Publication date not captured (search results suggest 2022–2023).
- No dental/endodontic outcome trial of the Four Habits or ask-tell-ask models was found; they are transferred from medicine. Frankel & Stein's original Permanente Journal paper (1999) is not PubMed-indexed and was not retrieved.
- No study of trauma-informed protocols with dental-anxiety or treatment-completion outcomes (i.e., effectiveness of TIC) was found; evidence is prevalence, qualitative, and expert framework.

## 6. Guidelines and position statements; how they triage by severity

### Takeaway
There is no US (ADA or AAE) clinical practice guideline dedicated to adult dental-anxiety management; authoritative severity-based guidance comes mainly from the UK (SDCEP Conscious Sedation in Dentistry 3rd ed. 2017; NHS England clinical standards; IACSD standards referenced by both) and a German S3 guideline (AWMF 083-020, 2019). All require assessment, non-pharmacological management as alternative or adjunct, the simplest/safest effective technique, documented justification for sedation, and CBT as the definitive treatment for phobia-level anxiety. Pain-side guidance comes from the ADA 2024 acute dental pain guideline and AAE educational newsletters.

### Cited Findings
- **SDCEP, Conscious Sedation in Dentistry, 3rd edition (June 2017)** **[FT]** (a 2022 surveillance review reportedly left it unchanged **[S]**): "The provision of adequate anxiety control is an integral part of the practice of dentistry… both a right for the patient and a duty placed on the dentist." "A 'one size fits all' approach is inappropriate. When treatment planning for individual patients, practitioners should consider the use of the range of non-pharmacological methods of behavioural management as an alternative to sedation or as an adjunct." Assessment: "Take a thorough medical, dental, social, anxiety and sedation history"; "Explore with the patient all relevant anxiety management techniques including non-pharmacological behaviour management techniques (e.g. cognitive behavioural therapy, distraction, guided imagery, hypnosis, play therapy) and, where necessary, general anaesthesia." "Adopting the principle of minimal intervention, the simplest and safest effective technique, based on patient assessment and clinical need, should be used." "It is preferable to use a carefully chosen technique for the patient from the outset, rather than progress through a range of techniques likely to fail." "Document the justifications for conscious sedation and for the chosen technique." Records should include "Assessment of anxiety and any tools used". "Indications for conscious sedation as a potential adjunct for patient management include patients with: dental anxiety and phobia; a need for prolonged or traumatic dental procedures; medical conditions potentially aggravated by stress; medical or behavioural conditions affecting the patient's ability to cooperate; special care requirements." — [SDCEP 2017](https://www.sdcep.org.uk/media/iegenn4c/sdcep-conscious-sedation-guidance.pdf); status page — [SDCEP guidance page](https://www.sdcep.org.uk/published-guidance/sedation)
- **NHS England, Clinical standards for dental anxiety management** **[FT via extract]**: MDAS-based complexity assessment; low-level techniques for all patients; advanced techniques (staging, longer appointments, CBT) for moderate/phobic anxiety or invasive treatment; "simplest and safest anxiety management technique"; multiple-drug sedation only if simpler options failed or are justified; referrals for sedation through a referral management system; GA when behavioural and sedation techniques are insufficient; CBT 6–10 sessions, deliverable by psychological professionals or specifically trained dental professionals; not recommended where mental-health comorbidity dominates (refer to specialist services). — [NHS England clinical standards](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/clinical-guide-for-dental-anxiety-management/)
- **German S3 guideline "Zahnbehandlungsangst beim Erwachsenen" (AWMF 083-020; DGZMK/AKPP; Oct 2019, listed valid to Oct 2024)** **[S – trade-press summaries; PDF not read]**: CBT is first-line for dental anxiety that reaches disorder threshold, with the most durable effect; hypnosis not recommended for severe dental anxiety because of lacking evidence; pharmacotherapy/sedation are further options; about 5–10% of the population estimated to have dental anxiety of disorder severity. — [AWMF 083-020 PDF](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/083-020l_S3_Zahnbehandlungsangst-beim-Erwachsenen_2019-11.pdf); [zm-online summary](https://www.zm-online.de/archiv/2020/13/zahnmedizin/zahnbehandlungsangst-bei-erwachsenen/)
- **ADA 2024 acute dental pain guideline (adolescents/adults/older adults)** **[FT]**: non-opioids first-line; NSAID alone or with acetaminophen; avoid "just-in-case" opioid prescribing; short-acting LA for immediate toothache relief; bupivacaine or articaine as extended bridge; applies only where definitive treatment is not immediately available and is "not a substitute for or a reason to delay the immediate provision of dental treatment". — [ADA 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.adaj.2023.10.009)
- **AAE educational guidance** **[FT]**: Winter 2009 Colleagues for Excellence (IANB misconceptions; test before starting; ask about previous anesthetic difficulty; supplemental articaine infiltration, intraosseous, intraligamentary, intrapulpal) — [AAE Winter 2009](https://www.aae.org/specialty/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2017/07/winter09ecfe.pdf); Winter 2015 "3D" approach (Diagnose, Dental treatment, Drugs), which opens by noting that difficulty obtaining pain control is "a source of frustration to the busy practitioner, and perhaps even more so for the anxious patient" — [AAE Winter 2015](https://www.aae.org/specialty/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2017/07/ecfeacutedentalpain.pdf)
- **Evidence reviews that function as de facto guidance for triage:** proportionate model — [Newton 2012](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.bdj.2012.830) **[A]**; clinic techniques — [Armfield & Heaton 2013](https://doi.org/10.1111/adj.12118) **[A]**; mild/moderate → psychological ± occasional anxiolysis, severe/phobic → combined approaches — [Appukuttan 2016](https://doi.org/10.2147/CCIDE.S63626) **[FT]**; CBT evidence — [Wide Boman 2013](https://doi.org/10.1111/eos.12032) **[A]**.

**Triage summary table (compiled from the sources above)**

| Severity (typical MDAS) | UK standards (SDCEP / NHS England) | Reviews (Newton; Armfield & Heaton; Appukuttan) | Pain-control overlay (AAE / ADA / endodontic evidence) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mild (≈5–10) | Low-level techniques for all: rapport, environment, control, arousal management, positive feedback | Communication, information, stop signal, tell-show-do | Painless-injection bundle; test anesthesia before access; expectation-setting |
| Moderate (≈10/11–18) | Longer/staged appointments so low-level techniques can be used; consider adjuncts | Relaxation, distraction, preparatory information; occasional anxiolysis/minimal sedation | NSAID premedication; planned supplemental anesthesia; bupivacaine; NSAID ± acetaminophen post-op |
| Severe / phobic (≥19) | CBT (trained provider); conscious sedation if justified and documented; GA only if behavioural + sedation insufficient | CBT/exposure-based therapy; combined psychological + pharmacological management | As above — sedation does not replace profound local anesthesia (Lindemann 2008) |

### Inferences
- Across jurisdictions the triage logic is "assess → universal behavioural base → adjuncts → CBT/sedation → GA", with the UK documents adding a governance layer (documented justification, trained teams, referral pathways). A US resident can adopt the logic while following ADA sedation rules and state regulations for the pharmacological tiers.
- The absence of an ADA/AAE anxiety-management guideline means the resident's "evidence-based protocol" will necessarily be a synthesis of UK standards, review articles, and endodontic anesthesia/analgesia evidence rather than a single citable US algorithm.

### Gaps
- **NICE:** no NICE guideline on adult dental anxiety was identified in this search (NICE sedation guidance concerns under-19s); this absence was not exhaustively confirmed on the NICE website.
- **IACSD (Intercollegiate Advisory Committee for Sedation in Dentistry, Royal Colleges) standards:** referenced by SDCEP and NHS England but not read directly; edition/date and anxiety-assessment wording unverified.
- **ADA:** the ADA "Guidelines for the Use of Sedation and General Anesthesia by Dentists" (2016) and any ADA policy text on anxiety were not retrieved; I found no ADA clinical practice guideline on anxiety management.
- **AAE:** no AAE position statement specifically on managing the anxious patient or on anesthesia in SIP was found; whether newer Colleagues for Excellence issues (post-2015) update the anesthesia guidance was not checked.
- **German S3 guideline:** specific sedation recommendations (e.g., midazolam) and grades were not confirmed; validity lapsed Oct 2024 and update status is unknown.
- **Sweden/Norway:** national programmes (e.g., Swedish national dental guidelines; Norwegian TOO service for torture/abuse survivors and odontophobia) are relevant but were not searched or verified.
- No international consensus statement (e.g., FDI, ESE) on adult dental anxiety management was located.

## 7. Special considerations in endodontics

### Takeaway
Endodontic patients concentrate the risk factors: emergency attenders in acute pain have very high anxiety prevalence and an ~8-fold higher block-failure risk; rubber dam is generally well accepted (but less so when placement is slow or use prolonged); longer appointments are associated with more intra-operative pain; catastrophizers and patients with chronic pain histories report more pain and are at higher risk of persistent post-treatment pain; and red-hair/MC1R-variant patients have more dental anxiety and avoidance but, in the only IANB trial, no lower block success.

### Cited Findings

**Emergency patients in acute pain**
- Adults with irreversible pulpitis: 83.1% moderate/high dental anxiety; 16.2% met specific-phobia criteria; 36.2% displayed moderate or severe anxiety at the endodontic visit by dentist's judgement. — [Dou 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-018-0563-x) **[A]**
- German emergency dental service (n = 286): 87.2% reported pain; toothache the main reason in 52.6%; mean Dental Fear Survey 47.6; of the 33.2% who attended irregularly, 58.4% were dentally fearful. Rauch A, Hahnel S, Schierz O. J Contemp Dent Pract 2019;20(1):3-7. PMID 31058610 **[A]** — [Rauch 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31058610/)
- Eight-fold higher IANB failure in pain patients vs controls; self-medication with ibuprofen can mask diagnostic test responses. — [AAE Winter 2015](https://www.aae.org/specialty/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2017/07/ecfeacutedentalpain.pdf) **[FT]**
- Expected pain relief predicts experienced and remembered relief and post-treatment anxiety in emergency SIP patients. — [Wu 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00784-021-03997-3) **[A]**
- ADA 2024: when definitive care must be deferred, short-acting LA for immediate relief, NSAID ± acetaminophen, bupivacaine/articaine as extended bridge. — [ADA 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.adaj.2023.10.009) **[FT]**

**Rubber dam: acceptance, claustrophobia, gagging**
- Questionnaire study (100 patients of dentists + 106 patients of students): the majority would prefer rubber dam at the next appointment and had a positive opinion; no association with age, sex, procedure, application time or duration for *preference*, but "Prolonged RD use showed some association with a negative opinion"; students took longer and their patients were less positive — "Operator experience improves patient compliance." Stewardson DA, McHugh ES. Int Endod J 2002;35(10):812-9. PMID 12406374 **[A]** — [Stewardson & McHugh 2002](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2591.2002.00571.x)
- Review: concerns over patient acceptance are among the "unfounded reasons" dentists cite for not using rubber dam. Ahmad IA. Int Endod J 2009;42(11):963-72. PMID 19825034 **[A]** — [Ahmad 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2591.2009.01623.x)
- Cochrane review of rubber dam in restorative treatment: none of the included trials reported "the level of patient acceptance/satisfaction". Wang Y et al. Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2016;9:CD009858. PMID 27648846 **[A]** — [Wang 2016](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD009858.pub2)
- Trauma-informed guidance names inability to talk, objects in the mouth, the supine position and fear of gagging as triggers, and recommends showing and letting the patient handle devices (e.g., a mouth prop) with a hand-raise removal signal. — [Raja 2014](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.2013.30) **[FT]**

**Appointment duration**
- Interventions shorter than 45 min had a significantly higher proportion of pain-free RCTs (P < .05); length of treatment was a risk factor for intra-operative pain. — [Segura-Egea 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2591.2009.01562.x) **[A]**
- NHS England standards list "treatment staging" and "longer appointments" (to allow behavioural techniques) as tools for moderate/phobic anxiety. — [NHS England clinical standards](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/clinical-guide-for-dental-anxiety-management/) **[FT via extract]**
- SDCEP lists "a need for prolonged or traumatic dental procedures" as an indication for considering conscious sedation as an adjunct, alongside dental anxiety/phobia. — [SDCEP 2017](https://www.sdcep.org.uk/media/iegenn4c/sdcep-conscious-sedation-guidance.pdf) **[FT]**

**Endodontic microsurgery**
- RCT in patients awaiting apical surgery: topical anesthesia reduced insertion and infiltration pain in the anterior maxilla; high-MDAS patients reported more injection pain, attenuated by topical. — [Cho 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2016.10.036) **[A]**
- Preliminary report (n = 20, periapical surgery of lower premolars/molars): less anesthetic was used in less anxious patients and with block technique, but differences were not statistically significant; more anesthetic was associated with better bony-crypt hemostasis; younger patients needed more reinforcement. Menéndez-Nieto I et al. J Clin Exp Dent 2020;12(6):e581-e587. PMID 32665818 **[A]** — [Menéndez-Nieto 2020](https://doi.org/10.4317/jced.56278)
- Risk of persistent pain was of borderline significance for surgical vs non-surgical endodontic treatment. — [Polycarpou 2005](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2591.2004.00923.x) **[A]**

**Pain catastrophizing and chronic pain**
- Review (JADA): catastrophizers ruminate, magnify and feel helpless; during dental treatment greater catastrophizing is associated with increased pain, dental anxiety and negative thoughts; management — "actively probe patients' pain experience, help them reappraise threat, manipulate their attention to pain and improve dentist-patient communication." Lin CS. J Am Dent Assoc 2013;144(11):1244-51. PMID 24177402 **[A]** — [Lin 2013 JADA](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.2013.0052)
- fMRI experiment (n = 15; electrical stimulation of an upper central incisor): pain and anxiety were higher when stimulus intensity was unpredictable; Pain Catastrophizing Scale score correlated with the unpredictability-related pain increase and with right posterior hippocampal activation. Lin CS et al. J Dent Res 2013;92(2):130-5. PMID 23232145 **[A]** — [Lin 2013 JDR](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022034512467804)
- RCT (n = 80 undergraduates, scaling/root planing): in controls, catastrophizers reported significantly more pain and distress than non-catastrophizers; after a brief pre-treatment emotional *disclosure* of dental worries the groups no longer differed (trait catastrophizing and dental anxiety unchanged). Sullivan MJ, Neish N. Pain 1999;79(2-3):155-63. PMID 10068161 **[A]** — [Sullivan & Neish 1999](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0304-3959(98)00163-8)
- Structural-equation study (n = 428): sensory sensitivity and pain catastrophizing independently associated with anticipatory and treatment-related dental anxiety and mediated the effect of alexithymia. Ogawa M et al. Eur J Oral Sci 2024;132(2):e12973. PMID 38311805 **[A]** — [Ogawa 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/eos.12973)
- Persistent pain after successful endodontic treatment (n = 175; prevalence 12%): risk factors — preoperative pain duration ≥3 months (OR 8.6), preoperative pain from the tooth (OR 7.8), tenderness to percussion (OR 7.8), previous chronic pain problems (OR 4.5), female gender (OR 4.5), history of painful treatment in the orofacial region (OR 3.8). Polycarpou N et al. Int Endod J 2005;38(3):169-78. PMID 15743420 **[A]** — [Polycarpou 2005](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2591.2004.00923.x)
- PBRN cohort: 10% pain at 6 months; pre-op pain duration increased and optimism decreased risk; age and gender did not predict. — [Nixdorf 2016](https://doi.org/10.1097/j.pain.0000000000000343) **[A]**
- ADA 2024: for patients already on long-term opioids for chronic pain, "clinicians should prioritize the use of nonopioid analgesics". — [ADA 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.adaj.2023.10.009) **[FT]**

**Red hair / MC1R**
- JADA study (n = 144; 67 red-haired, 77 dark-haired; 85 with MC1R variants): variant carriers reported significantly more dental-care anxiety and fear of dental pain and "were more than twice as likely to avoid dental care", after controlling for trait anxiety and sex. Binkley CJ et al. J Am Dent Assoc 2009;140(7):896-905. PMID 19571053 **[A]** — [Binkley 2009](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.2009.0283)
- IANB trial in red-haired vs dark-haired women with healthy pulps (2 cartridges 2% lidocaine 1:100,000; EPT for 60 min; MC1R genotyped): red hair and 2 red-hair-colour alleles were linked to significantly higher dental anxiety, but "No significant differences in anesthetic success were found between any of the groups for any of the teeth." Droll B et al. J Endod 2012;38(12):1564-9. PMID 23146638 **[A]** — [Droll 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2012.08.014)
- Experimental pain study in women: redheads more sensitive to cold pain (perception threshold 22.6 vs 12.6 °C; P = .004) and heat pain, and subcutaneous lidocaine significantly less effective (P = .005). Liem EB et al. Anesthesiology 2005;102(3):509-14. PMID 15731586 **[A]** — [Liem 2005](https://doi.org/10.1097/00000542-200503000-00006)
- Genetic-epidemiology cohort: MC1R variant alleles predicted higher dental fear and fear of pain; "fear of pain mediated the relation between MC1R variant status and dental fear (B = 1.60, 95% CI 0.281 to 3.056)". Randall CL et al. J Dent Res 2016;95(10):1132-7. PMID 27555332 **[A]** — [Randall 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022034516661151)

**Other "hard to numb" self-reports**
- Chronic marijuana users vs non-users (pilot, n = 35, maxillary infiltration): success 61% vs 88% (P = .073, NS). Moran MC et al. Anesth Prog 2022;69(4):15-21. PMID 36534774 **[A]** — [Moran 2022](https://doi.org/10.2344/anpr-69-02-08)

### Inferences
- The MC1R literature is best summarised to patients and residents as: redheads are more anxious and more pain-fearful (plausibly from earlier bad experiences or heightened thermal pain sensitivity), and deserve extra anxiety management and careful testing — but there is no direct evidence that standard IANB fails more often in them, and none at all in inflamed pulps.
- Predictability is a modifiable driver of pain in catastrophizers (Lin 2013 JDR): narrating upcoming sensations (tell-show-do), avoiding surprises, and a brief invitation to voice worries before starting (Sullivan & Neish 1999) are cheap, evidence-informed tactics for this subgroup.
- For rubber dam, the data suggest the resident's speed and duration of use, more than the dam itself, drive negative opinions — arguing for rehearsed placement, anesthetizing gingiva for the clamp, a clear airway explanation and a stop signal, with breaks in long sessions.
- In an emergency hot tooth in a highly anxious patient, a short pain-relieving visit (pulpotomy/pulpectomy under planned supplemental anesthesia) followed by scheduled definitive treatment aligns with duration–pain data and with the "staging" recommendation; this is an inference, not a tested strategy.
- Patients with long pre-op pain duration, chronic pain history or high catastrophizing should receive explicit, realistic counselling that some lingering discomfort is more likely, since unmet expectations may otherwise confirm fear.

### Gaps
- No studies were found on rubber-dam–specific claustrophobia or gagging in adult endodontic patients (prevalence or management); adult gag-reflex management evidence in endodontics was not located in this search.
- No endodontic study of single- vs multiple-visit treatment with anxiety as an outcome was reviewed.
- Evidence on anxiety and patient-reported outcomes specifically in endodontic microsurgery is sparse (one topical-anesthetic RCT and one n = 20 preliminary report found).
- No study tests MC1R/red hair against anesthetic success in symptomatic irreversible pulpitis; Droll 2012 was confined to women with healthy pulps.
- No endodontic RCT of catastrophizing-targeted interventions was found; Sullivan & Neish 1999 used hygiene treatment in undergraduates.

## 8. The clinician side: empathy, communication training, and operator stress

### Takeaway
Patients rate the dentist's explicit commitment to preventing pain as the single most anxiety-reducing behaviour, and perceived empathy/communicativeness tracks satisfaction and lower anxiety; dentists recognise anxiety only moderately well. Treating anxious patients is a recognised occupational stressor, stressed dentists are more likely to attribute anxiety to the patient's psychology, and endodontics is among the most stressful disciplines for learners — but evidence that communication or empathy training changes patient anxiety outcomes in dentistry is weak.

### Cited Findings
- 250 adult patients (two hospital dental clinics) rated 25 dentist behaviours immediately after treatment: ten were associated with anxiety reduction; in stepwise regression "the dentist's explicit dedication to prevent pain was the most important dentist behavior, with friendliness, working quickly, being calm, and giving moral support, being important auxiliary behaviors"; empathy and communicativeness were among the main correlates of satisfaction. Corah NL et al. J Am Dent Assoc 1988;116(1):73-6. PMID 3422668 **[A]** — [Corah 1988](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.1988.0162)
- Review of empathy in the dentist–patient relationship: empathy "positively associated with negotiated treatment plans, treatment adherence, increased patient satisfaction, and reduced dental anxiety", but rarely operationally defined or empirically measured. Jones LM, Huggins TJ. N Z Dent J 2014;110(3):98-104. PMID 25265748 **[A]** — [Jones & Huggins 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25265748/)
- Endodontic patients: correlation between patients' anxiety and dentists' recognition of it was only moderate (Spearman rho = 0.460, P < .001); dentists' expected and perceived patient pain did not differ, while patients expected more than they felt. — [Perković 2014](https://doi.org/10.15644/asc47/4/3) **[A]**
- Endodontic patients: "Dentists are more closely attuned to the pain experiences of their female patients"; authors advise improving "pain communication with male patients". — [Watkins 2002](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.2002.0020) **[A]**
- Survey of Danish dentists: nearly all "talked with anxious patients as their main treatment strategy"; dentists who attributed dental anxiety primarily to patients' general psychological problems more often reported high perceived stress (OR 2.2), solo practice (OR 2.4) and practices older than 18 years (OR 2.6); no association between dentists' stress and use of pharmacological strategies; "Some dentists appeared to require more knowledge about dental anxiety and managing their own stress." Moore R, Brødsgaard I. Community Dent Oral Epidemiol 2001;29(1):73-80. PMID 11153566 **[A]** — [Moore & Brødsgaard 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11153566/)
- 200 dentists (80% response): specialists had higher empathy scores than general practitioners; vicarious trauma correlated positively with weekly working hours and negatively with empathy; best predictor of empathy was hours of sleep per night, then vicarious trauma and age. Uziel N et al. Int Dent J 2019;69(5):348-353. PMID 31102260 **[A]** — [Uziel 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/idj.12487)
- Review: heart rate and blood pressure change in the *dentist* as well as the patient during treatment. — [Brand 1999](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1875-595x.1999.tb00509.x) **[A]**
- Narrative review (Int Endod J 2026) of undergraduate stress in endodontic education: endodontics is considered challenging and stressful; "The management of the endodontic patient's expectations, personality and sometimes acute pain can be stressful for the student"; supportive, consistent supervisors, hands-on practice, reflective teaching, systematic feedback and sufficient clinical time moderate stress and build confidence. Fouillen KJ, Duncan HF, Chevalier V. Int Endod J 2026;59(6):1013-1031. PMID 40778615 **[A]** — [Fouillen 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.70012)
- Operator experience affects patient experience: students' patients were less positive about rubber dam than dentists' patients, with longer placement times. — [Stewardson & McHugh 2002](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2591.2002.00571.x) **[A]**
- AAE reassurance for the operator: with profound lip anesthesia, failure of pulpal anesthesia "is NOT the fault of the clinician for giving an inaccurate injection". — [AAE Winter 2009](https://www.aae.org/specialty/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2017/07/winter09ecfe.pdf) **[FT]**
- Treating anxious patients is described as stressful for the dentist (reduced cooperation, more time and resources) and as producing an unpleasant experience for both parties. — [Appukuttan 2016](https://doi.org/10.2147/CCIDE.S63626) **[FT]**
- TIC pyramid level 4: providers should understand their own trauma history because of the risk of "retriggering their own feelings". — [Raja 2014](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.2013.30) **[FT]**
- Communication-skills frameworks with organisational outcome data come from medicine (Four Habits: sustained patient-satisfaction gains at Kaiser Permanente) — [Stein 2005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pec.2005.01.014) **[A]**; ask-tell-ask and empathic responding help clinicians "use time efficiently, avoid burnout" — [Back 2005](https://doi.org/10.3322/canjclin.55.3.164) **[A]**

### Inferences
- "I will not start until we have tested that the tooth is numb, and I will stop and add more if you feel anything" is simultaneously the highest-yield anxiety-reducing statement (Corah 1988) and an accurate description of the AAE-recommended anesthesia workflow — the pain-control protocol and the communication protocol are the same script.
- Knowing that IANB failure in a hot tooth is expected and not an operator error should reduce resident stress and the temptation to push on; a pre-planned supplemental sequence turns a stressful improvisation into routine.
- Because dentists' detection of anxiety is only moderate (rho ≈0.46) and worse for men's pain, a brief formal screen (MDAS) is preferable to clinical impression.
- Clinician sleep, workload and vicarious-trauma load plausibly affect empathy at the chair; residency programmes could treat these as patient-experience variables, though this is extrapolated from one cross-sectional survey.

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly measures the interaction between operator (resident) stress and patient anxiety or pain during endodontic treatment (e.g., dyadic physiological or outcome data).
- No RCT of communication-skills or empathy training for dentists/residents with patient dental-anxiety outcomes was located; Jones & Huggins note empathy is rarely measured.
- Data on stress specifically in *endodontic residents* (as opposed to undergraduates or dentists generally) were not found.
- Corah 1988 is a single cross-sectional patient-perception study from hospital clinics; causality untested (the authors themselves call for experimental work).
- Hill/Brahm-type surveys of dentists' stress when treating fearful adults (e.g., Brahm et al., Swed Dent J 2012) were not retrieved or verified in this session.
