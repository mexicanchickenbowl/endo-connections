# Pharmacological management of dental anxiety (anxiolysis and sedation) in adult endodontic patients

Sourcing note: bibliographic details and abstract-level results below were retrieved from PubMed (PMIDs given; DOI links included wherever PubMed supplied one). Guideline text was read directly from the ADA (2025 edition), SDCEP (2017), AAPD (nitrous oxide best practice) and Pfizer (Halcion label) documents. Unless stated otherwise, trial results are abstract-level; full texts were not opened. Items I could not verify are flagged in each Gaps section.

## 1. Oral benzodiazepines: anxiolytic efficacy, dosing, and effect on IANB success in symptomatic irreversible pulpitis

### Takeaway
Single-dose oral or sublingual benzodiazepines (best dental evidence: triazolam 0.25 mg, alprazolam 0.5-0.75 mg, midazolam 7.5-15 mg) reduce state anxiety during treatment, with low-certainty pooled evidence (SMD about -0.43), at the cost of amnesia and psychomotor impairment. They do not improve inferior alveolar nerve block (IANB) success in irreversible pulpitis (two placebo-controlled RCTs, pooled RR 0.99), so profound local anesthesia is still required.

### Cited Findings

Anxiolysis in endodontic patients (the only endodontic-specific benzodiazepine anxiety RCT found)
- Ehrich DG, Lundgren JP, Dionne RA, Nicoll BK, Hutter JW. J Endod 1997;23(3):181-4. Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled; 79 endodontic patients with Corah Dental Anxiety Scale score of 10 or more; oral triazolam 0.25 mg vs diazepam 5 mg vs placebo. Triazolam was significantly better than diazepam and placebo for decreased anxiety (p < 0.05), patient-rated drug effectiveness (p < 0.05), and amnesia for clinical events (p < 0.02); it also impaired cognitive function more (p < 0.05). Diazepam showed the same trends to a lesser degree and had "a much longer recovery period". No adverse effects with either drug. PMID 9594761 — [J Endod 1997](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0099-2399(97)80272-5)
- Verma et al. J Conserv Dent 2021;24(2):209-213. Small RCT (n = 30 root canal patients with VAS-anxiety > 4): yogic relaxation vs alprazolam 0.25/0.5 mg vs placebo. Global efficacy rated "excellent" in 90% (yoga), 20% (alprazolam), 10% (placebo). Very small, single-centre; treat as hypothesis-generating. PMID 34759592 — [J Conserv Dent 2021](https://doi.org/10.4103/jcd.jcd_97_21)

Triazolam (other dental models)
- Berthold CW, Dionne RA, Corey SE. Oral Surg Oral Med Oral Pathol Oral Radiol Endod 1997;84(2):119-24. Double-blind, placebo-controlled; 0.25 mg sublingual vs 0.25 mg oral triazolam vs placebo, 1 hour before oral surgery. Sublingual produced significantly less anxiety and pain at 15 minutes intraoperatively than oral or placebo (p < 0.05) and higher plasma levels; recovery and side effects did not differ. PMID 9269010 — [OOOOE 1997](https://doi.org/10.1016/s1079-2104(97)90055-x)
- Kaufman E, Hargreaves KM, Dionne RA. Oral Surg Oral Med Oral Pathol 1993;75(2):156-64. Two oral-surgery studies: triazolam 0.25 mg plus nitrous oxide gave therapeutic effect with faster recovery than 0.5 mg plus nitrous oxide; oral triazolam produced anxiolysis comparable to IV diazepam titrated to clinical endpoint (mean 19.3 mg) with less cognitive-psychomotor impairment. PMID 8426714 — [OOO 1993](https://doi.org/10.1016/0030-4220(93)90086-j)
- Milgrom P, Quarnstrom FC, Longley A, Libed E. Anesth Prog 1994;41(3):70-6. Double-blind RCT, 31 highly anxious dental patients, triazolam 0.375 or 0.5 mg vs placebo 1 hour before treatment: reduced anxious cognitions and disruptive movement during injection and drilling; episodic and implicit memory impaired. PMID 8934963 — [Anesth Prog 1994 (PMC2148823)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8934963/)
- Stopperich PS, Moore PA, et al. Anesth Prog 1993;40(4):117-21. Double-blind RCT, n = 22, triazolam 0.25 mg orally 45-60 minutes before venipuncture for IV sedation: lower VAS and ISAR anxiety, lower IV sedative dose requirements, less unpleasant venipuncture. PMID 7943920 — [Anesth Prog 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7943920/)
- Jackson DL, Milgrom P, Heacox GA, Kharasch ED. J Clin Psychopharmacol 2006;26(1):4-8. Incremental sublingual triazolam (0.25 mg, then 0.5 mg at 60 minutes, then 0.25 mg at 90 minutes; total 1.0 mg) in 10 healthy volunteers: 8 of 10 reached Observer's Assessment of Alertness/Sedation scores < 3 (consistent with deep sedation or general anesthesia) at later time points; large inter-subject variability; authors could not endorse the multidose strategy. PMID 16415697 — [J Clin Psychopharmacol 2006](https://doi.org/10.1097/01.jcp.0000186742.07148.da)
- Gordon SM, Shimizu N, Shlash D, Dionne RA. Gen Dent 2007;55(5):410-5. Mail survey of Dental Organization for Conscious Sedation members; 7,740 incremental enteral sedation cases (triazolam most common); 1,686 (21.8%) met pre-specified event criteria, most often a fall of more than 25% in diastolic BP; less than 12 months of experience predicted events (p = 0.001). PMID 17899717 — [Gen Dent 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17899717/)
- Dionne RA, Yagiela JA, Coté CJ, et al. J Am Dent Assoc 2006;137(4):502-13 (multi-organization workshop consensus). "Using oral medication to achieve anxiolysis in adults appears to have a wide margin of safety"; enteral benzodiazepines appear "safe but poorly documented in the office setting"; state regulation of enteral conscious sedation was called for. PMID 16637480 — [JADA 2006](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.2006.0223)
- Goodchild JH, Donaldson M. Anesth Prog 2005;52(1):17-20. Case report of hallucinations and delirium after triazolam for oral conscious sedation (patient also on oxycodone per MeSH terms). PMID 15859444 — [Anesth Prog 2005](https://doi.org/10.2344/0003-3006(2005)52[17:HADITD]2.0.CO;2)
- Kontinen VK, et al. Can J Anaesth 1993;40(9):829-34. Sublingual triazolam 0.2 mg vs oral diazepam 10 mg (n = 100, ophthalmic surgery): equal anxiolysis, deeper sedation with triazolam; 10 of 50 triazolam patients (20%) were judged too sedated, all aged 61-70; authors concluded the dose is excessive for elderly patients. PMID 8403177 — [Can J Anaesth 1993](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF03009253)

Alprazolam
- Coldwell SE, Milgrom P, Getz T, Ramsay DS. J Oral Maxillofac Surg 1997;55(10):1061-70. Two double-blind experiments (n = 36 and n = 48); oral alprazolam 0.25, 0.5 or 0.75 mg vs placebo 45 minutes before testing or oral surgery. Only 0.75 mg significantly reduced anxiety during surgery; 0.5 and 0.75 mg impaired word recall. Conclusion: alprazolam "produces memory impairment at the dosages necessary to produce clinically significant anxiolysis". PMID 9331228 — [JOMS 1997](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0278-2391(97)90280-9)
- Nutt D, Mandel F, Baldinetti F. J Psychopharmacol 2009;23(8):867-73. Dental anxiety model, n = 89, alprazolam 0.5 mg vs pregabalin 150 mg vs placebo given 4 hours before a dental procedure: both active drugs improved VAS-anxiety slopes vs placebo (alprazolam t = -2.39, P = 0.018); alprazolam separated from placebo from 2 hours. PMID 18635690 — [J Psychopharmacol 2009](https://doi.org/10.1177/0269881108094722)
- De Witte JL, et al. Anesth Analg 2002;95(6):1601-6 (ambulatory gynecologic surgery, n = 45): alprazolam 0.5 mg and midazolam 7.5 mg both reduced anxiety more than placebo; amnesia in 5 patients, all midazolam; alprazolam gave more early psychomotor impairment. PMID 12456424 — [Anesth Analg 2002](https://doi.org/10.1097/00000539-200212000-00024)

Diazepam, lorazepam, midazolam (oral)
- Araújo JO, et al. BMJ Open 2021;11(1):e043363. Systematic review of oral sedation in adult dental patients: 12 RCTs, n = 327 (9 benzodiazepine, 3 herbal). Reduced anxiety with alprazolam 0.5 and 0.75 mg; good satisfaction with midazolam 7.5 mg; midazolam 15 mg reduced anxiety more than Passiflora 260 mg but caused more adverse effects; diazepam 15 mg caused less heart-rate and BP change than placebo. Authors: evidence quality limited, further RCTs needed. PMID 33495257 — [BMJ Open 2021](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2020-043363)
- Chen Q, et al. PLoS One 2015;10(4):e0121410. Systematic review, 10 RCTs of midazolam for third-molar surgery (no meta-analysis possible): all but one showed anxiety relief; versus lorazepam and diazepam, midazolam was not clearly superior for sedation "but was safer"; multidrug IV regimens were more effective but riskier. PMID 25849859 — [PLoS One 2015](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0121410)
- Sharma et al. J Maxillofac Oral Surg 2019;18(2):256-259. Double-blind RCT (50 per group, third molars): lorazepam (night sedation plus premedication) preferred over diazepam, but recovery was longer. Doses not in abstract. PMID 30996548 — [JMOS 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12663-018-1096-1)
- Zanette G, et al. Minerva Stomatol 2013;62(10):355-74. RCT (n = 88, IV titration, diazepam up to 8 mg vs midazolam up to 4 mg): diazepam gave more "mild" sedation and higher subjective tranquillity; midazolam more moderate/deep sedation, lower SpO2, more amnesia. PMID 24217684 — [Minerva Stomatol 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24217684/)
- Conway A, Rolley J, Sutherland JR. Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2016;(5):CD009491 (30 trials, 2,319 participants; dental procedures were excluded): no high-quality evidence that midazolam alone sedates better than placebo or comparators; "inconsistent evidence that oral midazolam decreased anxiety"; low-quality evidence for IV midazolam vs placebo. PMID 27198122 — [Cochrane 2016](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD009491.pub2)

Pooled estimates
- Steenen SA, Linke F, van Westrhenen R, de Jongh A. J Anxiety Disord 2024;105:102891. 173 RCTs included, 67 in 14 pooled analyses. For state anxiety during oral surgery: benzodiazepines SMD = -0.43 (95% CI -0.74 to -0.12), low certainty; hypnosis SMD = -0.31 (moderate certainty). PMID 38945067 — [J Anxiety Disord 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.janxdis.2024.102891)
- Lu C, et al. Odontology 2023;111(1):20-32. 20 RCTs; "sedation drugs" subgroup SMD = -0.61 (95% CI -1.10 to -0.12) for anxiety; no effect on pain or vital signs overall. PMID 35536282 — [Odontology 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10266-022-00711-x)
- Stonkutė et al. Dent J (Basel) 2026;14(6). Systematic review, 8 trials (n = 617) of preoperative anxiolysis for oral surgery under local anesthesia: "benzodiazepines demonstrating the most consistent efficacy" with no significant inter-agent differences; adverse events mild. PMID 42345918 — [Dent J 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/dj14060327)

Effect on IANB success in irreversible pulpitis
- Lindemann M, Reader A, Nusstein J, Drum M, Beck M (Ohio State). J Endod 2008;34(10):1167-70. Prospective, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled; 58 emergency patients, irreversible pulpitis in a mandibular posterior tooth; sublingual triazolam 0.25 mg vs placebo 30 minutes before a conventional IANB; access 15 minutes after block; all had profound lip numbness; success = no or mild VAS pain on access/instrumentation. Success 43% triazolam vs 57% placebo (P = .43). Conclusion: "when using conscious sedation, profound local anesthesia is still required". PMID 18793913 — [J Endod 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2008.07.013)
- Khademi AA, Saatchi M, Minaiyan M, Rostamizadeh N, Sharafi F. J Endod 2012;38(10):1337-9. Prospective, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled; 60 patients, mandibular molars; oral alprazolam 0.5 mg vs placebo 45 minutes before IANB. Success 53% vs 40% (P = .301). Note: this trial is from Isfahan University of Medical Sciences, Iran, not Ohio State. PMID 22980173 — [J Endod 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2012.06.007)
- Lapidus D, et al. J Am Dent Assoc 2016;147(6):427-37. Meta-analysis of 9 RCTs of oral premedication plus 2% lidocaine IANB: NSAIDs improved "little or no pain" (RR 1.989; 95% CI 1.495-2.646; P < .001); the 2 benzodiazepine trials showed no benefit (RR 0.989; 95% CI 0.677-1.444; P = .954). PMID 26952243 — [JADA 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.adaj.2016.01.006)
- Pulikkotil SJ, et al. Int Endod J 2018;51(9):989-1004. Network meta-analysis, 19 RCTs, n = 1,654: dexamethasone (RR 2.92), NSAIDs (RR 1.92) and tramadol (RR 2.03) increased IANB success vs placebo; sensitivity analyses showed "the superiority of dexamethasone or NSAIDs over any other premedications". PMID 29480930 — [Int Endod J 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.12912)
- Conflicting, lower-quality report: Shetkar P, et al. J Conserv Dent 2016;19(5):390-5 (n = 198; IANB vs Gow-Gates vs Vazirani-Akinosi, each with or without premedication) concluded that a combination of alprazolam plus diclofenac potassium improved pain control. The anxiolytic was not tested separately from the NSAID, so the result cannot be attributed to alprazolam. PMID 27656053 — [J Conserv Dent 2016](https://doi.org/10.4103/0972-0707.190013)
- Anxiety does track with block failure: de Oliveira et al. Clin Oral Investig 2024;28(7):366 (n = 150, ibuprofen vs ibuprofen-arginine vs placebo). In the active groups, patients with successful IANB had lower median Modified Dental Anxiety Scale scores (8) than those with failed blocks (15). PMID 38850383 — [Clin Oral Investig 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00784-024-05765-5)

Dosing, pharmacokinetics, contraindications (triazolam label)
- Halcion (triazolam) label: adult dose 0.25 mg; "The maximum recommended dosage is 0.5 mg once daily"; geriatric start 0.125 mg, maximum 0.25 mg; peak plasma levels "within 2 hours"; elimination half-life 1.5-5.5 hours. Contraindicated with strong CYP3A inhibitors (ketoconazole, itraconazole, nefazodone, lopinavir, ritonavir; section 5.8 also lists indinavir, nelfinavir, saquinavir). Caution and consider dose reduction with erythromycin, clarithromycin, cimetidine; avoid grapefruit juice (48% rise in AUC). Boxed warning: concomitant opioids "may result in profound sedation, respiratory depression, coma, and death". Anterograde amnesia and paradoxical reactions reported; respiratory depression and apnea reported in patients with compromised respiratory function — [Pfizer Halcion label](https://labeling.pfizer.com/ShowLabeling.aspx?id=586)
- Flanagan D. J Oral Implantol 2004;30(2):93-7 (review): triazolam 0.125 or 0.25 mg, not exceeding 0.5 mg; contraindicated in pregnancy, breast-feeding, and with alcohol, macrolides, some protease inhibitors, azole antifungals, nefazodone; caution with grapefruit juice, cyclosporine and calcium channel blockers (nifedipine, verapamil, diltiazem); reversible with IV flumazenil "although there is a risk of seizure". PMID 15119458 — [J Oral Implantol 2004](https://doi.org/10.1563/1548-1336(2004)30<93:OTSIID>2.0.CO;2)

Escort
- SDCEP: a premedicated patient "will need to be escorted to and from the treatment facility and should not drive", and "A premedicated patient cannot give valid consent for treatment" — [SDCEP Conscious Sedation in Dentistry, 3rd ed., section 3.7](https://www.sdcep.org.uk/media/iegenn4c/sdcep-conscious-sedation-guidance.pdf)
- ADA 2025 guidelines require pre- and post-operative verbal and written instructions to "the patient, parent, escort, legal guardian and/or caregiver" at every sedation level, including minimal — [ADA Guidelines for the Use of Sedation and General Anesthesia by Dentists, adopted October 2025](https://www.ada.org/-/media/project/ada-organization/ada/ada-org/files/resources/library/oral-health-topics/ada_sedation_use_guidelines.pdf)

### Inferences
- The clinical message for a resident: a benzodiazepine treats the patient's experience of the visit, not the block. The two IANB trials are small (58 and 60 patients) and the confidence interval around RR 0.99 is wide (0.68-1.44), so a modest effect in either direction is not excluded, but there is no signal of benefit.
- Triazolam 0.25 mg has the best endodontic-specific support (Ehrich 1997), but that trial compared it with a low diazepam dose (5 mg), so "triazolam beats diazepam" is dose-confounded.
- Amnesia is a consistent accompaniment of effective anxiolytic doses (triazolam, alprazolam 0.5-0.75 mg, midazolam). That matters for consent (obtain before dosing) and for post-operative instructions (give in writing, to the escort).
- Sublingual triazolam gives higher plasma levels than swallowed tablets at the same dose, so sublingual dosing is not dose-equivalent to oral.
- Because the triazolam label maximum is 0.5 mg, stacked or "incremental" protocols that exceed 0.5 mg fall outside the ADA definition of minimal sedation (see section 6), and the Jackson 2006 data show such protocols can reach deep sedation.

### Gaps
- No RCT was found testing lorazepam or oral midazolam specifically in adult endodontic patients, and none testing any benzodiazepine's effect on IANB success other than Lindemann 2008 and Khademi 2012.
- Labelled onset, peak and half-life figures were verified only for triazolam. I did not retrieve label data for alprazolam, diazepam, lorazepam or oral midazolam; typical clinical doses for those agents are given above only as used in trials.
- No US guideline text was found stating an explicit escort rule for single-dose oral anxiolysis; the ADA text refers to an "escort" only as a recipient of instructions. State rules were not checked individually.
- The Verma 2021 and Shetkar 2016 trials are in journals with limited reporting detail; results should not be weighted like the J Endod trials.

## 2. Nitrous oxide/oxygen: anxiolysis in adults, effect on IANB success, contraindications

### Takeaway
Nitrous oxide is the one anxiolytic with randomized evidence of improving IANB success in symptomatic irreversible pulpitis (50% vs 28%, Stanley 2012), but success with nitrous oxide remains only 44-58% across later Ohio State trials, so supplemental anesthesia is still needed. Adult anxiolysis evidence is positive but thin, and no meta-analysis of nitrous oxide and IANB success was found.

### Cited Findings

IANB success and pain
- Stanley W, Drum M, Nusstein J, Reader A, Beck M. J Endod 2012;38(5):565-9. Prospective, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled; 100 emergency patients, symptomatic irreversible pulpitis, mandibular posterior tooth; 30%-50% nitrous oxide vs room air/oxygen started 5 minutes before IANB; access 15 minutes after block. Success (no or mild VAS pain) 50% vs 28% (P = .024). PMID 22515880 — [J Endod 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2012.02.010)
- Stentz D, Drum M, Reader A, Nusstein J, Fowler S, Beck M. J Endod 2018;44(1):9-13. RCT, n = 102; intranasal ketorolac 31.5 mg vs saline, both followed by nitrous oxide then IANB. No significant difference (odds 1.631 favouring saline/nitrous, P = .2523); "Supplemental anesthesia will still be needed". PMID 29153730 — [J Endod 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2017.09.011)
- MacDonald E, Drum M, Nusstein J, Fowler S, Beck M, Reader A. J Endod 2021;47(4):558-565. RCT, n = 62; nitrous oxide plus IANB with lidocaine/clonidine 27 µg vs lidocaine/epinephrine 18 µg: 58% success in both arms. PMID 33387550 — [J Endod 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2020.12.014)
- Chen, Nusstein J, Drum M, Fowler S, Reader A, Guo. J Endod 2021;47(12):1890-1895. Prospective single-arm, n = 94: IANB with nitrous oxide succeeded in 44% (95% CI 34%-54%); overall success rose to 69% after one set of supplemental PDL injections (4% articaine) and 80% after a second set. PMID 34492232 — [J Endod 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2021.08.013)
- Kushnir B, Fowler S, Drum M, Nusstein J, Reader A, Beck M. Anesth Prog 2021;68(2):69-75. Crossover, 105 asymptomatic subjects: nitrous oxide significantly reduced pain of needle insertion, placement and deposition (P < .05) and increased odds of pulpal anesthesia, but "the incidence of pulpal anesthesia was not 100%". PMID 34185865 — [Anesth Prog 2021](https://doi.org/10.2344/anpr-68-01-10)
- Chompu-Inwai P, et al. Anesth Prog 2018;65(3):156-161. Preliminary RCT in 33 children (mean age 10.4) with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis in permanent teeth, ibuprofen premedication, 4% articaine: perceived pulpal anesthesia success 71% (12/17) with nitrous oxide vs 19% (3/16) with oxygen (p = .003). Pediatric; included for mechanism only. PMID 30235437 — [Anesth Prog 2018](https://doi.org/10.2344/anpr-65-02-08)

Anxiolysis
- Gupta PD, et al. J Conserv Dent 2019;22(4):356-361. RCT, 60 anxious adults, irreversible pulpitis in a lower molar; local anesthesia alone vs local anesthesia plus nitrous oxide: significant reductions in Modified Dental Anxiety Scale anxiety and in VAS pain during injection and access. Effect sizes not in abstract. PMID 31802819 — [J Conserv Dent 2019](https://doi.org/10.4103/JCD.JCD_332_18)
- Edmunds, Drum M, Fowler S, Nusstein J, Reader A. Anesth Prog 2025;72(3):143-150. Crossover, 132 female subjects, maxillary infiltration with nitrous oxide vs virtual-reality headset: nitrous oxide lowered salivary cortisol more than VR (P = .0089); injection pain scores did not differ. PMID 40925631 — [Anesth Prog 2025](https://doi.org/10.2344/24-0036)
- Morse DR, et al. J Human Stress 1981. 29 endodontic clinic patients: hypnosis and nitrous oxide-oxygen were significantly more effective (p < 0.05) than local anesthesia alone for anxiety reduction, by salivary measures and questionnaire. Non-randomized, historical. PMID 7276547 — [J Human Stress 1981](https://doi.org/10.1080/0097840X.1981.9936829)
- Pereira-Santos D, et al. J Craniofac Surg 2013;24(5):1636-9. 28 men, third-molar extraction: midazolam was more effective than nitrous oxide at lowering salivary cortisol. PMID 24036742 — [J Craniofac Surg 2013](https://doi.org/10.1097/SCS.0b013e3182a15dfc)
- Thompson JM, et al. Br Dent J 1999;187(10):557-62. Three-way crossover: midazolam slowed reaction time and impaired recall; nitrous oxide did not differ from no drug, so "patients receiving nitrous oxide sedation can resume normal activities in the post-operative period", whereas an adult escort and written instructions "should be mandatory for midazolam sedation patients". PMID 10630045 — [Br Dent J 1999](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.bdj.4800330)
- Willumsen T, Vassend O, Hoffart A. Acta Odontol Scand 2001;59(5):290-6. RCT, 65 adults with severe dental fear randomized to nitrous oxide sedation, cognitive therapy or applied relaxation over 10 weekly sessions: dental fear scores fell significantly in all groups with no major between-group differences short term. PMID 11680648 — [Acta Odontol Scand 2001](https://doi.org/10.1080/000163501750541156)

Contraindications and practical points
- AAPD best practice (pediatric document, but the pharmacology is general): not candidates — bowel obstruction, raised intraocular pressure, recent retinal surgery, craniotomy, or correction of intestinal obstruction; "patients with untreated vitamin B12 deficiency are not candidates"; MTHFR variants raise homocysteine after exposure, with short exposures "unlikely to be of significance"; asthma is not a contraindication; nausea and vomiting occur in 0.5-1.2%; fasting not required; give 100% oxygen for at least 5 minutes afterwards to avoid diffusion hypoxia — [AAPD Use of Nitrous Oxide for Pediatric Dental Patients](https://www.aapd.org/globalassets/media/policies_guidelines/bp_useofnitrous26.pdf)
- ADA Oral Health Topic (last updated August 10, 2023): systems typically limited to a maximum of 70% nitrous oxide/30% oxygen; oxygen fail-safe; scavenging with vacuum up to 45 L/min; NIOSH recommended exposure limit 25 ppm; chronic unscavenged occupational exposure linked to infertility, spontaneous abortion, blood dyscrasias and neurologic deficits via vitamin B12 oxidation; 100% oxygen for 5 minutes post-procedure; a 2007 ADA survey estimated 70% of practices using any sedation used nitrous oxide — [ADA Nitrous Oxide](https://www.ada.org/resources/ada-library/oral-health-topics/nitrous-oxide)
- ADA 2025 guidelines: inhalation equipment must have a fail-safe and either a device that "prohibits the delivery of less than 30% oxygen" or an in-line oxygen analyzer with audible alarm, plus scavenging; and "Nitrous oxide/oxygen when used in combination with sedative agent(s) may produce minimal, moderate, deep sedation or general anesthesia" — [ADA 2025 use guidelines](https://www.ada.org/-/media/project/ada-organization/ada/ada-org/files/resources/library/oral-health-topics/ada_sedation_use_guidelines.pdf)
- SDCEP: "Adults receiving inhalation sedation with nitrous oxide/oxygen do not usually require an escort"; clinical monitoring alone is the minimum for nitrous oxide — [SDCEP 2017, sections 3.6 and 6](https://www.sdcep.org.uk/media/iegenn4c/sdcep-conscious-sedation-guidance.pdf)
- AAE-published practice article (Caputo AC, 3 Sept 2018): nitrous oxide-oxygen is "an excellent option for the mild to moderately anxious patient" and an adjunct to other sedation; if oral sedation is inadequate, consider raising nitrous oxide rather than giving more sedative — [AAE: 'Having a Baby is Easier Than Getting a Root Canal'](https://www.aae.org/specialty/having-a-baby-is-easier-than-getting-a-root-canal/)

### Inferences
- Stanley 2012 is a single trial with a low placebo success rate (28%). The later Ohio State studies used nitrous oxide in every arm, so they do not re-test the nitrous oxide effect; they do show that even with nitrous oxide roughly half of IANBs fail in symptomatic irreversible pulpitis.
- The benzodiazepine and nitrous oxide results diverge (no IANB benefit vs benefit). A plausible reading is that nitrous oxide's analgesic action, not anxiolysis alone, drives the difference; this is inference, not something the trials tested.
- For a resident without a moderate sedation permit, nitrous oxide is the most defensible adjunct: titratable, rapid recovery, no cognitive hangover, usually no escort, and a block-success signal.

### Gaps
- No systematic review or meta-analysis pooling nitrous oxide effects on IANB success was found.
- No systematic review of nitrous oxide anxiolysis restricted to adult dental patients was found; adult RCT evidence is small and heterogeneous.
- Contraindications were verified from a pediatric (AAPD) document. Commonly taught adult cautions — first-trimester pregnancy, severe COPD, pneumothorax, bleomycin therapy, recent middle-ear surgery — were not confirmed in a source I could read in full; an older AAPD guideline snippet listed COPD and first-trimester pregnancy as possible contraindications, and the current AAPD text advises consulting the prenatal provider.
- Gupta 2019 reports significance without effect sizes in the abstract.

## 3. IV moderate sedation and general anesthesia for endodontics: indications, outcomes, uptake, safety

### Takeaway
Demand for sedation in endodontics is high (about half of surveyed patients are interested), yet in a 2018 national survey only 4% of endodontists provided IV sedation themselves and 26% used another provider; only 10% felt adequately trained. IV midazolam-based regimens are effective anxiolytics in large oral-surgery trials; newer agents (dexmedetomidine, remimazolam) appear comparable rather than superior; endodontic outcomes under general anesthesia in special-needs patients are acceptable (81.5-90% success).

### Cited Findings

Demand and uptake in endodontics

Safety
- Perrott DH, et al. J Oral Maxillofac Surg 2003;61(9):983-95. Prospective cohort, 34,191 office patients of US oral and maxillofacial surgeons (71.9% deep sedation/GA, 15.5% conscious sedation, 12.6% local): complication rate 1.3 per 100, minor and self-limiting; 2 hospitalizations; 94.3% satisfied. PMID 12966471 — [JOMS 2003](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0278-2391(03)00668-2)
- Wardius et al. J Oral Maxillofac Surg 2019;77(8):1602-1610. Massachusetts OMS survey, 2015-2016: 0 deaths in 431,680 patient visits. Self-reported. PMID 30826394 — [JOMS 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joms.2019.01.048)
- Bennett JD, Kramer KJ, Bosack RC. J Am Dent Assoc 2015;146(9):705-8. Authors estimate that death and brain injury associated with deep sedation or GA administered by all dentists "most likely exceeds 1 per month"; airway compromise is a major contributor; they call for a national patient safety database. PMID 26314981 — [JADA 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.adaj.2015.04.005)

### Inferences
- Efficacy data for IV sedation come almost entirely from third-molar surgery. Applying them to endodontics is reasonable for anxiolysis but untested for endodontic-specific outcomes (visit length, rubber dam tolerance, anesthetic success).
- The safety denominators are from oral surgeons with residency anesthesia training and are self-reported; they should not be assumed to transfer to endodontists who took a continuing-education moderate sedation course.
- The Setty data show that in endodontics, 15% of moderate sedations were prompted by local anesthesia failure. Given the Lindemann finding, sedation in that scenario manages distress but does not fix the anesthesia.
- In practice the commonest model for endodontists is a separate anesthesia provider (26% vs 4% self-administered).

### Gaps
- No RCT of IV moderate sedation (midazolam with or without fentanyl, propofol, dexmedetomidine) specifically in adult endodontic patients was found, and no trial of IV sedation's effect on IANB success.
- No adverse-event or mortality data specific to sedation delivered in endodontic offices were found.
- No survey of endodontists' use of oral sedation or nitrous oxide (as opposed to IV sedation) was found; the Yehia surveys are from 2016-2017 and may not reflect current practice.
- GA outcome studies are in special-needs populations; no outcome data exist for GA used purely for dental phobia in endodontics.

## 4. Other agents: melatonin, antihistamines, beta-blockers, gabapentinoids, zolpidem, ketamine/intranasal agents, herbal agents, cannabinoids

### Takeaway
None of the non-benzodiazepine oral agents has convincing evidence for dental anxiolysis in adults. The only endodontic trial (oral CBD-rich extract, J Endod 2024) was negative; propranolol was negative in a quadruple-blind RCT; melatonin results are inconsistent to negative; pregabalin 150 mg and some herbal agents (valerian, passionflower) have small positive trials in oral-surgery models.

### Cited Findings

Cannabinoids
- de Andrade Silva et al. J Endod 2024;50(12):1698-1704. Double-blind RCT of preoperative oral CBD-rich cannabis extract vs placebo before endodontic treatment: no significant difference in reduction of dental anxiety (MDAS, VAS, salivary cortisol) and no difference in postoperative pain at 24, 48, 72 hours or 7 days. Mean anxiety scores were higher in the CBD group at each assessment (pre 5.06 vs 3.07; post 4.61 vs 2.60). Sample size and dose not in abstract. PMID 39505200 — [J Endod 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2024.10.010)
- Chrepa V, et al. J Dent Res 2024;103(3):235-242. RCT, 61 patients with moderate-severe toothache; single oral CBD 10 or 20 mg/kg vs placebo: maximum median VAS pain reduction 73% at 180 minutes; NNT 3.1 (10 mg/kg) and 2.4 (20 mg/kg); sedation, diarrhea and abdominal pain more frequent with CBD. This is an analgesia trial, not an anxiety trial. PMID 37910667 — [J Dent Res 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/00220345231200814)

Propranolol
- Steenen SA, et al. Front Psychiatry 2022;13:842353. Quadruple-blind RCT, patients with high fear of extractions; 120 mg perioperative oral propranolol (n = 19) vs placebo (n = 17) with fear-memory reactivation: no significant difference in change in dental trait anxiety at 1 month (Cohen's d = 0.23), intra-operative state anxiety, or phobia diagnoses. Protocol: 80 mg 1 hour pre-op plus 40 mg post-op. PMID 35264990 — [Front Psychiatry 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2022.842353); protocol PMID 26607848 — [Trials 2015](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13063-015-1065-7)

Melatonin
- Seet E, et al. Singapore Med J 2015;56(12):666-71. PMID 26702161 — [Singapore Med J 2015](https://doi.org/10.11622/smedj.2015186)
- Mukherjee et al. Br J Oral Maxillofac Surg 2025;63(5):393-400. Double-blind RCT, n = 78 (26 per arm), melatonin 6 mg vs music vs no intervention: both improved physiological parameters; cortisol fell post-operatively only with melatonin, suggesting delayed action. PMID 40263045 — [BJOMS 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bjoms.2025.03.009)
- Rosa et al. Med Oral Patol Oral Cir Bucal 2026;31(3):e390-e398. Double-blind RCT, n = 48, sublingual melatonin 15 mg vs placebo 45 minutes before third-molar surgery: no effect on state anxiety at any time point, psychomotor performance, sleep, or post-traumatic stress symptoms; no adverse events. PMID 41578909 — [Med Oral 2026](https://doi.org/10.4317/medoral.27846)

Gabapentinoids and clonidine
- Nutt D, et al. - Velasquez et al. Oral Maxillofac Surg 2024;28(3):1313-1320. Triple-blind RCT, n = 54: Valeriana officinalis and Passiflora incarnata each reduced STAI-state scores from before to after third-molar surgery (p < .05); placebo did not (p = .129). - Silexan (oral lavender oil): the only trial retrieved is in generalized anxiety disorder, not dentistry — Woelk H, Schläfke S. Antihistamines, zolpidem, zaleplon, ketamine/intranasal
- Dental trials of hydroxyzine and zolpidem retrieved were pediatric only, e.g. Faytrouny 2007 (hydroxyzine doses in children), PMID 17683328 — [Int J Paediatr Dent 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17683328/); Bhatnagar 2012 (midazolam vs tramadol, triclofos, zolpidem in children), PMID 22918094 — [J Indian Soc Pedod Prev Dent 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22918094/)
- An AAE-published practice article lists zolpidem and zaleplon alongside triazolam, diazepam, alprazolam and lorazepam as oral conscious sedation drugs, without doses or evidence — [AAE (Caputo 2018)](https://www.aae.org/specialty/having-a-baby-is-easier-than-getting-a-root-canal/)
- Intranasal sedation evidence in dentistry is predominantly pediatric (e.g. Swaminathan 2025 meta-analysis of intranasal sedation for pediatric dental procedures, PMID 39944848 — [J Dent Anesth Pain Med 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39944848/)); the adult exception found is Kaufman 1994 (intranasal midazolam in a dental phobia clinic, section 3).

### Inferences
- For an adult endodontic patient, none of these agents can be recommended over a benzodiazepine or nitrous oxide on current evidence. Pregabalin 150 mg is the most credible alternative signal, but its onset is slow (3-4 hours) and it is sedating.
- CBD's dental evidence is for analgesia in acute toothache, not anxiolysis; the endodontic anxiety trial was negative, and baseline imbalance in anxiety between arms limits interpretation.
- Propranolol blunts sympathetic symptoms but, in the one rigorous dental trial, did not reduce state or trait dental anxiety.

### Gaps
- No adult dental-setting RCT was found for hydroxyzine or other antihistamines, zolpidem, zaleplon, gabapentin, intranasal or oral ketamine, or oral lavender oil (Silexan). Searches returned pediatric or non-dental studies only.
- No endodontic-specific trial exists for melatonin, pregabalin, propranolol, clonidine or herbal agents.
- Lavender evidence in dentistry is inhaled aromatherapy (treated here as non-pharmacological and not reviewed).
- Herbal trials are small (10-18 per arm), mostly from single centres, with unstandardized extracts.

## 5. Systematic reviews and comparisons of pharmacological vs non-pharmacological strategies

### Takeaway
Meta-analyses support benzodiazepines for state anxiety during treatment (low certainty) and CBT for trait dental anxiety and phobia (moderate certainty); no network meta-analysis directly ranking pharmacological anxiolysis strategies in adult dentistry was found. Head-to-head trials show equivalence during the visit but better follow-through with psychological treatment.

### Cited Findings
- Steenen 2024 (173 RCTs): state anxiety — benzodiazepines SMD -0.43 (low certainty), hypnosis SMD -0.31 (moderate certainty); evidence does not support VR distraction, music, aromatherapy, video information or acupuncture for state anxiety. Trait anxiety — CBT SMD = -0.65 (95% CI -1.06 to -0.24), moderate certainty. Dental phobia — psychotherapy SMD = -0.48; CBT SMD = -0.43. Authors advise that clinicians "should ensure that interventions match their purpose", meaning either managing acute emotions during treatment or alleviating chronic anxiety and avoidance. PMID 38945067 — [J Anxiety Disord 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.janxdis.2024.102891)
- Wide Boman U, Carlsson V, Westin M, Hakeberg M. Eur J Oral Sci 2013;121(3 Pt 2):225-34. Systematic review, 10 RCT publications; comparators included sedation and general anesthesia: CBT/behavioural therapy reduced dental anxiety (mean difference -2.7 on the Dental Anxiety Scale; low quality of evidence); "some support that CBT/BT improves the patients' acceptance of dental treatment more than general anaesthesia does" (low quality). PMID 23659254 — [Eur J Oral Sci 2013](https://doi.org/10.1111/eos.12032)
- Unnikrishnan, Singh, Purohit. Evid Based Dent 2025;26(1):70-71. Meta-analysis, 940 participants (children and adults; RCTs and observational): behavioural therapy more effective than inhalational sedation for dental anxiety (SMD -0.87; 95% CI -1.29 to -0.45; I² 78%); in children SMD -0.64. PMID 39506128 — [Evid Based Dent 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41432-024-01081-9)
- Lu 2023 (20 RCTs): sedation drugs SMD -0.61; audio-visual distraction SMD -0.86; informational interventions not significant. PMID 35536282 — [Odontology 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10266-022-00711-x)
- Hoffmann B, et al. Aust Dent J 2022;67 Suppl 1:S3-S13. Systematic review of adult management strategies: psychotherapeutic behavioural strategies have "nil or negligible side effects"; pharmacological options (nitrous oxide, IV, oral sedation) "can have undesirable side effects, risks and contraindications" and "increase the cost and availability of dental treatment". PMID 35735746 — [Aust Dent J 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/adj.12926)
- Mahadevan et al. Front Dent Med 2026;7:1891238. Systematic review, 15 RCTs in implant surgery: behavioural methods suit mild-moderate anxiety; pharmacological sedation (notably dexmedetomidine) suits moderate-severe fear. PMID 42494375 — [Front Dent Med 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fdmed.2026.1891238)
- Thom A, Sartory G, Jöhren P. J Consult Clin Psychol 2000;68(3):378-87. Controlled trial, 50 dental phobics: one session of psychological treatment (stress management plus imaginal exposure) vs benzodiazepine 30 minutes before treatment (midazolam per MeSH terms) vs no anxiety treatment. Both active arms reduced anxiety during dental surgery vs control; the benzodiazepine arm relapsed afterwards while the psychological arm kept improving to 2 months; 70% of the psychological group continued dental treatment vs 20% (benzodiazepine) and 10% (control). PMID 10883554 — [J Consult Clin Psychol 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037//0022-006x.68.3.378)
- Jöhren P, et al. Br J Oral Maxillofac Surg 2000;38(6):612-616. Companion report (patient-preference allocation; 50 completers: 20 psychological, 20 oral midazolam, 10 control): both interventions lowered pre-operative fear; at 2 months the midazolam group returned to baseline; at 1 year "compliance and reduction of fear remained only in the psychologically treated group". PMID 11092778 — [BJOMS 2000](https://doi.org/10.1054/bjom.2000.0531)

### Inferences
- The evidence base sorts by goal: drugs for "get through today's root canal", psychological treatment for "stop avoiding dentistry". The two are complementary rather than competing.
- The Unnikrishnan meta-analysis mixes children and adults and observational designs with high heterogeneity; it should not be read as showing behavioural therapy beats nitrous oxide for adult single-visit state anxiety.
- Thom/Jöhren allocated partly by patient choice and had 50 completers; the direction of effect is consistent with other long-term studies (section 7), but the effect size is imprecise.

### Gaps
- No network meta-analysis comparing oral benzodiazepines, nitrous oxide, IV sedation and other agents against each other in adult dental patients was found.
- No head-to-head RCT of oral benzodiazepine vs nitrous oxide vs IV sedation with adequate reporting was found (Georgeno 2025 lacks numerical results in its abstract).
- No comparative study in an endodontic population.

## 6. Guidelines and regulation: ADA, ASA, state permits, AAE, monitoring, MRD rule, interactions, special populations

### Takeaway
The ADA guidelines were comprehensively revised (adopted October 2025, released April 20, 2026), superseding the 2016 edition: minimal sedation is still capped at the FDA maximum recommended dose for unmonitored home use; moderate sedation requires ACLS, continuous pulse oximetry, end-tidal CO2 monitoring and continuous ECG, and now supplemental oxygen. State rules, not ADA guidelines, are binding and vary widely (43 states require a moderate sedation permit). No AAE position statement on sedation was found.

### Cited Findings

ADA guidelines (current edition, read in full)
- Status: "Adopted by the ADA House of Delegates, October 2025"; released April 20, 2026 as "the first comprehensive revision in nearly a decade". Changes: updated ASA physical status and fasting recommendations, weight-based dosing documentation, BMI added to baseline vital signs, supplemental oxygen recommended for moderate sedation through GA, emergency drills. Contributors included AGD, AAPD, AAP, AAOMS, ADSA, ASA and ASDA — [ADA News, 20 April 2026](https://adanews.ada.org/ada-news/2026/april/ada-releases-updated-sedation-and-anesthesia-guidelines/)
- "Dentists must comply with their state laws, rules and/or regulations when providing sedation and anesthesia"; "Level of sedation is entirely independent of the route of administration" — [ADA 2025 use guidelines](https://www.ada.org/-/media/project/ada-organization/ada/ada-org/files/resources/library/oral-health-topics/ada_sedation_use_guidelines.pdf)
- Minimal sedation: "a minimally depressed level of consciousness, produced by a pharmacological method, that retains the patient's normal response to verbal command"; ventilatory and cardiovascular functions unaffected — [ADA 2025](https://www.ada.org/-/media/project/ada-organization/ada/ada-org/files/resources/library/oral-health-topics/ada_sedation_use_guidelines.pdf)
- MRD: "maximum FDA-recommended dose of a drug, as printed in FDA-approved labeling for unmonitored home use". Minimal sedation by the enteral route may use a drug "either singly or in divided doses ... not to exceed the maximum recommended dose (MRD)". "The administration of an enteral drug exceeding the maximum recommended dose for unmonitored home use during a single appointment is considered to be moderate sedation and the moderate sedation guidelines apply" — [ADA 2025](https://www.ada.org/-/media/project/ada-organization/ada/ada-org/files/resources/library/oral-health-topics/ada_sedation_use_guidelines.pdf)
- Routes: "enteral" includes oral, rectal and sublingual; intranasal is classed as parenteral — [ADA 2025](https://www.ada.org/-/media/project/ada-organization/ada/ada-org/files/resources/library/oral-health-topics/ada_sedation_use_guidelines.pdf)
- Moderate sedation definition: patients "respond purposefully to verbal commands, either alone or accompanied by light tactile stimulation. No interventions are required to maintain a patent airway" — [ADA 2025](https://www.ada.org/-/media/project/ada-organization/ada/ada-org/files/resources/library/oral-health-topics/ada_sedation_use_guidelines.pdf)
- Education: minimal sedation requires training per the ADA teaching guidelines (or moderate sedation training, or a CODA-accredited advanced program) plus BLS. Moderate sedation requires a comprehensive moderate sedation program or CODA-accredited advanced program, plus BLS and ACLS — [ADA 2025](https://www.ada.org/-/media/project/ada-organization/ada/ada-org/files/resources/library/oral-health-topics/ada_sedation_use_guidelines.pdf). The ADA moderate sedation teaching standard is at least 60 hours of instruction plus at least 20 individually managed patients (as reported by Simon 2025) — [J Patient Saf 2025](https://doi.org/10.1097/PTS.0000000000001320)
- Minimal sedation clinical requirements: review of history/medications (ASA III-IV "may require" physician consultation); baseline weight, height, BMI, BP, pulse, respiratory rate; one additional BLS-trained person; positive-pressure oxygen with bag-valve-mask and oral airways immediately available; monitoring "must include" level of consciousness, "Oxygen saturation by pulse oximetry", observation of chest excursions and respirations; BP and heart rate pre-op, post-op and intra-op as necessary; sedation record; emergency drills at least every six months — [ADA 2025](https://www.ada.org/-/media/project/ada-organization/ada/ada-org/files/resources/library/oral-health-topics/ada_sedation_use_guidelines.pdf)
- Moderate sedation clinical requirements: NPO status and fasting instructions per ASA (clear liquids 2 hours, light meal 6 hours, fried/fatty food 8 hours or more; modify for GLP-1 agonists); BMI assessment ("Patients with elevated BMI may be at increased risk for airway associated morbidity, particularly if in association with other factors such as obstructive sleep apnea"); supraglottic airways; equipment for IV or intraosseous access; supplemental oxygen throughout; continuous pulse oximetry; "The dentist must monitor ventilation and/or breathing by monitoring end-tidal CO2 unless precluded or invalidated by the nature of the patient, procedure or equipment"; continual BP and heart rate; "Continuous ECG monitoring unless precluded or invalidated"; time-oriented anesthesia record; the dentist must not leave the facility until discharge; longer observation after any reversal agent because "re-sedation may occur" — [ADA 2025](https://www.ada.org/-/media/project/ada-organization/ada/ada-org/files/resources/library/oral-health-topics/ada_sedation_use_guidelines.pdf)
- Rescue principle: practitioners "should be able to diagnose and manage the physiologic consequences (rescue) for patients whose level of sedation becomes deeper than initially intended" — [ADA 2025](https://www.ada.org/-/media/project/ada-organization/ada/ada-org/files/resources/library/oral-health-topics/ada_sedation_use_guidelines.pdf)
- 2016 edition (search-snippet level only): adopted by the House of Delegates October 2016; enteral dosing above the MRD in a single appointment is moderate sedation; use of more than one enteral drug, with or without nitrous oxide, also triggered the moderate sedation guidelines — [Kentucky Board of Dentistry copy of the 2016 guidelines](https://dentistry.ky.gov/Documents/ADA_Anesthesia_Use_Guidelines.pdf)

- Obstructive sleep apnea/obesity: ADA 2025 flags elevated BMI with OSA as increased airway risk and classifies BMI 30-40 as ASA II and BMI of 40 or more as ASA III — [ADA 2025](https://www.ada.org/-/media/project/ada-organization/ada/ada-org/files/resources/library/oral-health-topics/ada_sedation_use_guidelines.pdf). ### Inferences
- Applying the ADA MRD definition to the triazolam label: up to 0.5 mg total in an adult (0.25 mg in a geriatric patient) in a single appointment sits within minimal sedation; more than that is moderate sedation by definition, regardless of how awake the patient appears.
- The 2025 ADA definitions section no longer contains the 2016 language that more than one enteral drug automatically constitutes moderate sedation; it retains the caution that nitrous oxide combined with sedatives may produce any depth. I could not compare the two editions line by line, so this should be checked before being stated as a change. Many state rules still encode the older "single enteral drug with or without nitrous oxide" wording (26 states per Simon 2025).
- A resident should treat the state dental practice act as the operative rule and the ADA document as the floor most boards reference; ACLS for minimal sedation (12 states) and capnography mandates (27 states) are the commonest places states exceed or differ.
- US (ADA) and Scottish (SDCEP) guidance diverge on capnography and ECG for moderate/conscious sedation (required vs not required), reflecting SDCEP's single-drug, verbal-contact definition of conscious sedation.

### Gaps
- The 2016 ADA guideline full text could not be retrieved (state-board copies returned HTTP 404); the 2016 points above come from search-result snippets. The 2016 definitions of "incremental dosing" and "supplemental dosing" and its statement on children 12 and under were not verified.
- The ASA 2018 guideline full text was paywalled; only the citation and authoring organizations were verified. ## 7. Does sedation only mask anxiety for the visit, or reduce long-term dental fear and avoidance?

### Takeaway
A single dose of benzodiazepine helps the patient through that visit but does not durably reduce dental fear: in the head-to-head trials, fear returned to baseline within 2 months and only 20% continued care, versus 70% after brief psychological treatment. Evidence is more favourable for repeated, titrated nitrous oxide delivered within a supportive programme, where 5-year fear outcomes matched cognitive therapy in one RCT.

### Cited Findings
- Thom, Sartory, Jöhren 2000 (n = 50 dental phobics): benzodiazepine group relapsed after treatment; 20% continued dental treatment vs 70% after one-session psychological treatment and 10% of controls. PMID 10883554 — [J Consult Clin Psychol 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037//0022-006x.68.3.378)
- Jöhren et al. 2000: oral midazolam group "showed a return to baseline fear" at 2 months; at 1 year compliance and fear reduction "remained only in the psychologically treated group". PMID 11092778 — [BJOMS 2000](https://doi.org/10.1054/bjom.2000.0531)
- Hakeberg M, Berggren U, Carlsson SG. Scand J Dent Res 1990;98(1):53-9. 10-year follow-up, 14 dental phobics: regular dental care in 100% after systematic desensitization vs 63% after diazepam premedication; fear reduction greater with desensitization. PMID 1970194 — [Scand J Dent Res 1990](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0722.1990.tb00939.x)
- Hakeberg M, Berggren U, Carlsson SG, Gröndahl HG. Anesth Prog 1993;40(3):72-7. 10-year follow-up, 29 patients: behavioural therapy (n = 12), benzodiazepine premedication (n = 8), general anesthesia (n = 9). Regular attendance 91.6% after behavioural therapy vs 33.3% after GA (65.5% overall); Dental Anxiety Scale scores fell significantly except in GA patients and irregular attenders. PMID 7645792 — [Anesth Prog 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7645792/)
- Berggren U. J Dent Res 1986;65(6):874-6. Follow-up beyond 2 years of 84 of 99 patients: regular attendance significantly higher after broad-based behavioural therapy than after general anesthesia. PMID 3458737 — [J Dent Res 1986](https://doi.org/10.1177/00220345860650060101)
- Aartman IH, de Jongh A, Makkes PC, Hoogstraten J. Community Dent Oral Epidemiol 2000;28(6):435-42. Dutch dental fear clinic, 280 patients treated with behavioural management, nitrous oxide sedation or IV sedation: anxiety fell in all, but IV sedation patients showed less anxiety reduction than behavioural management patients post-treatment and at 1 year; 62% had visited a general dentist at follow-up; "a relatively large proportion of patients did not improve". Non-randomized. PMID 11106016 — [Community Dent Oral Epidemiol 2000](https://doi.org/10.1034/j.1600-0528.2000.028006435.x)
- Willumsen T, Vassend O. Acta Odontol Scand 2003;61(2):93-9. 5-year follow-up of the nitrous oxide vs cognitive therapy vs applied relaxation RCT (43 of 62 responded): all had attended a dentist; mean Corah Dental Anxiety Scale 10.4; no between-group differences; gains maintained from post-treatment; 7 participants (3 nitrous oxide, 2 cognitive therapy, 2 relaxation) had scores above 14; 81% rated the treatment useful. PMID 12790506 — [Acta Odontol Scand 2003](https://doi.org/10.1080/00016350310001442)
- Collado V, et al. J Clin Psychopharmacol 2006;26(5):474-81. 3-year prospective cohort, 543 uncooperative special-care patients (age 1-94, mean 17), 50% nitrous oxide premix over repeat visits: very uncooperative patients fell from 23% to 3.7% at first contact and from 22.3% to 8.5% at mask application between first and third sessions. PMID 16974188 — [J Clin Psychopharmacol 2006](https://doi.org/10.1097/01.jcp.0000236660.01039.eb)
- Coldwell SE, et al. J Anxiety Disord 2007;21(7):871-87. RCT, 144 subjects with dental injection phobia: systematic desensitization plus placebo vs alprazolam 0.5 or 0.75 mg. Alprazolam neither sped nor impaired progress; no state-dependent learning; dental fear reduced similarly in all groups at 1 year. "No advantage was found to combining alprazolam with systematic desensitization". PMID 17320345 — [J Anxiety Disord 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.janxdis.2007.01.001)
- Steenen 2022: perioperative propranolol aimed at blocking fear-memory reconsolidation did not reduce trait dental anxiety at 1 month. PMID 35264990 — [Front Psychiatry 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2022.842353)
- Steenen 2024 meta-analysis: benzodiazepines are supported only for state anxiety; trait anxiety and phobia respond to CBT. PMID 38945067 — [J Anxiety Disord 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.janxdis.2024.102891)
- Wide Boman 2013: CBT/behavioural therapy improves acceptance of dental treatment more than general anesthesia (low quality evidence). PMID 23659254 — [Eur J Oral Sci 2013](https://doi.org/10.1111/eos.12032)
- Perrott 2003 (OMS office anesthesia cohort): 80.3% reported anxiety before the procedure; afterwards 61.2% reported no anxiety about future operations. Uncontrolled, immediate post-procedure self-report. PMID 12966471 — [JOMS 2003](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0278-2391(03)00668-2)

### Inferences
- The pattern is consistent across designs: the deeper and more amnestic the technique (GA, IV sedation, single-dose benzodiazepine), the less the patient learns that dental treatment is tolerable, and the worse long-term attendance looks relative to behavioural treatment. Nitrous oxide, which leaves memory and awareness largely intact, behaves more like a graded-exposure aid when used repeatedly.
- Benzodiazepine amnesia is a plausible mechanism for the lack of carry-over, but the Coldwell 2007 trial found no evidence of state-dependent learning, so the mechanism is not established.
- For the endodontic resident: pharmacological anxiolysis is appropriate to complete urgent treatment, but a fearful patient who needs ongoing care should also be offered or referred for behavioural treatment; sedation alone should not be expected to fix avoidance.

### Gaps
- All long-term comparative studies are small (14-65 randomized or followed), European, and mostly from the 1980s-2000s; several used patient-preference allocation. No modern adequately powered RCT compares oral sedation with CBT on long-term attendance.
- No long-term data exist for endodontic patients specifically (for example, whether sedation for an emergency root canal changes completion of the definitive restoration or recall attendance).
- No long-term follow-up data were found for oral triazolam or alprazolam given alone (without a behavioural programme).
