# Non-pharmacological interventions for dental anxiety in adults (priority: endodontic / root canal treatment)

> **How these notes were compiled.** Searches were run in PubMed (MCP connector plus NCBI E-utilities) on 26 Nov 2026. Every numeric result below is taken from the PubMed abstract of the cited paper unless marked otherwise. Full texts were read for only two open-access reviews (Shih 2024 and Wolf 2022); the full texts of Steenen 2024, Troian-Michel 2023, Burghardt 2018, Khan 2016 and Bleyen 2025 were paywalled and could **not** be read, so per-intervention pooled estimates that are absent from their abstracts are listed under Gaps. One review that is not PubMed-indexed (Allan 2018, acupuncture) is cited from its institutional-repository record.
> **Scope.** Adults only unless labelled "PEDIATRIC". Drugs, sedation and local-anaesthetic technique are excluded (pharmacological comparators are mentioned only where they were the control arm of a non-pharmacological trial).
> **Labelling.** In each section, findings are split into **(a) Endodontic-specific** and **(b) Extrapolated (general dentistry / oral surgery / periodontics / implants)**.
> **Abbreviations.** RCT = randomized controlled trial; RCTx = root canal treatment; STAI-S = State-Trait Anxiety Inventory, state form; MDAS = Modified Dental Anxiety Scale; (C)DAS = Corah Dental Anxiety Scale; DFS = Dental Fear Survey; VAS-A = visual analogue scale for anxiety; HR = heart rate; SBP/DBP = systolic/diastolic blood pressure; HRV = heart-rate variability; GSR = galvanic skin response; SMD = standardized mean difference; RoB = risk of bias; IANB = inferior alveolar nerve block; SIP = symptomatic irreversible pulpitis.

## Background facts needed to interpret every section (how anxious are endodontic patients, and why it matters)

### Takeaway
Anxiety before root canal treatment is on average moderate, is driven largely by expected pain that exceeds experienced pain, falls after treatment, and is associated with more intra-operative pain; the injection and the drilling/access phases are the peak-arousal moments.

### Cited Findings
- Systematic review of 36 articles; meta-analysis of 18 articles (n=1,989) gave a pre-treatment anxiety rating of 39 (SD 9) on a normalized 100-point scale; 4 articles (n=232) gave a post-treatment rating of 27 (SD 5), a 30% reduction; RCTx-associated anxiety ranked high among dental treatments, "often close to oral surgery" — Khan S, Hamedy R, Lei Y, Ogawa RS, White SN. J Endod 2016;42(12):1726-1736. [PMID 27776881](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27776881/), doi:10.1016/j.joen.2016.08.007
- Systematic review/meta-analysis of 23 studies (very-low to low certainty): RCTx was more psychologically stressful for patients than routine restorative or cleaning procedures; comparison with extractions inconclusive — Huynh R, Peters CI, Zafar S, Peters OA. Eur J Oral Sci 2023;131(4):e12941. [PMID 37357152](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37357152/)
- 333 adult endodontic patients: 43% anticipated high pain but only 22% experienced high pain and 18% high unpleasantness; women and younger patients anticipated more; authors note "patients perceive each ET experience as new" — Watkins CA, Logan HL, Kirchner HL. J Am Dent Assoc 2002;133(1):45-54. [PMID 11811742](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11811742/)
- 97 patients undergoing extraction or RCTx: patients generally expected more pain than they experienced; those high in both dental anxiety and pain sensitivity did **not** disconfirm their exaggerated expectations — Klages U et al. Eur J Oral Sci 2004;112(6):477-83. [PMID 15560829](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15560829/)
- 92 patients: anxiety and expected pain ranked extraction > root canal > filling > calculus removal — Eli I et al. J Endod 1997;23(11):694-7. [PMID 9587311](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9587311/)
- Cross-sectional, n=180 RCTx patients: anxiety correlated with intra-operative pain (R=0.406); anxiety associated with moderate/intense intra-operative pain, OR 4.0 (95% CI 1.7-9.3) — Murillo-Benítez M et al. Int Endod J 2020;53(4):447-454. [PMID 31691312](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31691312/)
- Systematic review of 4 observational studies (n=471): dental anxiety associated with pre- and intra-operative endodontic pain; GRADE low/very low — Farias ZBBM et al. Clin Oral Investig 2023;27(10):5709-5718. [PMID 37526740](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37526740/)
- Scoping review of 48 studies: procedural and post-procedural endodontic pain consistently associated with anxiety, pain expectations, depression and pain beliefs; "desire for control" and "perceptions of dentists" were among 12 constructs studied — Sadr A et al. Br J Pain 2025;20(2). [PMID 41458166](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41458166/)
- 594 patients who had or were planned for RCTx: cognitive conditioning and parental pathways were the primary reported origins of fear; women more influenced by indirect (informative, vicarious, verbal-threat) pathways — Carter AE, Carter G, George R. Int Endod J 2015;48(6):528-32. [PMID 25066388](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25066388/)
- Observational, 76 adults in single-visit RCTx: HR peaked during local anaesthetic administration (93.9 ± 8.2) and drilling (94.7 ± 8.07); prior negative dental experience associated with higher anxiety (p=0.018) — Pandey P et al. J Contemp Dent Pract 2025;26(3):244-249. [PMID 40444553](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40444553/)
- Small 1993 endodontic trial (n=30) recording GSR and pulse at 8 treatment stages "reinforced the belief that local anesthetic injection is the most anxiety producing aspect of endodontic treatment" — Morse DR, Chow E. Int J Psychosom 1993;40(1-4):68-76. [PMID 8070990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8070990/)

### Inferences
- Because pre-treatment anxiety is "moderate" on average (39/100) and drops ~30% simply by completing treatment, any uncontrolled pre/post comparison in an endodontic trial will show "significant improvement"; only between-group differences are interpretable. Several endodontic trials below report only within-group change.
- The expected-vs-experienced pain gap (43% vs 22%) is the logical target for expectation-management/information interventions.

### Gaps
- Khan 2016 full text not read; which patient/treatment factors moderate anxiety is described in the abstract only as "limited data".

## 1. Communication and information: preoperative information (verbal, written, video; procedural vs. sensory), dentist communication style, empathy, rapport, tell-show-do, stop signals / perceived control, expectation management

### Takeaway
Endodontic-specific evidence is thin and mixed: one large experiment shows that positively framed information about pain lowers *fear of pain* from RCTx, and one large RCT in RCTx patients shows that a sensory-focus instruction lowers pain only in patients with high desired/low felt control, whereas adding a preoperative information video or leaflet has **not** outperformed ordinary verbal explanation in endodontic RCTs. The best-replicated, cheapest communicative manoeuvre (from general practice, not endodontics) is to screen with the MDAS and have the dentist openly acknowledge the score; in oral surgery, graphic audiovisual procedural information can *increase* anxiety.

### Cited Findings

**(a) Endodontic-specific**
- **Positive information about pain.** n=437 randomly allocated to read one of five informative paragraphs taken from patient brochures, then complete dental-anxiety and fear-of-dental-pain questionnaires; those who read the paragraph with positive information about pain during endodontic treatment "indicated they were less fearful of pain associated with ET". Authors' implication: "patients should be accurately informed about pain associated with ET" — van Wijk AJ, Hoogstraten J. Int Endod J 2006;39(5):384-8. [PMID 16640637](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16640637/), doi:10.1111/j.1365-2591.2006.01090.x. (Design caveat from abstract: outcome is a questionnaire immediately after reading; the abstract calls participants "subjects"/"a large sample" and does not state they were patients about to undergo RCTx; effect size not given in abstract.)
- **Sensory focus vs. procedural information during root canal therapy.** RCT with four audiotaped conditions before RCTx (sensory focus; sensory focus + procedural information; procedural information; no intervention); patients classified by desired and felt control. Sensory focus significantly reduced sensory pain intensity only in the "high desire for control / low felt control" subgroup, immediately (F(1,303)=13.37, p≤.001) and at 1 week (F(1,303)=14.30, p≤.001); "adding procedural information to the instructions to focus on sensation did not increase the pain-reducing effects" — Logan HL, Baron RS, Kohout F. Psychosom Med 1995;57(5):475-84. [PMID 8552739](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8552739/). (Outcome is pain, not an anxiety scale.)
- **Video information vs. routine verbal information (Türkiye).** RCT in single-canal teeth with asymptomatic irreversible pulpitis/pre-prosthetic RCTx; visual video information immediately pre-treatment vs. verbal information; anxiety scales + wrist GSR during treatment. Anxiety fell pre-to-post in the video group "but this decrease was not significant between the groups (p>0.05)"; GSR did not differ between groups (p>0.05). Authors nevertheless conclude the video "is effective" — Anatürk Ş, Özkan HD, Saral İP, Çakar T. J Oral Rehabil 2025;52(11):1933-1944. [PMID 40506422](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40506422/). (Abstract internally inconsistent on sample size: "Patients (n=120)" in Methods vs. "60 endodontic treatments on 60 patients" in Results.)
- **Informative preoperative video vs. music vs. control (UAE/multinational).** RCT, n=90 (30/arm), RCTx and retreatment; CDAS and vitals at four time points. Only the music arm showed reductions in SBP, HR (P<.001) and DBP (P=.003) at rubber-dam removal vs. baseline; authors conclude music reduced anxiety "compared to informative preoperative videos and no distraction technique". Both music (P<.001) and video (P=.003) groups had lower post-operative VAS pain — Abdulhameed SA, Nagendrababu V, Gorduysus MO, Dummer PMH et al. Quintessence Int 2024;55(8):670-680. [PMID 39078172](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39078172/)
- **AI-generated (ChatGPT) education vs. verbal explanation + pamphlet.** n=100 patients undergoing restorative or endodontic procedures; Likert anxiety 2.63 vs 3.38 (p<0.001) favouring AI materials — Islam S. BMC Oral Health 2025;25(1):689. [PMID 40335999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40335999/). (Single-author; abstract calls the design both "cross-sectional, comparative" and "randomized"; non-validated single-item anxiety rating; mixed procedures.)
- **Paper information leaflet added to verbal information (Czech private clinic; mixed procedures including endodontic treatment).** Single-blind RCT, n=167 (82 vs 85); no effect on pre-procedure anxiety (β=−0.11, P=0.54) or post-procedure well-being; authors: "verbal communication alone may be sufficient" — Plachý R et al. J Int Soc Prev Community Dent 2025;15(1):69-79. [PMID 40151554](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40151554/). (Study-specific, non-validated questionnaires.)
- **Information before RCTx or extraction (Israel).** 42 patients; half received information about the forthcoming procedure; no difference in threat-word recall; abstract concludes "preliminary information given prior to dentistry surgical procedures does not decrease anxiety" — Bodner E, Iancu I. Isr J Psychiatry Relat Sci 2013;50(1):61-6. [PMID 24029113](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24029113/)
- **Perceived control via an electronic chairside signalling device ("Touch N' Tell").** 60 anxious patients (MDAS-selected) undergoing endodontic therapy, two groups; "significant decrease in the mean anxiety levels" (post-op MDAS) with the device — Singh H, Meshram G, Warhadpande M, Kapoor P. J Conserv Dent 2012;15(1):51-5. [PMID 22368336](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22368336/). (Abstract does not state randomization or blinding; device designed and patented by an author.)
- **Perceived control via a mobile app during endodontic treatment.** RCT, n=46; app designed on the "perceived control" principle vs. no app; salivary cortisol pre-op, post-anaesthesia, post-op; significant between-group difference in cortisol (Mann-Whitney p<.05), with stepwise reductions only in the app group (p<.01) — Nalci G, Alaçam T, Altunkaynak B. J Oral Rehabil 2022;49(5):535-540. [PMID 35148428](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35148428/). (No self-report anxiety scale reported in abstract.)

**(b) Extrapolated**
- **Desired vs. felt control.** Three studies: patients with high desire for control but low felt control showed particularly high dental distress before and immediately after treatment and remembered treatment as more aversive 1 week later — Logan HL, Baron RS, Keeley K, Law A, Stein S. Health Psychol 1991;10(5):352-9. [PMID 1935871](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1935871/)
- **Signalling device, early contrary data.** Adults given a signalling device showed *more* GSR arousal during high-stress conditions than controls — Corah NL, Bissell GD, Illig SJ. J Dent Res 1978;57(1):74-6. [PMID 277503](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/277503/). In an 80-patient RCT of amalgam restorations (control, relaxation, perceived control, active distraction; 20 each), "both relaxation and distraction were found to be effective in reducing patient discomfort" (perceived control is not listed as effective) — Corah NL, Gale EN, Illig SJ. J Dent Res 1979;58(4):1347-51. [PMID 372266](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/372266/)
- **Dentist behaviours (observational).** 250 adults rated 25 dentist behaviours after treatment; "the dentist's explicit dedication to prevent pain was the most important dentist behavior" for anxiety reduction, with friendliness, working quickly, being calm and giving moral support as auxiliary behaviours; empathy and communicativeness correlated with satisfaction. Authors note an experimental (causal) test is needed — Corah NL et al. J Am Dent Assoc 1988;116(1):73-6. [PMID 3422668](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3422668/)
- **Telling the dentist the patient's MDAS score.** RCT, 8 general practitioners, 119 highly anxious patients (MDAS ≥19 or 5 on any item): informing the dentist of the score, with the patient involved, produced a greater reduction in STAI-S (F[1,119]=8.74) — Dailey YM, Humphris GM, Lennon MA. J Dent Res 2002;81(5):319-22. [PMID 12097444](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12097444/). Three subsequent randomized studies in UK primary care: presenting the MDAS sheet to the dentist "was effective in reducing patient state anxiety when leaving the surgery" in the first two, and the third suggested a more durable effect when the dentist responded openly to the fears expressed — Hally J, Freeman R, Yuan S, Humphris G. Patient Educ Couns 2017;100(11):2102-2105. [PMID 28619274](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28619274/)
- **Screening itself is safe.** RCT, n=1,004 across 18 practices: completing the MDAS before seeing the dentist did not raise state anxiety (mean difference 0.35, 95% CI −0.18 to 0.89; p=0.2) — Humphris GM, Clarke HM, Freeman R. Br Dent J 2006;201(1):33-5. [PMID 16829885](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16829885/)
- **Iatrosedation (structured calming explanation/education) vs. music vs. control.** RCT, n=60, crown preparation; HR and verbal rating scale lowest with iatrosedation, then music, highest in control — Lal A et al. Psych J 2024;13(4):566-574. [PMID 38363639](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38363639/)
- **Structured communication model as part of an active comparator.** RCT, n=96 (85% MDAS ≥19), general practice: dentist-administered CBT vs. dental treatment using the "Four Habits" communication model + midazolam; MDAS fell by a mean 6.6 with no between-arm difference — Hauge MS et al. Eur J Oral Sci 2021;129(4):e12794. [PMID 33960536](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33960536/) (communication was confounded with midazolam, so its independent effect cannot be isolated).
- **Video information in third-molar surgery — no benefit.** Systematic review, 9 RCTs (5 pooled, n=529): informative videos did not reduce pre- or post-operative dental anxiety vs. verbal and/or written information (MDAS, STAI-S) — Souza MRF et al. Oral Maxillofac Surg 2024;28(1):15-27. [PMID 36525143](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36525143/)
- **Audiovisual procedural information can increase anxiety.** RCT, n=90 third-molar patients: audiovisual group had *higher* preoperative STAI-S (P=.002) and MDAS (P=.009) than the verbal group — Akomolafe AG et al. J Oral Maxillofac Surg 2023;81(3):329-336. [PMID 36493800](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36493800/). RCT, n=300 single-implant patients: audiovisual information produced more anxiety and fear than verbal information (STAI, MDAS, DFS; P<.001) — Camacho-Alonso F et al. Clin Implant Dent Relat Res 2019;21(6):1189-1198. [PMID 31639265](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31639265/)
- **Meta-analytic verdict on video information.** Steenen et al. 2024 (173 RCTs) state that evidence "does not support ... video information" for reducing state anxiety — Steenen SA, Linke F, van Westrhenen R, de Jongh A. J Anxiety Disord 2024;105:102891. [PMID 38945067](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38945067/)
- **Live display of the operating-microscope video.** Randomized crossover, 130 male adults, bilateral mandibular-molar restorations (not RCTx): greater anxiety reduction in the session with the live DOM video (mean difference 2.61 ± 1.08 vs 1.64 ± 1.00; P<0.05) — Sayed A et al. J Conserv Dent Endod 2026;29(2):185-192. [PMID 41756793](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41756793/). (Men only; customized scales; restorative, not endodontic.)
- **Narrative/critical reviews.** Gordon et al. conclude that "cognitive techniques, relaxation, and techniques to increase patients' sense of control over dental care are also efficacious but perform best when combined with repeated, graduated exposure" — Gordon D, Heimberg RG, Tellez M, Ismail AI. J Anxiety Disord 2013;27(4):365-78. [PMID 23746494](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23746494/). Armfield & Heaton describe a range "from good communication and establishing rapport to the use of systematic desensitization and hypnosis" and judge there is "an acceptable evidence base for several non-pharmacological anxiety management practices" — Armfield JM, Heaton LJ. Aust Dent J 2013;58(4):390-407. [PMID 24320894](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24320894/)

### Inferences
- In endodontics, *what* is said about pain (accurate, positively framed expectation-setting; a sensory-focus instruction for control-seeking patients) has more support than *how much* procedural detail is delivered or the medium used (video, leaflet). Procedural videos have repeatedly failed to beat a normal verbal explanation, and realistic surgical footage has raised anxiety in oral-surgery/implant RCTs.
- The Logan data imply a simple triage question ("How much do you like to be in control at the dentist, and how much control do you feel you have?") identifies the patients who benefit most from control-enhancing strategies (stop signal, sensory focus). This is an inference from a subgroup effect, not a tested screening protocol.
- Stop-signal/perceived-control evidence is inconsistent across eras (Corah 1978/1979 null or adverse on GSR; Singh 2012 and Nalci 2022 positive but small, weakly controlled, and one with an inventor conflict). It is low-risk and ethically expected, but should not be presented as strongly evidence-based.
- MDAS screening with explicit acknowledgement by the clinician is the best-replicated chairside communication intervention, but it has not been tested in an endodontic specialty setting.

### Gaps
- **Tell-show-do in adults:** no adult RCT (endodontic or otherwise) was found; all tell-show-do trials retrieved were pediatric (e.g., pulpotomy studies) and are not summarized here.
- **Empathy/rapport:** no RCT manipulating dentist empathy or communication style in adults was found; evidence is observational (Corah 1988) or bundled with other components (Hauge 2021).
- **Iatrosedation in endodontics:** no endodontic trial found (only crown preparation, Lal 2024).
- van Wijk & Hoogstraten 2006 and Logan 1995: effect sizes and exact participant populations could not be extracted beyond the abstracts.
- The frequently cited Law, Logan & Baron (1994) stress-inoculation/desire-for-control paper and Thrash et al. stop-signal work were not located in PubMed during this search and are therefore not cited.

## 2. Music and audio (music listening, binaural beats) during root canal treatment — anxiety scales, heart rate, blood pressure, cortisol

### Takeaway
Music is the most-studied non-pharmacological intervention in endodontics, but the endodontic evidence is graded **very low certainty**: about half of the endodontic RCTs show a benefit on self-reported anxiety or vital signs and half are null, with cortisol unaffected in both trials that measured it. Across dentistry, pooled effects on anxiety are small-to-moderate (SMD about −0.5 in adults) with very high heterogeneity, patient-selected music performs best, and the most rigorous meta-analysis (Steenen 2024) concludes that the evidence does *not* support music for state anxiety.

### Cited Findings

**(a) Endodontic-specific**
- **Systematic review of music during endodontic treatment.** Six databases to April 2022; 5 RCTs included; RoB 2 "low to high"; descriptive synthesis favoured music for state anxiety, HR and BP; GRADE **very low**; conclusion: clinicians "may consider playing background music" as a cost-effective option — Troian-Michel CH, Tietz L, Mendes AT, Duarte PHM et al. Clin Oral Investig 2023;27(11):6321-6332. [PMID 37728616](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37728616/), doi:10.1007/s00784-023-05247-0
- **Lai et al. 2008 (Taiwan).** Investigator-blind RCT, n=44 adults (22/22); sedative music via headphones throughout RCTx vs. headphones without music; HR, BP, finger temperature every 10 min; anxiety pre and post. Music group: significant decrease in anxiety score and increase in finger temperature over time vs. control; reported effect sizes 0.34 (state anxiety) and 0.14 (finger temperature); groups differed in gender at baseline — Lai HL, Hwang MJ, Chen CJ, Chang KF et al. J Clin Nurs 2008;17(19):2654-60. [PMID 18808630](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18808630/), doi:10.1111/j.1365-2702.2008.02350.x. Independent commentary: music "does not significantly affect blood pressure and heart rate over the procedure" — Newton JT. Evid Based Dent 2009;10(1):15. [PMID 19322222](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19322222/)
- **Di Nasso et al. 2016 (Italy).** RCT, n=100; 432 Hz music vs. no music during endodontic treatment; baseline anxiety by CDAS; SBP, DBP and HR "decreased considering the overall period (during and after the canal therapy)" in the music group (P<.05) — Di Nasso L, Nizzardo A, Pace R, Pierleoni F et al. J Endod 2016;42(9):1338-43. [PMID 27430941](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27430941/), doi:10.1016/j.joen.2016.05.015. (Outcomes are vital signs; abstract reports no post-intervention anxiety scale and no sham/blinding.)
- **Santana et al. 2017 (Brazil).** n=50 adults 18-40 y, upper anterior teeth with irreversible pulpitis or necrosis; randomized to music or no music; "no difference regarding salivary cortisol and anxiety score"; music group showed HR decrease and HRV changes interpreted as enhanced autonomic modulation — Santana MD, Martiniano EC, Monteiro LR, Valenti VE et al. Evid Based Complement Alternat Med 2017;2017:4847869. [PMID 28182118](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28182118/)
- **Wazzan et al. 2022 (UAE).** RCT, n=46 (23/23), urgent endodontic access and pulp extirpation; slow instrumental music vs. none; salivary cortisol, BP, HR and body temperature were lower with music but differences "did not reach to statistical significance" — Wazzan M et al. Acta Biomed 2022;92(6):e2021393. [PMID 35075079](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35075079/)
- **Abdulhameed et al. 2024.** RCT, n=90 (RCTx and retreatment): music reduced SBP, HR (P<.001) and DBP (P=.003) at end of treatment vs. baseline; better than informative video or control (details in Section 1) — Quintessence Int 2024;55(8):670-680. [PMID 39078172](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39078172/)
- **Mousavi et al. 2025 (Iran).** Single-blind, parallel RCT, n=72 adults with irreversible pulpitis; 4 arms (control; 432 Hz music; lavender aromatherapy via humidifier; music + aromatherapy); "no statistically significant differences compared to the control group (p>0.05)" in SBP, DBP or HR — Mousavi SA et al. Clin Exp Dent Res 2025;11(3):e70156. [PMID 40497523](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40497523/). (Roughly 18 per arm; no anxiety questionnaire reported in abstract.)
- **Binaural beats vs. 432 Hz music vs. white noise (Pakistan).** Double-blind RCT (patients and assessors), n=99 (33/arm), mean age 28; VAS-A before/after local anaesthesia and after 10 min of listening; both binaural beats and 432 Hz music significantly reduced preoperative anxiety vs. baseline and vs. the white-noise control (p<0.05), with no difference between them — Zaki H, Ahmed S, Rehman Qazi FU, Fatima N, Ali YS. J Pak Med Assoc 2025;75(4):540-544. [PMID 40269359](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40269359/). (The only endodontic audio trial with an attention-matched sound control and blinding; outcome is preoperative VAS only.)
- **Music vs. etizolam vs. placebo in highly anxious SIP patients (India).** RCT, n=110, mandibular molars, MDAS-assessed anxiety; self-selected music by headphones throughout the visit; IANB success (no/mild pain on Heft-Parker VAS during treatment) significantly higher with music than with etizolam 0.5 mg or placebo (P<.01) — Singla R, Gill GS, Sheoran H, Jain N et al. Anesth Prog 2025;72(4):224-231. [PMID 41360139](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41360139/). (Outcome is anaesthetic success/pain, not anxiety score; success percentages not in abstract.)
- **Combined aromatherapy + music.** Systematic review of 24 controlled studies (2019-2026): "Two lithotripsy trials and the adult endodontic study did not show a clear benefit"; combinations seldom outperformed either component alone — Zhai H et al. Front Psychol 2026;17:1955987. [PMID 42923310](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42923310/)
- **Historical audio-visual entrainment.** n=30 (10/arm) private endodontic patients: "brain wave synchronizer" ± alpha relaxation tape added to calming words reduced anxiety (GSR, pulse, questionnaire) more than calming words alone — Morse DR, Chow E. Int J Psychosom 1993;40(1-4):68-76. [PMID 8070990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8070990/)
- **RETRACTED — do not cite as evidence.** A meta-analysis of "sensory art therapies" (music, audiovisual, yoga, aromatherapy, VR) for RCTx anxiety (13 RCTs) — Guo S et al. PLoS One 2025;20(9):e0328917, [PMID 40892734](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40892734/) — was retracted by the PLOS One Editors (PLoS One 2026;21(8):e0357077, [PMID 42658835](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42658835/)).

**(b) Extrapolated**
- **Meta-analysis restricted to invasive procedures.** 24 controlled trials, n=1,830; music vs. control during invasive procedures under local anaesthesia: SMD −0.50 (95% CI −0.80 to −0.21; I²=83%); adults SMD −0.51 (95% CI −0.80 to −0.22; I²=71%), children SMD −0.47 (NS); patient-selected music SMD −1.01 (95% CI −1.76 to −0.26; I²=92%) vs. researcher-selected SMD −0.24 (95% CI −0.44 to −0.04; I²=37%); classical music in adults SMD −0.69 (p=0.009). GRADE **low**; 9 trials low and 15 moderate quality on modified Jadad, none high; almost none double-blind. Only one included trial (Lai 2008) was an RCTx trial — Shih KC et al. J Clin Med 2024;13(21):6491. [PMID 39518630](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39518630/); full text [PMC11546414](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11546414/)
- **Meta-analysis including Chinese databases.** Music reduced HR (WMD −7.33 bpm, 95% CI −10.07 to −4.58; I²=81.2%), SBP (WMD −6.10 mmHg; I²=85.6%), DBP (WMD −4.29 mmHg, 95% CI −6.57 to −2.02; I²=79.7%), anxiety scores (WMD −9.04; I²=19.6%) and pain scores (WMD −7.64, 95% CI −9.43 to −5.85) — Tan K, Liu H, Huang S, Li C. Medicina (Kaunas) 2023;59(2):209. [PMID 36837411](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36837411/). (Some confidence limits in the abstract are printed with apparent sign errors, e.g. "−9.25, 2.95" and "−11.45, 6.63".)
- **Meta-analysis of music before dental treatment.** 14 RCTs; overall effect favoured music (p=0.005); stronger in children — López-Valverde N et al. Front Psychiatry 2024;15:1352817. [PMID 38463434](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38463434/)
- **Network meta-analysis, tooth extraction.** 11 RCTs; relaxing music SMD −0.64 (95% CI −1.04 to −0.25), VR SMD −0.54 (95% CI −1.08 to −0.02), audiovisual SMD −0.34 (95% CI −0.97 to 0.33; NS); music ranked first — Hao T, Pang J, Liu Q, Xin P. BMC Oral Health 2023;23(1):684. [PMID 37735362](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37735362/)
- **Contrary high-level synthesis.** Steenen et al. 2024: evidence "does not support ... music" for reducing adult state anxiety during dental treatment — J Anxiety Disord 2024;105:102891. [PMID 38945067](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38945067/)
- **Music is weaker than active relaxation and weak in the highly anxious.** RCT, n=90 dentally anxious patients: brief relaxation was significantly superior to music distraction on STAI-S; music "did not have a clinically relevant effect" in highly anxious subjects — Lahmann C et al. J Am Dent Assoc 2008;139(3):317-24. [PMID 18310736](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18310736/)
- **Earlier JBI review.** 7 studies (598 patients; only 2 RCTs): "enough evidence to suggest that adult patients may benefit from a procedural music-listening program"; two of the five adult studies found relaxation more effective than music — Moola S, Pearson A, Hagger C. JBI Libr Syst Rev 2011;9(18):588-630. [PMID 27819961](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27819961/)
- **Pre-procedure music and autonomic activity.** RCT, n=86 adults awaiting impacted-tooth extraction under IV sedation: in fearful patients, music lowered LF/HF ratio change (Cohen's d = −0.75 and −0.71 at two time points) and VAS anxiety — Miyata K et al. JDR Clin Trans Res 2016;1(2):153-162. [PMID 30931796](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30931796/)
- **Music vs. diazepam.** Unblinded randomized split-mouth crossover, n=30, third-molar surgery: preoperative STAI-S 33.6 ± 5.98 (music) vs 37.4 ± 4.81 (diazepam), P<.001; diazepam produced more pronounced reductions in physiological measures — Amini E et al. J Oral Maxillofac Surg 2026;84(9):1471-1479. [PMID 42202875](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42202875/)
- **Binaural beats, all dentistry.** Systematic review/meta-analysis of 9 studies (children and adults): significant reduction in dental anxiety vs. controls; "high risk of selection and detection bias"; pain results inconclusive — Shukla AD et al. Int J Clin Pediatr Dent 2025;18(8):1023-1030. [PMID 40989974](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40989974/)

### Inferences
- Endodontic music trials split roughly evenly: positive on at least one outcome (Lai 2008; Di Nasso 2016; Abdulhameed 2024; Zaki 2025; Singla 2025 for anaesthetic success) vs. null on primary outcomes (Santana 2017; Wazzan 2022; Mousavi 2025). Null trials tend to be the ones that relied on cortisol/vital signs with ≤25 patients per arm, so they are underpowered rather than convincingly negative.
- Physiological "benefits" in pooled analyses (HR about −7 bpm, SBP about −6 mmHg) are of doubtful clinical importance and come from highly heterogeneous, mostly unblinded trials; cortisol was unchanged in both endodontic trials that measured it.
- The dose/selection signal (patient-selected SMD −1.01 vs. researcher-selected −0.24) is the most actionable chairside detail: let the patient choose the music and use headphones.
- Music is essentially free and harmless, which is why very-low-certainty evidence still translates into a reasonable "offer it" recommendation; it should not be relied on as the sole strategy for highly anxious or phobic patients (Lahmann 2008; Steenen 2024).

### Gaps
- Identity, per-study risk of bias and numeric results of the five RCTs in Troian-Michel 2023 could not be extracted (paywalled).
- Steenen 2024's pooled SMD/CI for music is not in the abstract and was not verified.
- No endodontic trial has tested music in patients selected for *high* dental anxiety with an anxiety scale as primary outcome (Singla 2025 selected anxious patients but measured anaesthetic success).
- 432 Hz tuning has never been compared with standard-tuned (440 Hz) versions of the same music in an endodontic trial, so any tuning-specific claim is untested.

## 3. Virtual reality (VR) and audiovisual distraction in adult endodontic / dental patients

### Takeaway
There is almost no endodontic-specific VR evidence (one uncontrolled two-arm comparison and one non-immersive video RCT); in adult dentistry generally, meta-analyses conflict — from a non-significant pooled effect (SMD −0.35) to a moderate one (SMD −0.69) — and the effect in adult oral surgery is small (SMD about −0.26 to −0.34). VR works more convincingly in children than in adults.

### Cited Findings

**(a) Endodontic-specific**
- **Relaxing video before treatment.** RCT, n=160 adult endodontic patients (80/80); the experimental group watched a self-chosen relaxing internet video during a 10-minute preoperative interval; VAS anxiety fell significantly more in the video group (P<.05); no between-group difference in SBP, DBP or HR — Craveiro MA, Caldeira CL. J Endod 2020;46(7):909-914. [PMID 32389383](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32389383/), doi:10.1016/j.joen.2020.03.024. (Control group simply waited; unblinded; outcome is pre-operative only.)
- **VR relaxation vs. audio-guided relaxation (Indiana University).** n=58 patients needing non-surgical RCTx, randomized to a Meta Quest 2 immersive relaxation experience or an earphone-delivered guided relaxation (diaphragmatic breathing + body scan). Both arms: STAI-State decreased (p<0.001 for both), VAS decreased (p<0.001 for both), HR decreased (p=0.019 audio; p=0.026 VR); no differences between arms; BP mixed — Mintz C, Spolnik KJ, Mody DP, Ehrlich Y et al. Front Dent Med 2025;6:1625411. [PMID 41334019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41334019/). (No untreated control, so natural pre-to-post decline cannot be separated from treatment effect; authors acknowledge small cohort.)
- **Scoping review.** 46 studies (2000-2024) of VR for dental anxiety across pediatrics, oral surgery, periodontics and endodontics; 31 reported significant reductions; mild adverse effects (nausea, claustrophobia); "research is still limited, particularly in adults ... further research is needed to fully evaluate the role of VR in endodontic treatments" — Bleyen S, Mooens K, Jancic L, Lambrechts P, Meschi N, Pedano MS. J Dent 2025;160:105905. [PMID 40505807](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40505807/)
- **VR vs. nitrous oxide for injection (Ohio State endodontic research group; volunteers, not RCTx).** Crossover, 132 female subjects, maxillary lateral incisor infiltration: N2O lowered salivary cortisol significantly more than VR (P=.0089); no difference in needle-insertion or deposition pain — Edmunds K, Drum M, Fowler S, Nusstein J, Reader A. Anesth Prog 2025;72(3):143-150. [PMID 40925631](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40925631/)

**(b) Extrapolated**
- **All-ages dental meta-analysis.** 27 RCTs; VR reduced anxiety in children (SMD −1.44, 95% CI −2.24 to −0.63) "but not adults (SMD −0.35, 95% CI −1.11 to 0.4)"; pain reduced in adults (SMD −0.59, 95% CI −1.187 to −0.001); high heterogeneity; evidence low to moderate — Nezhad HM, Ashourioun A, Sadeghdaghighi A. Community Dent Health 2024;41(4):248-255. [PMID 39535423](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39535423/)
- **Adult-only meta-analysis (2026).** 12 RCTs in the review, 9 in meta-analysis (1,272 patients): VR reduced anxiety (SMD −0.69, 95% CI −1.16 to −0.22), pain (MD −0.17) and HR (MD −5.11 bpm, 95% CI −9.83 to −0.38); no effect on BP or SpO2; RoB 1 tool; "high heterogeneity and methodological limitations" — Cao W et al. J Stomatol Oral Maxillofac Surg 2026;127(6):103013. [PMID 42801915](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42801915/). (A corrigendum was subsequently published, [PMID 42994395](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42994395/); its content was not reviewed.)
- **Adult oral surgery.** 9 RCTs: intra-operative anxiety SMD −0.26 (95% CI −0.48 to −0.03); pain SMD −0.67 (95% CI −1.08 to −0.26) — Liu X et al. Oral Surg Oral Med Oral Pathol Oral Radiol 2026;141(4):450-464. [PMID 41407647](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41407647/)
- **Third-molar surgery.** 6 studies (3 pooled): post-intervention anxiety −0.34 (95% CI −0.49 to −0.19), but the VR groups were already less anxious at baseline (−0.28); "small reduction in anxiety"; pain uncertain — Falci SGM et al. Oral Maxillofac Surg 2024;28(3):1095-1104. [PMID 38834821](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38834821/)
- **Network meta-analysis (extractions).** VR SMD −0.54 (95% CI −1.08 to −0.02); non-immersive audiovisual distraction SMD −0.34 (NS) — Hao T et al. BMC Oral Health 2023;23(1):684. [PMID 37735362](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37735362/)
- **Contrary high-level synthesis.** Steenen et al. 2024: evidence does not support "virtual reality distraction" for adult state anxiety — [PMID 38945067](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38945067/)
- **Brief preoperative VR relaxation.** RCT, 255 analysed adults in public primary dental care: a 1-3.5 min 360° nature video reduced MDAS total more than sitting quietly (β = −0.75, P<.001), with a larger effect in women (β = −1.08) — Lahti S, Suominen A, Freeman R, Lähteenoja T, Humphris G. JDR Clin Trans Res 2020;5(4):312-318. [PMID 31962052](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31962052/)
- **Technology-based interventions for moderate-severe dental anxiety.** 7 RCTs (video modelling, computerized CBT, VR exposure, music/audiovisual distraction); 6 showed benefit vs. inactive controls; 4 of 7 at high RoB; no meta-analysis possible — Gujjar KR, van Wijk A, Kumar R, de Jongh A. J Evid Based Dent Pract 2019;19(2):140-155. [PMID 31326046](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31326046/)
- **Publication-integrity note.** The first VR dental meta-analysis (López-Valverde et al., J Clin Med 2020;9:1025, [PMID 32260538](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32260538/)) was retracted and republished as J Clin Med 2020;9(10):3086 ([PMID 32987885](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32987885/)); the republished version reports significant effects in children only (anxiety SMD −1.75; pain SMD −1.46).

### Inferences
- For an endodontic resident, VR is currently an *unproven* adjunct in RCTx: there is no sham- or no-treatment-controlled endodontic RCT with an immersive headset. The single endodontic VR study showed it was no better than a cheap audio relaxation track.
- A headset under a rubber dam and operating microscope is practically awkward (operator access, head position), and no trial has addressed feasibility during microscope-assisted endodontics; pre-operative use in the waiting room/chair (Lahti 2020; Craveiro 2020 with ordinary video) is the better-supported use case.
- Divergent adult meta-analytic estimates (−0.35 NS vs. −0.69) reflect different trial sets and high heterogeneity; the oral-surgery-only estimates (−0.26 to −0.34) are probably the most realistic expectation for an invasive adult procedure.

### Gaps
- No RCT of VR distraction *during* adult root canal treatment with a no-VR control was found.
- No data on cybersickness/claustrophobia rates specifically in endodontic patients under rubber dam.
- The Arthur et al. 2025 "panoramic meta-analysis" of extended-reality for procedural anxiety (J Med Internet Res 27:e58086, [PMID 39778203](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39778203/)) was identified but not reviewed.

## 4. Aromatherapy (lavender, orange/citrus) in waiting rooms and during treatment

### Takeaway
Ambient lavender or orange scent has reduced *state* anxiety in several waiting-room trials, but results are inconsistent (a large replication was null; a 2024 cluster trial found an effect only in women and trait-anxious patients), blinding is impossible, and the dedicated systematic review grades the evidence low to very low. Endodontic-specific aromatherapy trials (three from two groups) are conflicting.

### Cited Findings

**(a) Endodontic-specific**
- **Lavender-scented operatory, SIP, IANB.** Block-randomized trial, n=46 (22 no fragrance vs 24 lavender via candle warmer): baseline MDAS and VAS similar; "statistically significant difference in mean VAS (P=.000) and MDAS (P=.001) during access opening"; authors conclude lavender alleviated anxiety and increased IANB success — Jadhav GR, Mittal P. Quintessence Int 2020;51(10):864-870. [PMID 32901242](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32901242/)
- **Lavender vs. rose vs. no aromatherapy, SIP, IANB (same group).** Block-randomized, n=78 (26/arm), ultrasonic diffuser: MDAS and VAS during access opening significantly lower with lavender (p=0.03, p=0.04) and rose (p=0.02, p=0.03) than control; no difference between scents — Mittal P, Jadhav GR et al. Heliyon 2024;10(14):e34514. [PMID 39113957](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39113957/). (MDAS is a trait-type scale re-administered mid-procedure; allocation was by operatory.)
- **Null trial.** 4-arm single-blind RCT, n=72, irreversible pulpitis: lavender via humidifier, alone or with 432 Hz music, produced no significant differences in SBP, DBP or HR vs. control (p>0.05) — Mousavi SA et al. Clin Exp Dent Res 2025;11(3):e70156. [PMID 40497523](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40497523/)
- **Oral (ingested) lavender — borderline scope.** Double-blind RCT, n=64 SIP patients: 20 drops of lavender extract in 250 ml water vs. plain water, anxiety questionnaire 60 min later; greater reduction with lavender (p=0.001) — Razavian H, Mazaheri M, Shiri A. Eur Endod J 2025;10(1):66-72. [PMID 40145478](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40145478/). (This is an ingested herbal anxiolytic rather than aromatherapy, so arguably pharmacological; the abstract's results sentence contains a wording error, naming the control group twice.)

**(b) Extrapolated**
- **Systematic review.** 11 RCTs + 6 quasi-randomized trials to Jan 2020; lavender most used, then orange; RoB high or unclear in all RCTs (performance and detection bias most common); aromatherapy "might reduce" anxiety-related physiological and psychometric measures vs. no aromatherapy and was comparable to music; GRADE **low to very low**; no meta-analysis because of heterogeneity — Cai H, Xi P, Zhong L, Chen J, Liang X. Oral Dis 2021;27(4):829-847. [PMID 32267044](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32267044/)
- **Conflicting meta-analysis.** 6 trials; pooled mean difference −3.36 (95% CI −3.77 to −2.95; I²=1%); authors claim "high certainty" — Purohit A et al. J Dent Anesth Pain Med 2021;21(4):311-319. [PMID 34395898](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34395898/). This certainty rating conflicts with Cai 2021 (low to very low) and with Steenen 2024, which states the evidence does not support aromatherapy for adult state anxiety — [PMID 38945067](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38945067/). A 2026 review of 126 studies rated aromatherapy "moderate-certainty" — Wawrzyniak A et al. Public Health Rev 2026;47:1609316. [PMID 42601922](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42601922/)
- **Waiting-room orange.** n=72: women exposed to ambient orange odour had lower state anxiety, better mood and more calmness; no effect reported in men — Lehrner J et al. Physiol Behav 2000;71(1-2):83-6. [PMID 11134689](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11134689/)
- **Waiting-room orange and lavender.** n=200, four groups (orange, lavender, music, control): both odours reduced anxiety and improved mood vs. control — Lehrner J et al. Physiol Behav 2005;86(1-2):92-5. [PMID 16095639](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16095639/)
- **Failed replication.** n=219 in three large clinics: orange (n=81) and apple (n=69) odours had "no effect on the anticipatory anxiety or mood" — Toet A et al. Chemosens Percept 2010;3(3-4):182-189. [PMID 21125006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21125006/)
- **Cluster RCT, lavender (King's College London).** n=340: lavender group had significantly lower current anxiety on STAI-6 (F(1,338)=74.69, P<0.001) but MDAS was unaffected (F=2.17, P>0.05): "anxiety about future dental visits seems to be unaffected" — Kritsidima M, Newton T, Asimakopoulou K. Community Dent Oral Epidemiol 2010;38(1):83-7. [PMID 19968674](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19968674/)
- **Ten-clinic comparison (India).** n=597: lavender in the reception area associated with lower MDAS scores than control clinics — Zabirunnisa M et al. J Res Pharm Pract 2014;3(3):100-3. [PMID 25328900](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25328900/)
- **AROMA_dent cluster-randomized single-blind study (Germany).** 4 practices, n=486, four essential-oil blends vs. water: STAI-S 38.4 ± 10.5 (oils) vs 40.7 ± 11.6 (control); effect robust only in women (n=296, p=0.044) and, post hoc, in patients with trait anxiety ≥42 (46.8 vs 51.1, p=0.0379) — Czakert J et al. Sci Rep 2024;14(1):14143. [PMID 38898054](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38898054/)
- **During surgery.** RCT, n=56 moderately/highly anxious third-molar patients: ambient orange fragrance lowered mean BP, pulse and respiratory rate during surgery (P<.05) — Hasheminia D et al. J Oral Maxillofac Surg 2014;72(9):1671-6. [PMID 24985959](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24985959/). RCT, n=126 dentally anxious third-molar patients: lavender inhalation produced significant post-operative BP changes; 79.4% enjoyed the scent — Karan NB. Physiol Behav 2019;211:112676. [PMID 31505191](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31505191/)

### Inferences
- The between-group difference in the best-controlled recent trial (about 2 STAI-S points overall; about 4 points in trait-anxious patients) is small relative to the scale's SD of about 10-12, i.e. roughly SMD 0.2-0.4 — a clinically modest effect.
- Aromatherapy appears to act on momentary (state) anxiety in the waiting room, not on dental trait anxiety or avoidance (Kritsidima 2010), and a recurrent sex difference (effects mainly in women: Lehrner 2000; Czakert 2024) suggests expectancy or scent-preference effects.
- The two positive endodontic IANB trials come from one research group and use operatory-level allocation; with the null Iranian trial, the endodontic evidence should be regarded as unconfirmed.

### Gaps
- No endodontic aromatherapy trial used a placebo odour, assessed blinding success, or reported adverse effects (headache, scent aversion, staff exposure).
- Steenen 2024's pooled estimate for aromatherapy was not accessible.
- No trial has tested aromatherapy in patients with diagnosed dental phobia.

## 5. Relaxation techniques: diaphragmatic/paced breathing, progressive muscle relaxation (PMR), guided imagery, mindfulness, biofeedback

### Takeaway
Brief, clinician-taught relaxation has consistent small-RCT support in general dentistry (and outperformed music in the one head-to-head RCT, especially in highly anxious patients), but endodontic-specific data are limited to very small or non-randomized yoga/breathing studies and an uncontrolled audio-relaxation arm.

### Cited Findings

**(a) Endodontic-specific**
- **Yogic relaxation vs. alprazolam vs. placebo.** Randomized placebo-controlled study, n=30 RCTx patients with baseline VAS-A >4 (10/arm); STAI-S 10 min before and after RCTx significantly lower with yogic relaxation than alprazolam (0.25/0.5 mg) or placebo (P<0.0001); global efficacy "excellent" in 9/10 (yoga), 2/10 (alprazolam), 1/10 (placebo) — Verma MR et al. J Conserv Dent 2021;24(2):209-213. [PMID 34759592](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34759592/). (10 per arm; a 30-minute supervised session; an unusually large contrast with an active benzodiazepine; the yoga arm cannot be blinded to patients.)
- **Pranayama + marma relaxation before RCTx.** 50 participants, yoga therapy (n=25) vs. self-relaxation (n=25); lower single-item Likert anxiety (P<0.001), SBP, HR and derived cardiovascular indices in the yoga group — Thiruvalluvan A et al. Int J Yoga 2021;14(2):146-151. [PMID 34188387](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34188387/). (Self-described "cross sectional study design"; randomization not stated; non-validated outcome.)
- **Audio-guided brief relaxation (diaphragmatic breathing + body scan).** In 58 RCTx patients, the audio arm showed pre-to-post decreases in STAI-State (p<0.001), VAS (p<0.001) and HR (p=0.019), equivalent to VR; no untreated control — Mintz C et al. Front Dent Med 2025;6:1625411. [PMID 41334019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41334019/)
- **Historical pilot work (non-randomized).** 34 private endodontic patients managed with local anaesthesia, N2O, hypnosis and/or meditation: "meditation and hypnosis, either alone or in combination with local anesthesia, were most effective in anxiety reduction" by questionnaire and salivary changes — Morse DR et al. Oral Surg Oral Med Oral Pathol 1981;52(3):308-13. [PMID 7027112](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7027112/)

**(b) Extrapolated**
- **Brief relaxation vs. music vs. control.** RCT, n=90 dentally anxious patients; both active arms reduced STAI-S; brief relaxation significantly superior to music and "particularly effective in highly anxious subjects" — Lahmann C et al. J Am Dent Assoc 2008;139(3):317-24. [PMID 18310736](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18310736/)
- **PMR.** RCT, n=68 periodontal patients with dental anxiety score ≥13 (34/arm): 20 min PMR + 15 min oral health education weekly for 4 weeks vs. education only; dental anxiety change −3.82 vs −0.89 at 4 weeks and −4.22 vs −0.28 at 3 months; also lower BP, pulse and salivary cortisol — Park ES, Yim HW, Lee KS. Eur J Oral Sci 2019;127(1):45-51. [PMID 30430667](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30430667/)
- **Relaxation and distraction vs. perceived control.** RCT, n=80, amalgam restorations: relaxation and active distraction effective in reducing discomfort — Corah NL, Gale EN, Illig SJ. J Dent Res 1979;58(4):1347-51. [PMID 372266](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/372266/)
- **Paced-breathing biofeedback before IANB.** Exploratory study, n=81 (40 device, 41 control): RESPeRATE respiratory-rate biofeedback significantly reduced negative feelings about the overall injection experience (VAS) — Morarend QA et al. Appl Psychophysiol Biofeedback 2011;36(2):63-70. [PMID 21365307](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21365307/)
- **Respiratory biofeedback during extraction.** Single-blind RCT, n=60 (30/30): lower respiratory rate (−2.75 bpm, P=.03), lower post-extraction STAI-State (−2.04, P=.015), larger pulse reduction (−3.61 bpm, P=.030); no effect on MDAS or DFS — Wong NSM et al. Int Dent J 2025;75(5):100882. [PMID 40614467](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40614467/)
- **Mindfulness meditation during implant surgery.** RCT: improved STAI-S and lower HR, BP, cortisol and bispectral index vs. control — Turer OU et al. Sci Rep 2023;13(1):21686. [PMID 38066232](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38066232/). (Sample size not stated in abstract.)
- **Applied relaxation, long-term.** 5-year follow-up of an RCT (43/62 responders) comparing N2O sedation, cognitive therapy and applied relaxation for dental fear: all had attended a dentist; mean CDAS 10.4 (SD 4.1), no between-group differences — Willumsen T, Vassend O. Acta Odontol Scand 2003;61(2):93-9. [PMID 12790506](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12790506/)
- **Stress ball — null.** Randomized split-mouth, n=20, scaling/root planing under local anaesthesia: no difference in GSR, STAI or MDAS change with a stress ball — Torres-Gomez J et al. J Clin Exp Dent 2021;13(4):e350-e356. [PMID 33841733](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33841733/)
- **Meta-analytic context.** In Burghardt et al. (29 RCTs, 2,886 adults) relaxation was one of five intervention classes; overall effect on mental distress g=0.58 (95% CI 0.39-0.76), with "no significant differences ... between various types of non-pharmacological interventions" — Burghardt S et al. J Dent 2018;69:22-31. [PMID 29154798](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29154798/)

### Inferences
- A 5-10-minute scripted relaxation (slow diaphragmatic breathing ± body scan/PMR), delivered live or by audio before the injection, is the relaxation format with the most transferable evidence and is cheaper than VR while performing equivalently in the one endodontic comparison.
- Biofeedback effects are confined to state measures during the visit (STAI-S, respiratory parameters), not dental trait anxiety (Wong 2025) — consistent with these being *coping* tools rather than treatments for dental fear.
- The endodontic yoga trials are too small and too weakly designed to support the claim that relaxation outperforms a benzodiazepine.

### Gaps
- No adequately powered, controlled endodontic RCT of paced breathing, PMR, guided imagery or mindfulness was found.
- No guided-imagery-only adult dental RCT was retrieved by the searches used.
- A protocol for app-based mindfulness before dental surgery exists (Hoetger C et al. JMIR Res Protoc 2026;15:e95932, [PMID 43056719](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43056719/)) but no results are available.

## 6. Hypnosis in dentistry / endodontics

### Takeaway
Hypnosis is the only non-pharmacological intervention that Steenen et al. (2024) rate as supported by moderate-certainty evidence for state anxiety during oral surgery, but the effect is small (SMD −0.31), a dedicated hypnosis meta-analysis found inconsistent results, and it underperforms CBT for dental phobia. There are no modern endodontic RCTs — only 1970s-80s case series and pilot studies.

### Cited Findings

**(a) Endodontic-specific**
- Case reports of using a meditative state for hypnotic induction in endodontic practice — Morse DR. Oral Surg Oral Med Oral Pathol 1976;41(5):664-72. [PMID 775373](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/775373/)
- Non-randomized pilot, 34 endodontic patients: meditation and hypnosis (alone or with local anaesthesia) "most effective in anxiety reduction" — Morse DR et al. 1981. [PMID 7027112](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7027112/). Follow-up, 29 endodontic clinic patients treated by students: "hypnosis and nitrous oxide-oxygen were significantly more effective (p<0.05) than local anesthesia in anxiety reduction as measured by salivary changes and questionnaires" — Morse DR et al. J Human Stress 1981;7(3):19-26. [PMID 7276547](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7276547/)

**(b) Extrapolated**
- **Steenen 2024.** For state anxiety during oral surgery, "moderate-certainty evidence supports employing hypnosis (SMD=−0.31, 95% CI [−0.56, −0.05])" — [PMID 38945067](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38945067/)
- **Burghardt 2018.** 29 RCTs (hypnosis, enhanced information, relaxation, music, CBT/distraction) in adults under regional or general anaesthesia: mental distress g=0.58 (95% CI 0.39-0.76); pain g=0.00 (95% CI −0.28 to 0.28); analgesic use g=0.26 (NS); "largest effects being shown for hypnosis", although subgroup differences between intervention types were not statistically significant; effects were larger in low-RoB studies — [PMID 29154798](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29154798/); commentary: Heaton LJ. J Evid Based Dent Pract 2018;18(2):165-167. [PMID 29747799](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29747799/)
- **Dedicated hypnosis review.** 19 adult trials (1979-2021), 15 rated good and 4 fair quality; only 5 could be pooled, "showing contrasting results for the application of hypnosis"; the review itself notes that CBT "showed the most evidence" — Wolf TG, Schläppi S, Benz CI, Campus G. Brain Sci 2022;12(5):521. [PMID 35624907](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35624907/); full text [PMC9138388](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9138388/) (the full text gives no single pooled estimate; individual effect sizes ranged from −4.30 to 6.20).
- **Narrative summary of 12 reviews/meta-analyses.** Supports hypnosis for local anaesthesia, extraction and dental anxiety in adults and children; literature "remains somewhat fragmented" owing to varied techniques — Thibault A, Rainville P, Rei N. Am J Clin Hypn 2025;67(3):227-243. [PMID 40079847](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40079847/)
- **Adjunctive hypnosis for extraction.** Controlled trial, n=102: lower anxiety *during* treatment but not after; >90% of patients had positive attitudes to hypnosis — Glaesmer H, Geupel H, Haak R. Patient Educ Couns 2015;98(9):1112-5. [PMID 26054452](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26054452/)
- **Dental phobia, practice-based comparison.** 137 phobic patients (77 completers): standardized hypnosis had significantly more premature termination than CBT; intent-to-treat analysis showed significant improvement only after CBT; "CBT is the treatment of choice in dental phobia" — Wannemueller A et al. Psychother Psychosom 2011;80(3):159-65. [PMID 21372624](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21372624/)
- **Long-term outcome.** Hypnotherapy vs. group therapy vs. systematic desensitization for extreme dental anxiety: at 3 years, 54.5%, 69.6% and 65.5% respectively maintained regular dental care (vs 46.1% of untreated reference patients) — Moore R, Brødsgaard I, Abrahamsen R. Eur J Oral Sci 2002;110(4):287-95. [PMID 12206590](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12206590/)

### Inferences
- Hypnosis is best positioned as an in-session adjunct for state anxiety delivered by a trained clinician; it should not be presented as a treatment for dental phobia, where it has higher dropout and weaker durability than CBT.
- "Moderate certainty" in Steenen 2024 applies to oral surgery; applying it to RCTx is an extrapolation.

### Gaps
- No RCT of hypnosis or hypnotic communication during contemporary root canal treatment was found.
- Burghardt 2018's hypnosis-subgroup effect size was not available from the abstract.
- The Cochrane review of hypnosis in dentistry covers children only and has been withdrawn (Al-Harasi S et al. Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2017;6:CD007154, [PMID 28632910](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28632910/)).

## 7. Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) and exposure-based therapy for dental anxiety/phobia, including brief, single-session, dentist-delivered and computer/online formats

### Takeaway
CBT/exposure has the strongest and most durable evidence of any non-pharmacological approach — it is the only one that reduces *trait* dental anxiety and avoidance (moderate certainty; SMD about −0.65) and it works in one-session, dentist-delivered and online formats — but none of the trials were conducted in endodontic patients, and it is a between-visits treatment rather than a chairside technique.

### Cited Findings

**(a) Endodontic-specific**
- No CBT or exposure-therapy trial conducted specifically in endodontic patients was found in PubMed (searches combining endodontic/root canal terms with CBT, exposure, desensitization and phobia).

**(b) Extrapolated**
- **Meta-analysis of behavioural interventions.** 38 of 80 studies pooled: self-reported anxiety ES 1.8 (95% CI 1.6-1.8); attendance ES 1.4 at 6 months and 1.2 at 6 months-4 years; "mean long-term attendance (>4 years after treatment) is 77%"; extensive heterogeneity — Kvale G, Berggren U, Milgrom P. Community Dent Oral Epidemiol 2004;32(4):250-64. [PMID 15239776](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15239776/). (Largely pre-post effect sizes from older trials, which inflates magnitude.)
- **Systematic review of RCTs.** 10 RCT publications in adults with validated dental anxiety or phobia: CBT/behavioural therapy reduced DAS by a mean difference of −2.7 vs. control; some support that it improves acceptance of dental treatment more than general anaesthesia; both rated **low quality of evidence** — Wide Boman U, Carlsson V, Westin M, Hakeberg M. Eur J Oral Sci 2013;121(3 Pt 2):225-34. [PMID 23659254](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23659254/)
- **Critical review.** 22 randomized trials (1974-2012): "CBT delivered in a variety of formats, including one-session treatment, has the most evidence for its efficacy" — Gordon D, Heimberg RG, Tellez M, Ismail AI. J Anxiety Disord 2013;27(4):365-78. [PMID 23746494](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23746494/)
- **Largest current meta-analysis.** 173 RCTs, 67 in 14 pooled analyses: trait anxiety — CBT SMD −0.65 (95% CI −1.06 to −0.24), moderate certainty; dental phobia — psychotherapy SMD −0.48 (95% CI −0.72 to −0.24) and CBT SMD −0.43 (95% CI −0.68 to −0.17), low-to-moderate certainty; VR exposure therapy not supported; for *state* anxiety, psychotherapy evidence "inconclusive". Authors: "Clinicians should ensure that interventions match their purpose — managing acute emotions during treatment, or alleviating chronic anxiety and avoidance tendencies" — Steenen SA et al. J Anxiety Disord 2024;105:102891. [PMID 38945067](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38945067/)
- **One vs. five sessions.** RCT, n=40 DSM-IV dental phobics (mean 11.4 years' avoidance): one-session and five-session exposure equally effective at reducing avoidance; five-session lower on anxiety scales post-treatment but equal at 1 year; 77% sought dental care in the follow-up year — Haukebø K et al. J Behav Ther Exp Psychiatry 2008;39(3):381-90. [PMID 18005932](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18005932/)
- **Single-session cognitive restructuring.** RCT, n=52 phobic patients: one ≤1-hour session reduced negative cognitions and dental trait anxiety vs. information or waiting list; advantage over information not maintained at 1 year; "repeated exposure to the dental situation seems necessary" — de Jongh A et al. Behav Res Ther 1995;33(8):947-54. [PMID 7487854](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7487854/)
- **One-session psychological treatment vs. benzodiazepine.** Controlled trial, n=50 phobic patients: both reduced anxiety during surgery vs. no treatment; benzodiazepine patients relapsed afterwards while psychological-treatment patients kept improving; 70% vs 20% vs 10% continued dental treatment — Thom A, Sartory G, Jöhren P. J Consult Clin Psychol 2000;68(3):378-87. [PMID 10883554](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10883554/)
- **Brief CBT in a private dental clinic.** RCT, n=104 (53 immediate vs 51 waiting list): dental fear reduction d=1.5-2.2 vs d=0.3-0.4; maintained at 2 years in those followed — Spindler H et al. J Public Health Dent 2015;75(1):64-73. [PMID 25234823](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25234823/)
- **Dentist-delivered CBT in general practice.** RCT, n=96: MDAS −6.6 on average, no difference from Four Habits communication + midazolam — Hauge MS et al. Eur J Oral Sci 2021;129(4):e12794. [PMID 33960536](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33960536/). At 1 year (52/82 completers responding): MDAS reduction 7.8 in both arms (Cohen's d 1.2 and 1.4); 74% and 80% continued dental treatment — Eur J Oral Sci 2022;130(4):e12872. [PMID 35569119](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35569119/)
- **King's College London CBT service (service evaluation, not an RCT).** 130 patients (99 women; mean age 39.9); 77% scored in the phobic range on MDAS; injections and the drill were the highest-scoring items; 36.9% had high general anxiety, 12.3% clinically significant depression, 12% suicidal ideation; **79% went on to have dental treatment without sedation** and 6% with sedation; mean of five CBT appointments before treatment — Kani E, Asimakopoulou K, Daly B, Hare J, Lewis J, Scambler S, Scott S, Newton JT. Br Dent J 2015;219(10):501-6. [PMID 26611310](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26611310/)
- **Computerized CBT.** RCT, N=151 highly anxious adult dental patients (74 immediate vs 77 wait-list): significantly greater improvement in dental anxiety, fear, avoidance and phobia severity at 1 month; fewer still met phobia criteria — Tellez M et al. J Dent Res 2015;94(9 Suppl):174S-80S. [PMID 26202996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26202996/)
- **Online CBT, 12-month RCT.** N=499 highly anxious adults in a dental-school setting, 3 arms (psychology-staff-assisted n=162; dental-staff-assisted n=167; control n=170): MDAS significantly lower in both intervention arms vs. control; dental-staff-assisted delivery equivalent to psychology-staff delivery — Tellez M, Dunne EM, Konneker E, Zhao H, Ismail AI. Community Dent Oral Epidemiol 2025;53(5):543-555. [PMID 40514778](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40514778/)
- **Computerized desensitization for injection fear (CARL).** Multicentre dentist-blind RCT, 8 US sites: greater reduction in general and injection-specific anxiety vs. pamphlet (p<.001); 35.3% vs 17.6% opted to receive an injection (not significant) — Heaton LJ, Leroux BG, Ruff PA, Coldwell SE. J Dent Res 2013;92(7 Suppl):37S-42S. [PMID 23690352](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23690352/)
- **VR exposure therapy.** Single-blind RCT, n=30 dental phobics: VRET reduced VAS-A by 44.4, MDAS by 7.1 and DFS by 21.2 vs. essentially no change with an information pamphlet, through 6 months — Gujjar KR, van Wijk A, Kumar R, de Jongh A. J Anxiety Disord 2019;62:100-108. [PMID 30717830](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30717830/). Conflicts with Steenen 2024, whose pooled analyses do "not support" VRET for trait anxiety or phobia — [PMID 38945067](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38945067/)
- **Trauma-focused therapy (EMDR).** RCT, n=31 DSM-IV-TR dental phobics vs. waitlist: d=2.52 (DAS) and 1.87 (DFS), maintained at 12 months; 83.3% in regular dental treatment at 1 year — Doering S et al. Eur J Oral Sci 2013;121(6):584-93. [PMID 24206075](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24206075/)

### Inferences
- For the patient who has avoided care for years and presents for RCTx, chairside distraction is unlikely to be sufficient; the evidence-based pathway is brief CBT/exposure (one to five sessions), which in the KCL service enabled about four in five phobic patients to be treated without sedation.
- Online/computerized CBT delivered with dental-staff support (Tellez 2025) is the most scalable format for a specialty practice or residency clinic; it can be assigned between the consultation and treatment visits. This workflow has not been tested in endodontics, where treatment is often urgent (symptomatic pulpitis), limiting lead time.
- Because injections and the drill are the dominant fears (Kani 2015) and are also the peak-arousal phases of RCTx (Morse 1993; Pandey 2025), injection-focused desensitization (e.g., CARL) is conceptually well matched to endodontic patients.

### Gaps
- No Cochrane review of psychological interventions for adult dental anxiety was identified (a PubMed title search of the Cochrane Database for dental anxiety/fear/phobia returned no records); the highest-level syntheses are Wide Boman 2013 and Steenen 2024.
- No trial has evaluated CBT, one-session exposure or online CBT in patients specifically referred for endodontic treatment, nor examined whether prior CBT changes intra-operative endodontic pain or anaesthetic success.
- Most CBT trials use wait-list or information controls, which inflate effect sizes relative to attention-matched controls.

## 8. Acupuncture / acupressure / auricular acupuncture

### Takeaway
Two sham-controlled RCTs show auricular acupuncture reduces pre-treatment state anxiety (about 3-4 STAI points more than sham in the larger trial) and small crossover trials suggest equivalence to midazolam for third-molar surgery, but the trials are few and extraction-based, one sham-controlled body-acupuncture trial was null, and both meta-analyses agree that acupuncture does not reliably beat sham (Allan 2018: large effect vs. no treatment, non-significant vs. sham; Steenen 2024: not supported). There are no endodontic anxiety trials.

### Cited Findings

**(a) Endodontic-specific**
- Only narrative reviews were found — Kumar G et al. "Acupuncture for Management of Endodontic Emergencies: a Review." J Acupunct Meridian Stud 2023;16(1):1-10. [PMID 36804816](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36804816/); and "Acupuncture as a Complementary Therapy in Conservative and Endodontic Procedures: A Narrative Review." Cureus 2024;16(12):e75768. [PMID 39822472](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39822472/). No endodontic RCT with an anxiety outcome was identified.

**(b) Extrapolated**
- **Karst et al. 2007.** RCT, n=67 extraction patients: auricular acupuncture vs. placebo acupuncture vs. intranasal midazolam, with a no-treatment comparison; auricular acupuncture and midazolam groups were significantly less anxious at 30 min than the placebo-acupuncture group (STAI X1; P=0.012 and <0.001); dentist-rated compliance improved with both (P=0.032, 0.049); "similarly effective" — Karst M et al. Anesth Analg 2007;104(2):295-300. [PMID 17242083](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17242083/)
- **Michalek-Sauberer et al. 2012.** Patient-blinded RCT, n=182: STAI-state fell from 54.7 ± 10.8 to 46.9 ± 10.4 with auricular acupuncture, from 51.9 ± 10.2 to 48.4 ± 10.0 with sham points, and rose from 51.0 to 54.0 with no intervention; baseline-adjusted reduction −7.3 (95% CI −9.0 to −5.6) vs −3.7 (95% CI −5.4 to −1.9) for sham (p=0.008) — Michalek-Sauberer A et al. Clin Oral Investig 2012;16(6):1517-22. [PMID 22219023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22219023/)
- **Auriculotherapy vs. midazolam 15 mg.** Double-blind randomized crossover, n=30, bilateral third molars: "anxiolytic effect equivalent to the midazolam effect" with fewer adverse effects; patient preference not different (53.3% vs 46.7%) — Dellovo AG et al. Int J Oral Maxillofac Surg 2019;48(5):669-674. [PMID 30442551](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30442551/)
- **Null sham-controlled trial.** Split-mouth, triple-blind, n=16, third molars: no difference between acupuncture and placebo acupuncture in preoperative anxiety (STAI, VAS), pain or trismus; less oedema with acupuncture — Armond ACV et al. Int J Oral Maxillofac Surg 2019;48(2):274-281. [PMID 30139712](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30139712/)
- **Dedicated meta-analysis (not PubMed-indexed).** First systematic review/meta-analysis of RCTs of acupuncture for anxiety in dental patients; six trials (reported in press coverage as about 800 patients); vs. no-intervention controls, mean difference −8.43 (95% CI −11.90 to −5.00), described as clinically relevant; vs. placebo/sham acupuncture, −1.54 (95% CI −4.73 to 1.64), not significant; authors suggest context effects — Allan FK, Peckham EJ, Liu J, Dietz KC, Zhang T, Arakaki A, MacPherson H. Eur J Integr Med 2018;20:22-35. doi:10.1016/j.eujim.2018.03.008; [White Rose repository record](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/130332). (Figures taken from the repository abstract via web search; the full text and the scale on which the mean difference is expressed were not checked.)
- **Pooled verdict.** Steenen 2024: evidence does not support acupuncture for adult state anxiety — [PMID 38945067](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38945067/). Contrasting integrative review: acupuncture/acupressure among interventions "reducing anxiety in over 70% of experiments" across medical and dental procedures (vote counting; authors caution about publication bias, small samples and lack of placebo controls) — Weisfeld CC et al. J Altern Complement Med 2021;27(9):717-726. [PMID 34076531](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34076531/)

### Inferences
- The sham arm in Michalek-Sauberer 2012 improved by about half as much as true auricular acupuncture, indicating a substantial expectancy/attention component; the specific effect (about 3.6 STAI points) is modest.
- Auricular acupuncture requires training, about 20-30 minutes of lead time and needle acceptance — a questionable fit for needle-fearful endodontic patients, whose dominant fear is the injection.

### Gaps
- Allan et al. 2018 is not indexed in PubMed; its numbers above come from the repository abstract only, and the trial count/patient total (six trials, about 800 patients) comes from press summaries — treat as partially verified.
- No adult acupressure RCT for dental anxiety was found (acupressure trials retrieved were pediatric).
- Steenen 2024's pooled estimate for acupuncture was not accessible.

## 9. Environmental and operational factors: waiting time, appointment timing, waiting-room design, sensory-adapted environments, weighted blankets, operatory sights and sounds, rubber dam, single- vs. multiple-visit treatment

### Takeaway
This is the least-evidenced domain: beyond ambient scent (Section 4), adult data consist of small autonomic-outcome trials of deep-pressure input during third-molar extraction, two small noise-masking studies with partly contradictory results, and a single low-quality endodontic study suggesting less anxiety with single-visit RCTx. No trials were found on appointment timing, waiting time, or rubber dam and anxiety in adults.

### Cited Findings

**(a) Endodontic-specific**
- **Single- vs. multiple-visit RCTx.** 60 patients with irreversible pulpitis in two groups: anxiety fell from 18.6 to 11.2 (single visit) vs 19.1 to 13.7 (multiple visits); lower pain at 1 week with single visit; success 93% vs 90% (p>0.05) — G S, Mathew J, Madhavan R et al. Bioinformation 2025;21(6):1563-1566. [PMID 40978627](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40978627/). (Abstract does not name the anxiety scale, the allocation method or the significance of the anxiety difference; low-quality evidence.)
- **Treatment itself reduces anxiety.** Meta-analysis: anxiety 39 → 27 on a 100-point scale after non-surgical RCTx — Khan S et al. J Endod 2016;42(12):1726-1736. [PMID 27776881](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27776881/)
- **Which operatory events are most arousing.** Local anaesthetic injection most anxiety-producing (GSR, pulse) across 8 recorded stages including rubber-dam application, drilling, radiographs, instrumentation and obturation — Morse DR, Chow E 1993. [PMID 8070990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8070990/); HR peaks at local anaesthesia and access preparation — Pandey P et al. 2025. [PMID 40444553](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40444553/)
- **Scented operatory.** See Section 4 (Jadhav 2020; Mittal 2024 positive; Mousavi 2025 null).

**(b) Extrapolated**
- **Deep-pressure input (weighted blanket/vest).** Randomized controlled crossover in healthy adults undergoing wisdom-tooth extraction: deep pressure associated with higher HF-HRV and lower LF/HF ratio during extraction — Chen HY et al. J Formos Med Assoc 2016;115(10):853-859. [PMID 27568389](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27568389/). Randomized adults, weighted vest in the waiting phase and weighted blanket during extraction vs. none: significantly lower HR and higher HF-HRV; authors call for "a large-scale clinical study" before clinical application — Chen HY et al. J Dent Sci 2025;20(2):1204-1212. [PMID 40224049](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40224049/). (Autonomic outcomes only; no self-reported anxiety in abstracts; sample sizes not stated in abstracts.) An adolescent crossover RCT showed the same HRV pattern — J Formos Med Assoc 2019;118(9):1317-1324. [PMID 31204146](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31204146/) (PEDIATRIC/adolescent).
- **Sensory-adapted dental environments (SADE).** Systematic review/meta-analysis of 5 RCTs in children and young adults (≤24 y) with intellectual/developmental disabilities: SADE superior to a regular environment for psychophysiological anxiety responses (standardised mean change −0.66, 95% CI −1.01 to −0.30); no significant difference in maladaptive behaviours — Reynolds K et al. BMC Oral Health 2023;23(1):769. [PMID 37858057](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37858057/) (PEDIATRIC/special-needs evidence; no neurotypical adult data found).
- **Noise masking.** 55 adults, ultrasonic scaling with headphones, active noise-cancelling on vs. off: noise-related discomfort 2.95 ± 1.99 vs 3.84 ± 2.12 and pain-related discomfort 3.09 ± 1.96 vs 3.78 ± 2.00 (both P<0.05) — Kim JW et al. J Periodontal Implant Sci 2023;53(4):269-282. [PMID 36468488](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36468488/). In contrast, a 4-period crossover in 30 adults with dental anxiety found nature sounds decreased respiratory rate and skin conductance and raised HF-HRV, whereas noise-cancelling headphones alone *increased* respiratory rate and *decreased* HF-HRV; a heated eye mask lowered HR — Hayashida Y et al. Cureus 2025;17(5):e84422. [PMID 40535407](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40535407/)
- **Handpiece noise.** Observational, n=297: handpiece sound provoked anxiety and HR change, more in first-time than experienced patients, and more in women and younger patients — Ghaffar RSA et al. BMC Oral Health 2024;24(1):265. [PMID 38389070](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38389070/)
- **Seeing the procedure.** Live microscope video reduced anxiety in a male-only restorative crossover trial (Section 1) — Sayed A et al. 2026. [PMID 41756793](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41756793/); by contrast, graphic preoperative surgical videos increased anxiety in third-molar and implant RCTs — [PMID 36493800](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36493800/), [PMID 31639265](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31639265/)
- **Waiting time/waiting-room environment.** A 1983 study exists — Coffey PA, Di Giusto J. "The effects of waiting time and waiting room environment on dental patients' anxiety." Aust Dent J 1983;28(3):139-42. [PMID 6579925](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6579925/) — but PubMed holds no abstract, so its findings could not be verified.
- **Brief pre-treatment waiting-room interventions with positive RCTs:** 1-3.5 min VR nature video (Lahti 2020, [PMID 31962052](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31962052/)); 10-min relaxing video (Craveiro 2020, endodontic, [PMID 32389383](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32389383/)); music before surgery in fearful patients (Miyata 2016, [PMID 30931796](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30931796/)).

### Inferences
- The time between arrival and anaesthesia is the window with the most (if weak) evidence for environmental manipulation — scent, music, a short relaxing video — and it coincides with the anticipatory peak before the injection.
- Passive silence (noise-cancelling without replacement audio) may not help and could heighten arousal in anxious patients (Hayashida 2025); masking drill noise with chosen audio is the more defensible approach. This rests on two small studies with different outcomes.
- If single-visit treatment genuinely lowers anxiety, the plausible mechanisms are removal of inter-appointment anticipation and the documented post-treatment fall in anxiety; the only direct study is too weak to rely on.

### Gaps
- **Appointment timing (time of day, first-of-day scheduling) and waiting duration:** no adult trial or analysable observational study was found.
- **Rubber dam and adult anxiety:** no adult study found; retrieved rubber-dam stress trials were pediatric (e.g., Kucukyilmaz E et al. BMC Oral Health 2025;25:855, [PMID 40448060](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40448060/)). A 2026 randomized study of a mouth-breathing device for rubber dam addressed breathing convenience, not anxiety (Jain G et al. J Oral Biol Craniofac Res 2026;16(1):132-137, [PMID 41399450](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41399450/)).
- **Waiting-room design (nature imagery, aquaria, lighting, colour):** no adult dental RCT retrieved.
- **Weighted blankets:** no trial with a validated self-report anxiety outcome in adults, and none in endodontics.
- **Single- vs. multiple-visit RCTx:** no adequately reported RCT with anxiety as a pre-specified outcome.

## 10. Which interventions have the strongest evidence overall, and what are the major limitations?

### Takeaway
Across umbrella-level syntheses, (1) CBT/exposure has the strongest and only durable evidence, but for trait anxiety/phobia rather than in-chair distress; (2) for in-chair state anxiety the only non-pharmacological approach reaching moderate certainty is hypnosis, with a small effect; (3) music, VR, aromatherapy, video information and acupuncture show positive pooled effects in some meta-analyses but are judged unsupported or low/very-low certainty in the most rigorous ones. Endodontic-specific evidence is very low certainty for every modality.

### Cited Findings
- **Most comprehensive RCT synthesis (adults).** 173 RCTs; 67 in 14 pooled analyses. State anxiety during oral surgery: hypnosis SMD −0.31 (moderate certainty); benzodiazepines SMD −0.43 (low certainty) as the pharmacological benchmark. "Evidence for reducing state anxiety is inconclusive regarding psychotherapy, and does not support virtual reality exposure therapy (VRET), virtual reality distraction, music, aromatherapy, video information and acupuncture." Trait anxiety: CBT SMD −0.65 (moderate). Dental phobia: psychotherapy SMD −0.48, CBT SMD −0.43 (low-to-moderate). Calls for trials "to minimize bias and follow CONSORT reporting guidelines" — Steenen SA, Linke F, van Westrhenen R, de Jongh A. J Anxiety Disord 2024;105:102891. [PMID 38945067](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38945067/), doi:10.1016/j.janxdis.2024.102891
- **Earlier meta-analysis (adults).** 29 RCTs, 2,886 patients: non-pharmacological interventions reduced mental distress (g=0.58, 95% CI 0.39-0.76) but not pain (g=0.00) or analgesic use (g=0.26, NS); substantial heterogeneity; larger effects in studies at low risk of selection and attrition bias; no significant differences between intervention types; "largest effects being shown for hypnosis" — Burghardt S, Koranyi S, Magnucki G, Strauss B, Rosendahl J. J Dent 2018;69:22-31. [PMID 29154798](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29154798/)
- **Behavioural-treatment meta-analysis.** ES 1.8 for self-reported anxiety; 77% long-term attendance — Kvale G et al. 2004. [PMID 15239776](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15239776/); RCT-only review: DAS −2.7, low quality — Wide Boman U et al. 2013. [PMID 23659254](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23659254/)
- **Umbrella review.** 35 reviews (2008-2021) on dentophobia: "the evidence concerning the efficacy of the various interventions is still rather weak"; no standardized guidelines — Aburas S et al. Spec Care Dentist 2023;43(2):163-173. [PMID 35700448](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35700448/)
- **Broad 2026 systematic review (children and adults, 126 studies, GRADE).** Strongest evidence for computer-controlled anaesthesia systems (outside the scope of these notes); "moderate-certainty evidence ... for aromatherapy, laser-based interventions, and audiovisual distraction"; other interventions limited by methodological weaknesses — Wawrzyniak A et al. Public Health Rev 2026;47:1609316. [PMID 42601922](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42601922/). (Mixes pediatric and adult data and observational designs; conflicts with Steenen 2024 on aromatherapy and audiovisual distraction in adults.)
- **Vote-counting review across medical and dental procedures.** 501 trials, 50,343 patients, overall 71% "success"; music, CBT, relaxation, massage, acupuncture/acupressure, hypnosis and natural sounds reduced anxiety in >70% of experiments; "confidence in results is limited by publication bias, small sample sizes, and the lack of placebo controls" — Weisfeld CC et al. J Altern Complement Med 2021;27(9):717-726. [PMID 34076531](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34076531/)
- **Extraction-specific syntheses.** NMA of 11 RCTs: music SMD −0.64, VR −0.54, audiovisual −0.34 (NS) — Hao T et al. 2023. [PMID 37735362](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37735362/). Systematic review of 25 RCTs in adult extraction patients: most reported reductions, but heterogeneity of interventions and outcomes "limits direct comparison" — Mažutė B et al. BMC Oral Health 2026;26(1). [PMID 42252433](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42252433/)
- **Endodontic-specific certainty.** Music during endodontic treatment: 5 RCTs, GRADE very low — Troian-Michel CH et al. 2023. [PMID 37728616](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37728616/). The only meta-analysis pooling non-pharmacological trials for RCTx anxiety has been retracted — [PMID 40892734](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40892734/); retraction notice [PMID 42658835](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42658835/)
- **Pediatric contrast (PEDIATRIC, for context only).** NMA of 61 RCTs (6,113 children aged 4-16): music ranked best for dental anxiety (SUCRA 93.6%), then aromatherapy (78.6%) and games (71.0%); hypnosis best for HR — Kong X et al. BMC Oral Health 2024;24(1):1151. [PMID 39342194](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39342194/). Umbrella review: audiovisual distraction effective in children — Quek JS et al. Eur J Paediatr Dent 2022;23(3):230-242. [PMID 36172904](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36172904/). No equivalent adult all-intervention network meta-analysis was found.
- **Documented limitations, as stated in the sources.** (i) Blinding: music meta-analysis — "almost none were double-blind" (Shih 2024, [PMC11546414](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11546414/)); aromatherapy — performance and detection bias most prevalent (Cai 2021, [PMID 32267044](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32267044/)); binaural beats — high risk of selection and detection bias (Shukla 2025, [PMID 40989974](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40989974/)); technology-based interventions — 4 of 7 RCTs high RoB (Gujjar 2019, [PMID 31326046](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31326046/)). (ii) Heterogeneity: I² 71-92% for music (Shih 2024); "high heterogeneity" for VR (Nezhad 2024, [PMID 39535423](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39535423/); Cao 2026, [PMID 42801915](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42801915/)). (iii) Small samples and no placebo controls (Weisfeld 2021). (iv) Baseline imbalance favouring the intervention (VR third-molar trials: Falci 2024, [PMID 38834821](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38834821/)). (v) No adverse-event reporting in music trials (Shih 2024). (vi) Research-integrity problems: two retractions in this literature (Guo 2025; López-Valverde 2020 first version, [PMID 32260538](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32260538/)).

### Inferences
**Evidence ranking for an endodontic resident (synthesis of the sections above; certainty labels are this note's judgement, informed by the cited GRADE ratings):**

| Intervention | Endodontic-specific evidence | Best general-dentistry evidence | Practical verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| CBT / graded exposure (incl. one-session, dentist-delivered, online) | None | Moderate certainty for trait anxiety (SMD about −0.65) and phobia (about −0.43 to −0.48); durable to 1-5 years | Strongest overall; for phobic/avoidant patients; needs lead time and referral or an online programme |
| MDAS screening + open acknowledgement by the dentist | None | Replicated RCTs in general practice | Cheap, safe, replicated; adopt routinely |
| Accurate, positively framed pain-expectation information; sensory focus for high-desire/low-felt-control patients | 2 trials (large, older; pain or fear-of-pain outcomes) | — | Reasonable and low-cost; more is not better (videos/leaflets add nothing; graphic video may harm) |
| Brief relaxation / paced breathing | Very low (tiny or uncontrolled studies) | Several small positive RCTs; beat music head-to-head | Good value; teach before the injection |
| Hypnosis | Historical pilots only | Moderate certainty, small effect (SMD −0.31), oral surgery | Useful adjunct if trained; not for phobia |
| Music (patient-selected, headphones) | Very low; about half of RCTs positive | Low certainty; SMD about −0.5 in adults; unsupported in Steenen 2024 | Offer routinely (free, harmless); do not rely on it alone |
| Aromatherapy (lavender/orange) | Conflicting (2 positive from one group, 1 null) | Low to very low; inconsistent replication | Optional ambience; small state-anxiety effect, mainly in women |
| VR / audiovisual distraction | 1 pre-op video RCT; 1 uncontrolled VR study | Conflicting adult meta-analyses; small in oral surgery | Unproven in RCTx; practical barriers under rubber dam/microscope |
| Auricular acupuncture | None | 2 sham-controlled RCTs positive, 1 null; pooled effect vs. sham non-significant (Allan 2018); unsupported in Steenen 2024 | Niche; requires training; poor fit for needle fear |
| Weighted blankets, noise-cancelling, single-visit scheduling, stop-signal devices | 1 low-quality study (single-visit); 2 small device studies | Small autonomic-outcome trials | Plausible, low-risk, essentially untested |

- **Mismatch between population and question.** Most distraction/sensory trials enrol unselected patients with mild-to-moderate anxiety (the mean pre-RCTx anxiety is only 39/100), so they show whether an intervention makes an ordinary visit somewhat more comfortable — not whether it enables treatment of a highly anxious or phobic patient. Where highly anxious subgroups were examined, music lost its effect (Lahmann 2008) while relaxation and CBT retained theirs.
- **Outcome problems specific to this literature.** (i) Trait scales (MDAS/CDAS) are often misused as pre/post state measures within a single visit; (ii) vital signs and cortisol are noisy surrogates and were usually null when adequately controlled; (iii) pre-to-post designs capitalize on the natural fall in anxiety once treatment ends; (iv) attention-matched controls are rare (exceptions: Lai 2008 silent headphones; Zaki 2025 white noise; Michalek-Sauberer 2012 sham acupuncture — in which the sham arms captured a substantial share of the effect).
- **Stepped approach implied by the evidence:** screen everyone (MDAS) and acknowledge the score → for all patients: accurate pain expectations, an agreed stop signal, patient-chosen music, a brief breathing/relaxation exercise before the injection → for high anxiety: add structured relaxation or hypnosis if trained, and consider the pharmacological options covered by the other researcher → for phobia/avoidance: refer for (or assign online) CBT before elective endodontic treatment.

### Gaps
- No adult network meta-analysis comparing all non-pharmacological modalities head-to-head was found (only extraction-specific and pediatric NMAs).
- No valid (non-retracted) meta-analysis of non-pharmacological interventions specifically for root-canal-treatment anxiety exists beyond the narrative review of music (Troian-Michel 2023).
- Per-intervention pooled estimates, trial counts and GRADE tables from Steenen 2024 for music, VR, aromatherapy, video information and acupuncture could not be retrieved (abstract gives the verdict only); whether any endodontic trials entered its pooled analyses is unknown.
- No trials report cost-effectiveness, chair-time impact or implementation in endodontic specialty practice, and almost none report adverse effects.
- No endodontic trial has tested multi-component "bundles" (e.g., screening + expectation-setting + music + breathing), even though this is how the interventions would be used clinically.
- Minimal clinically important differences for STAI-S, MDAS and VAS-A in dental settings were not identified in the sources reviewed, so the clinical meaning of statistically significant differences (e.g., 2-4 STAI points) remains a judgement.
