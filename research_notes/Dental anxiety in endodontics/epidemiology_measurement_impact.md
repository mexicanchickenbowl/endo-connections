# Dental anxiety in endodontics: epidemiology, etiology, measurement and clinical impact

Source note for the report writer: all journal citations below were retrieved from PubMed (PubMed MCP connector) on 2026-11-26 and are linked by DOI (or by PubMed record where no DOI exists). Unless stated otherwise, findings are taken from the PubMed abstract only; the single paper read in full text is Dou et al. 2018 (PMC5992818). AAE survey figures come from the AAE's own 2025 press release and from trade-press coverage of earlier AAE releases, not from peer-reviewed reports. Items I could not verify against a source are confined to the "Gaps" subsections and labelled as unverified. Abbreviation: RCT means root canal treatment throughout; randomized controlled trials are written out as "randomized trial".

## 1. Prevalence of dental anxiety/fear: general adult population vs. patients presenting for root canal treatment, retreatment and endodontic surgery

### Takeaway
In population samples, roughly 10–20% of adults have high dental fear/anxiety and about 3% severe fear, with the figure depending heavily on the instrument and cut-off. Endodontic patients as a group report "moderate" pre-treatment anxiety (pooled 39/100), root canal treatment is consistently ranked among the most feared procedures alongside oral surgery, and one emergency irreversible-pulpitis cohort reported 83% moderate-to-high MDAS anxiety; there are no robust prevalence estimates for retreatment or endodontic surgery.

### Cited Findings

General adult population
- Silveira, Cademartori, Schuch, Armfield, Demarco 2021, J Dent 108:103632 (PMID 33711405). Systematic review and meta-analysis of population-based observational studies: 31 publications, 72,577 adults. Pooled global prevalence: dental fear and anxiety 15.3% (95% CI 10.2–21.2), high fear 12.4% (95% CI 9.5–15.6), severe fear 3.3% (95% CI 0.9–7.1). Higher in women and younger adults; the instrument used changed the prevalence. Quality: 28 of 31 studies rated high risk of bias (JBI checklist), only 3 low risk. — [Silveira 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdent.2021.103632)
- Leśna, Górna, Kwiatek 2026, Br J Clin Psychol 65(4):907-924 (PMID 41906350). Systematic review and meta-analysis restricted to MDAS ≥19: 19 studies, 9,267 participants, pooled prevalence of high dental anxiety 18% (95% CI 10–30%) with substantial heterogeneity. Includes clinical observational samples as well as surveys, so it is not a pure population estimate. — [Leśna 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjc.70049)
- Humphris, Dyer, Robinson 2009, BMC Oral Health 9:20 (PMID 19709436). Telephone survey, representative quota sample of 1,000 UK adults (April 2008): 11.6% scored MDAS ≥19. Dental anxiety was four times greater in 18–39-year-olds than in those aged 60+, after controlling for sex, social class and attendance. Limitation: high attrition during recruitment. — [Humphris 2009](https://doi.org/10.1186/1472-6831-9-20)
- Sohn & Ismail 2005, J Am Dent Assoc 136(1):58-66 (PMID 15693497). Random-digit-dial sample, 630 dentate adults in the Detroit tricounty area: about 12% had high dental anxiety (Corah DAS ≥13). — [Sohn 2005](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.2005.0027)
- Dionne, Gordon, McCullagh, Phero 1998, J Am Dent Assoc 129(2):167-73 (PMID 9495047). US national telephone survey: nearly 30% reported being somewhat nervous, very nervous or terrified about going to the dentist. — [Dionne 1998](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.1998.0173)
- Moore, Birn, Kirkegaard, Brødsgaard, Scheutz 1993, Community Dent Oral Epidemiol 21(5):292-6 (PMID 8222604). Random sample of 645 Danish adults (88% participation): extreme dental anxiety (DAS ≥15) 4.2%, moderate (DAS 12–14) 6%. — [Moore 1993](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0528.1993.tb00777.x)
- Schuller, Willumsen, Holst 2003, Community Dent Oral Epidemiol 31(2):116-21 (PMID 12641592). Norwegian adults (Trøndelag-94): prevalence of dental fear 6.6%. — [Schuller 2003](https://doi.org/10.1034/j.1600-0528.2003.00026.x)
- Ibrahim, Lyons, Armfield, Thomson 2017, Aust Dent J 62(4):478-484 (PMID 28736912). New Zealand mailed population survey (523 sampled, 51.8% response): high dental anxiety 18.6% by DAS vs 13.0% by IDAF-4C in the same sample, illustrating instrument dependence. — [Ibrahim 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/adj.12541)
- Locker, Liddell, Dempster, Shapiro 1999, J Dent Res 78(3):790-6 (PMID 10096455). Mail surveys of a random adult sample (n=1,420): 16.4% dentally anxious; onset in childhood 50.9%, adolescence 22.0%, adulthood 27.1%. — [Locker 1999](https://doi.org/10.1177/00220345990780031201)
- White, Giblin, Boyd 2017, J Dent Hyg 91(1):30-34 (PMID 29118148). Cross-sectional, 308 patients in three types of US private dental offices: MDAS ≥15 in 19%, MDAS ≥19 in 6.82%. — [White 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29118148/)

Patients presenting for non-surgical root canal treatment
- Khan, Hamedy, Lei, Ogawa, White 2016, J Endod 42(12):1726-1736 (PMID 27776881). Systematic review of anxiety related to non-surgical root canal treatment (NSRCT): 835 titles, 36 articles included. Meta-analysis of 18 articles (1,989 subjects): pre-treatment anxiety 39 (SD 9) on a normalized 100-point scale, described as "generally moderate". NSRCT-associated anxiety "was ranked high among dental treatments, often close to oral surgery". Limitations stated by the authors: heterogeneous aims, outcome measures, settings, operators and sample sizes. — [Khan 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2016.08.007)
- Dou, Vanschaayk, Zhang, Fu, Ji, Yang 2018, BMC Oral Health 18:101 (PMID 29879974), full text read. Cross-sectional, 130 Chinese adults with irreversible pulpitis seeking emergency care. Mean MDAS 14.17 (SD 4.76). Distribution: 16.9% low (5–9), 66.9% moderate (10–18), 16.2% MDAS ≥19 (labelled dental phobia), i.e. 83.1% moderate or high. Dentist-rated anxiety during treatment (clinical anxiety rating scale): 36.2% moderate or severe. Limitations: single centre, patients who took analgesics within 24 h excluded, "moderate" band is very wide (10–18). — [Dou 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-018-0563-x)
- Peretz & Moshonov 1998, J Endod 24(6):435-7 (PMID 9693590). Records of 98 patients (42 men, 56 women) referred to an endodontist in Israel; Corah DAS mean 9.4 ± 3.3, reported by the authors as higher than scores from other countries. — [Peretz 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0099-2399(98)80028-9)
- Murillo-Benítez, Martín-González, Jiménez-Sánchez, Cabanillas-Balsera, Velasco-Ortega, Segura-Egea 2020, Int Endod J 53(4):447-454 (PMID 31691312). Cross-sectional, 180 patients (90 men, 90 women), short-form Dental Anxiety Inventory: mean S-DAI 27.2 ± 12.5; 50% of men had mild anxiety, 70% of women moderate or high (P=0.017). — [Murillo-Benítez 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.13245)
- Alroomy, Kim, Hochberg, Chubak, Rosenberg, Malek 2020, Eur Endod J 5(3):199-204 (PMID 33353908). Cross-sectional, 95 patients at a US endodontic visit, VAS-based: anxiety detected in 60% of females vs 33% of males (P=0.016); 62% of patients awaiting new treatment vs 39% of those returning to continue (P=0.049). — [Alroomy 2020](https://doi.org/10.14744/eej.2020.17363)
- Olivieri, de España, Encinas, Ruiz, Miró, Ortega-Martinez, Durán-Sindreu 2021, J Endod 47(2):189-195 (PMID 33161001). Observational, 96 patients having primary RCT by endodontists in Spain during COVID-19 confinement: median MDAS 8 (IQR 6–9.25) and 6 (IQR 5.5–8) in the two periods, i.e. low. — [Olivieri 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2020.10.024)
- Mungia, Funkhouser, Law, Nixdorf, Rubin, Gordan et al. 2025, J Dent 157:105723 (PMID 40157709). National Dental PBRN PREDICT study, 1,723 RCT patients (788 treated by general dentists, 935 by endodontists): the two patient groups were similar in dental fear, depression and anxiety. — [Mungia 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdent.2025.105723)
- Akhavan, Mehrvarzfar, Sheikholeslami, Dibaj, Eslami 2007, Iran Endod J 2(1):29-31 (PMID 24348655). Cross-sectional, 150 patients in a university endodontic department in Iran, Dental Fear Survey: highest anxiety in age 20–30, women, and those with less than diploma education (P<0.05). — [Akhavan 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24348655/)

How root canal treatment ranks against other procedures
- Wong & Lytle 1991, J Endod 17(9):461-5 (PMID 1811042). Questionnaire, 349 respondents from a general patient population (authors based at a US Army dental programme), 8 treatments: 35% had heard that root canal therapy was the most unpleasant treatment and 29% named oral surgery; among those who had actually experienced each, 53% rated oral surgery most unpleasant vs 17% for root canal therapy. RCT and oral surgery fell in the high-anxiety category; crowns, dentures and fillings moderate; cleaning and examination low. — [Wong 1991](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0099-2399(07)80138-5)
- Eli, Bar-Tal, Fuss, Silberg 1997, J Endod 23(11):694-7 (PMID 9587311). 92 patients about to undergo different treatments: anxiety and expected pain ranked extraction > root canal treatment > filling > calculus removal. — [Eli 1997](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0099-2399(97)80404-9)
- Udoye, Oginni, Oginni 2005, J Contemp Dent Pract 6(2):91-8 (PMID 15915208). Nigerian teaching hospital, Corah DAS: highest score for root canal therapy (9.30 ± 2.84), followed by extraction. — [Udoye 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15915208/)
- Huynh, Peters, Zafar, Peters 2023, Eur J Oral Sci 131(4):e12941 (PMID 37357152). Systematic review and meta-analysis, 23 studies from 3,639 records: RCT was more psychologically stressful for patients than routine restorative or cleaning procedures; comparison with extractions was inconclusive. Certainty of evidence very low to low. — [Huynh 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/eos.12941)
- Lin, Lee, Chen, Wu, Yang 2021, BMC Oral Health 21:328 (PMID 34210309). 402 adults, 12 treatment conditions, IDAF-4C+: fear of treatments never experienced was higher than fear of treatments experienced; wisdom-tooth extraction and root canal treatment had the highest "non-experienced" fear ratings. — [Lin 2021](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-021-01682-1)
- Peric & Tadin 2024, Medicina 60(8):1303 (PMID 39202584). Online survey, 1,551 Croatian adults (76.5% women): mean MDAS 9.70 ± 5.11, 7.8% phobic; procedures causing most anxiety were oral surgery (58.7%) and prosthetic/restorative treatment (15.2%). Self-selected social-media sample. — [Peric 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/medicina60081303)

AAE public surveys (not peer reviewed)
- October 2019 AAE survey of 1,000 US adults: 59% afraid of getting a root canal (65% of women, 53% of men), more than were afraid of public speaking (57%) or spiders (55%). Vendor and question wording not reported in the coverage. — [News-Medical report of AAE 2019 release](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20191022/AAE-survey-More-people-afraid-of-getting-a-root-canal-than-spiders-snakes-sharks.aspx)
- 2014 AAE annual survey: 54% said root canals make them "apprehensive", down from 60% in 2013, described as the lowest since the survey began in 2011. Sample size not stated; the article also contains a typographical "5%" in its introduction. — [DentistryIQ, March 2014](https://www.dentistryiq.com/clinical/endodontics/article/16359424/fewer-americans-fear-root-canals-more-want-to-keep-their-natural-teeth)
- June 2025 AAE release: 38% of the overall sample fear root canals, vs 48% of Gen Z and 46% of millennials; 45% of adults say they have had a root canal and 71% of those are glad they chose it over extraction; 42% would only see a dentist if in pain. Sample size not stated on the AAE page. — [AAE 2025 release](https://www.aae.org/specialty/new-survey-finds-gen-z-millennials-most-vulnerable-to-online-dental-misinformation/)

Retreatment and endodontic surgery
- Georgelin-Gurgel, Diemer, Nicolas, Hennequin 2009, J Endod 35(1):19-22 (PMID 19084118). Observational comparison, 30 surgical vs 30 non-surgical endodontic patients: variations in heart rate and systolic blood pressure depended significantly on treatment group (HR F=6, p<0.001; SBP F=4, p=0.05), with differences widening during active steps; stress during care correlated with anticipated anxiety in both groups. — [Georgelin-Gurgel 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2008.09.019)
- Khoo, Lopez, Ode, Yu, Lui 2022, Int Endod J 55(5):467-479 (PMID 35141909). Qualitative focus groups (18 retreatment, 15 apical surgery patients drawn from a 150-patient OHIP cohort): surgery patients described anxiety related to loss of control during surgery and apprehension on seeing the wound; retreatment patients reported time loss from work. — [Khoo 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.13691)
- Cho, Kim, Park, Roh, Lee, Lee, Jung 2017, J Endod 43(3):364-369 (PMID 28110919). Randomized double-blind split-mouth trial in 44 patients awaiting apical surgery, all completing MDAS: the higher-MDAS group reported significantly higher needle-insertion and injection pain, except insertion pain with topical anesthetic. — [Cho 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2016.10.036)
- de España, Olivieri, Ortega, Morelló, Encinas, Durán-Sindreu 2026, Odontology 114(3):1374-1382 (PMID 40993494). Cross-sectional interviews 5 years after anterior RCT, retreatment or single-tooth implant: implant treatment induced the highest perceived fear and pain scores (p<0.05); no differences between RCT and retreatment groups in OHIP-14 or satisfaction. — [de España 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10266-025-01199-x)
- Maraş, Başar, Çolakoğlu 2026, J Clin Med 15(12):4660 (PMID 42355827). Cross-sectional, 253 adults scheduled for endodontic treatment (MDAS, DFS, STAI): higher anxiety in patients with anterior teeth needing primary treatment or retreatment than posterior teeth (p<0.05). — [Maraş 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/jcm15124660)

### Inferences
- Endodontic patients are not uniformly more anxious than other dental patients: specialist-practice samples (Olivieri, MDAS median 6–8) sit near population norms, while emergency symptomatic-pulpitis samples (Dou, MDAS mean 14.2) are far higher. Setting, acute pain and referral status probably explain more variance than "root canal" per se.
- The 83.1% figure from Dou is driven by a broad 10–18 "moderate" band; the comparable high-anxiety figure (MDAS ≥19) is 16.2%, close to the 11.6–18% seen in general samples using the same cut-off.
- Reputation exceeds experience: the proportion who have heard RCT is the worst treatment (35%) is about double the proportion of experienced patients who rate it worst (17%) in Wong & Lytle, and non-experienced fear is highest for RCT in Lin 2021.
- AAE percentages (38–60%) answer a different question (any fear/apprehension about the procedure) and cannot be compared with psychometric prevalence estimates; wording and sampling changed between releases.

### Gaps
- No study was found that reports prevalence of clinically significant dental anxiety, with a validated instrument and stated cut-off, specifically in endodontic retreatment or apical surgery cohorts.
- The Khan 2016 pooled value (39/100) is a mean intensity on a normalized scale, not a prevalence; I could not access the full text to extract the instruments and per-study values.
- Unverified from memory (not confirmed against a source in this session): the UK Adult Dental Health Survey 2009 figures commonly quoted as 36% moderate and 12% extreme dental anxiety. The Hill 2013 abstract retrieved here does not state them.
- The 2009 AAE survey figure (54% afraid of root canal, most feared procedure) appeared only in a search-result summary of a UPI item ([UPI 2009](https://www.upi.com/Health_News/2009/03/27/Survey-Most-fear-the-dentist-root-canals/85781238210145/)); I did not open the page.
- Oosterink 2008 (67 dental stimuli, n=960) shows invasive/surgical stimuli are the most anxiety-provoking, but the abstract does not give the rank of root canal treatment.

## 2. Predictors and etiologic factors

### Takeaway
The most reproducible correlates in endodontic samples are a previous negative or painful dental experience, current pre-operative pain, female sex, younger age, and high expected pain; indirect learning (hearsay, parental, media) and perceived loss of control are well supported as etiologic pathways. Several of these associations are inconsistent across studies, almost all the evidence is cross-sectional, and catastrophizing is better documented as a predictor of pain than of anxiety.

### Cited Findings

Previous negative or painful dental experience
- Dou 2018 (n=130, irreversible pulpitis): MDAS 15.84 ± 5.04 in those with a bad most-recent dental experience vs 12.09 ± 3.96 with a good one (ANOVA P<0.05); pain at the most recent dental visit correlated with MDAS (Spearman 0.283, P=0.009). — [Dou 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-018-0563-x)
- Olivieri 2021 (n=96, RCT): a previous bad dental experience was associated with higher dental anxiety and fear (P<.05). — [Olivieri 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2020.10.024)
- Pandey, Jasrasaria, Bharti, Pandey, Singh 2025, J Contemp Dent Pract 26(3):244-249 (PMID 40444553). Observational, 76 single-visit RCT patients: prior negative dental experience associated with higher MDAS (p=0.018). — [Pandey 2025](https://doi.org/10.5005/jp-journals-10024-3828)
- Vitali, Mafra, Santos, da Fonseca Roberti Garcia et al. 2024, Int Endod J 57(12):1758-1768 (PMID 39150401). Structural equation model, 154 single-visit RCT patients: previous negative experiences acted on post-operative pain indirectly through dental anxiety (coefficient 0.048, p=.04). — [Vitali 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.14137)
- Saatchi, Abtahi, Mohammadi, Mirdamadi, Binandeh 2015, Dent Res J 12(3):248-53 (PMID 26005465). 473 dental school patients (MDAS, DFS): previous traumatic experience raised anxiety and fear (P<0.001); prevalence of dental anxiety 58.8%. — [Saatchi 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26005465/)
- Contradictory: Peric & Tadin 2024 (n=1,551 online) found that although over half reported negative dental experiences, anxiety levels were unaffected. — [Peric 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/medicina60081303)
- Population level: Locker 1999 found negative dental experiences predicted dental fear regardless of age of onset; family history predicted child-onset only; adolescent onset was characterized by trait anxiety and adult onset by multiple severe fears and psychiatric symptoms. — [Locker 1999](https://doi.org/10.1177/00220345990780031201)

Pre-operative pain
- Alroomy 2020 (n=95): 69% of patients in pain reported being anxious (P=0.015). — [Alroomy 2020](https://doi.org/10.14744/eej.2020.17363)
- Dou 2018: pain before the present visit correlated weakly with MDAS (Spearman 0.191, P=0.030). — [Dou 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-018-0563-x)
- Maraş 2026 (n=253): results were consistent with pre-operative pain partially mediating the relation between state-trait anxiety and dental anxiety (PROCESS mediation; cross-sectional). — [Maraş 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/jcm15124660)
- Farias, Campello, da Silveira, Moraes et al. 2023, Clin Oral Investig 27(10):5709-5718 (PMID 37526740). Systematic review of observational studies: 4 articles, 471 patients; two studies found a positive association between pre-endodontic pain and anxiety. Certainty low to very low (GRADE). — [Farias 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00784-023-05181-1)
- Sadr, Gholamrezaei, McNeilage, Randall et al. 2025, Br J Pain 20(2) (PMID 41458166). Scoping review, 48 studies, 12 psychological constructs: pre-procedural endodontic pain was most frequently linked to anxiety. — [Sadr 2025](https://doi.org/10.1177/20494637251408718)
- Contradictory: Peretz & Moshonov 1998 found no DAS difference between patients with and without previous pain in the treated tooth; Çoban Öksüzer 2025 found pre-operative pain had no significant effect on anxiety in adjusted regression. — [Peretz 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0099-2399(98)80028-9); [Çoban Öksüzer 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-19374-z)

Gender
- Women more anxious in endodontic samples: Peretz & Moshonov 1998; Murillo-Benítez 2020 (70% of women moderate/high vs 50% of men mild); Alroomy 2020 (60% vs 33%); Akhavan 2007. — [Peretz 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0099-2399(98)80028-9); [Murillo-Benítez 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.13245); [Alroomy 2020](https://doi.org/10.14744/eej.2020.17363); [Akhavan 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24348655/)
- Watkins, Logan, Kirchner 2002, J Am Dent Assoc 133(1):45-54 (PMID 11811742). 333 adults before and after endodontic therapy by faculty or residents: women were significantly more likely to anticipate higher pain and unpleasantness, but experienced levels did not differ by sex. — [Watkins 2002](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.2002.0020)
- Çoban Öksüzer, Hatipoğlu, Şanal Çıkman, Pertek Hatipoğlu 2025, Sci Rep 15:35442 (PMID 41073511). Cross-sectional, 259 adults needing RCT (191 women): women in the menstrual and secretory phases had higher MDAS and STAI-T than men (p<0.001), persisting after adjustment; blood pressure and SpO2 did not differ. — [Çoban Öksüzer 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-19374-z)
- Heft, Meng, Bradley, Lang 2007, Community Dent Oral Epidemiol 35(6):421-8 (PMID 18039283). Telephone survey of 504 Florida adults: women more likely to report dental fear and fear of dental pain; both sexes endorsed "dread" more readily than "fear". — [Heft 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0528.2006.00344.x)
- Contradictory: Dou 2018 found no sex difference in MDAS (14.08 vs 14.25); Peric 2024 found none. — [Dou 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-018-0563-x)

Age
- Watkins 2002: anticipated and experienced pain decreased significantly with increasing age. — [Watkins 2002](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.2002.0020)
- Carter, Carter, Boschen, AlShwaimi, George 2015, J Endod 41(9):1437-40 (PMID 26027874). 879 patients (20–90 y) with past or planned RCT: the 40+ and 65+ groups showed less fear than 20–39-year-olds (P<.05) across ethnicities. — [Carter 2015 JOE](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2015.04.014)
- Peretz & Moshonov 1998: highest DAS in women in their mid-thirties to mid-forties and men in their twenties. — [Peretz 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0099-2399(98)80028-9)
- Contradictory: Dou 2018 (ANOVA P>0.05) and Saatchi 2015 (r=−0.08, P=0.07) found no age effect. — [Dou 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-018-0563-x); [Saatchi 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26005465/)

Education
- Peretz & Moshonov 1998: higher education associated with lower DAS. Akhavan 2007: highest anxiety in those below diploma level. do Nascimento 2011 (400 Brazilian public clinic patients, MDAS): schooling was the only significant socioeconomic factor for anxiety and fear. — [Peretz 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0099-2399(98)80028-9); [Akhavan 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24348655/); [do Nascimento 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22238731/)
- Contradictory: no education effect in Dou 2018 or Saatchi 2015 (r=−0.046, P=0.32). — [Dou 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-018-0563-x); [Saatchi 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26005465/)

Expectation of pain
- Santos-Puerta & Peñacoba-Puente 2023, Community Dent Health 40(2):85-91 (PMID 36862423). 100 patients requiring endodontic therapy: dental fear, pain anticipation and dental avoidance were positively correlated (p<0.001), fear–pain anticipation showing the largest effect; self-efficacy moderated the link between pain anticipation and avoidance. — [Santos-Puerta 2023](https://doi.org/10.1922/CDH_00157Santos-Puerta07)
- Perković, Romić, Perić, Krmek 2014, Acta Stomatol Croat 48(4):258-67 (PMID 27688374). 66 endodontic patients: higher anxiety increased expected pain (Spearman rho 0.401, p=0.001). — [Perković 2014](https://doi.org/10.15644/asc47/4/3)
- Klages, Ulusoy, Kianifard, Wehrbein 2004, Eur J Oral Sci 112(6):477-83 (PMID 15560829). 97 patients having extraction or root canal treatment: high-DAS patients both expected and experienced more pain; among high-DAS patients only those with low pain sensitivity disconfirmed their expectations. — [Klages 2004](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0722.2004.00167.x)
- Klages, Kianifard, Ulusoy, Wehrbein 2006, Community Dent Oral Epidemiol 34(2):139-45 (PMID 16515678). 97 restorative patients: dentally fearful patients high in anxiety sensitivity expected and experienced more pain (significant interactions). Not an endodontic sample. — [Klages 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0528.2006.00265.x)

Fear of the needle and of failed anesthesia
- Huh, Montagnese, Harding, Aminoshariae, Mickel 2015, J Endod 41(2):182-9 (PMID 25458014). 24-item survey of adults at a graduate endodontic clinic: concerns about endodontic therapy were fear of pain (35%), fear of needles (16%), difficulty getting numb (10%) and anxiety (7%). — [Huh 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2014.10.009)
- Milgrom, Coldwell, Getz, Weinstein, Ramsay 1997, J Am Dent Assoc 128(6):756-66 (PMID 9188235). University survey: more than 25% of adults had at least one clinically significant fear of dental injections; almost 1 in 20 had avoided, cancelled or not appeared for appointments because of it. Four dimensions: general injection fear including pain, bodily injury, acquired disease, and local-anesthetic-related fear. — [Milgrom 1997](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.1997.0301)
- Humphris, Morrison, Lindsay 1995, Community Dent Health 12(3):143-50 (PMID 7584581). 1,392 subjects: 13% expressed extreme anxiety about injections on the MDAS while being only "fairly" or less anxious about drilling. — [Humphris 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7584581/)
- Erten, Akarslan, Bodrumlu 2006, Quintessence Int 37(4):304-10 (PMID 16594362). 1,437 Turkish dental clinic patients (DAS, DFS): most fear-producing stimuli were sight of the needle (25.1%) and sensation of the injection (24.1%). — [Erten 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16594362/)

Loss of control and the Cognitive Vulnerability Model
- Armfield 2006, Clin Psychol Rev 26(6):746-68 (PMID 16806621). Theoretical review proposing that perceptions of a stimulus as uncontrollable, unpredictable, dangerous and disgusting create a vulnerability schema that determines fear. — [Armfield 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2006.03.007)
- Armfield, Slade, Spencer 2008, BMC Oral Health 8:2 (PMID 18218075). National Australian sample, 3,937 participants: each vulnerability perception was strongly associated with high dental fear; uncontrollability and dangerousness remained significant in logistic regression after age and sex, unpredictability did not. — [Armfield 2008](https://doi.org/10.1186/1472-6831-8-2)
- Logan, Baron, Keeley, Law, Stein 1991, Health Psychol 10(5):352-9 (PMID 1935871). Three studies: patients with high desire for control combined with low felt control had particularly high dental distress before and after treatment and remembered treatment as more aversive one week later. — [Logan 1991](https://doi.org/10.1037//0278-6133.10.5.352)
- Maggirias & Locker 2002, Community Dent Oral Epidemiol 30(2):151-9 (PMID 12000356). Longitudinal population study, 1,422 subjects: pain during treatment was more likely in those with previous painful experiences, dental anxiety, expectation of pain and a feeling of little control. — [Maggirias 2002](https://doi.org/10.1034/j.1600-0528.2002.300209.x)
- Khoo 2022: apical surgery patients reported anxiety related to loss of control during surgery (qualitative). — [Khoo 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.13691)

Indirect learning, hearsay, culture and media
- Carter, Carter, George 2015, Int Endod J 48(6):528-32 (PMID 25066388). 594 patients (20–90 y) with past or planned RCT, modified Öst & Hugdahl survey: cognitive conditioning and parental pathways were the primary sources of RCT fear (P<0.05); women were more influenced by informative, parental, verbal-threat and vicarious pathways. — [Carter 2015 IEJ](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.12343)
- Carter 2015 J Endod (n=879, "My Endodontic Fear" questionnaire): White (54.5%) and Arab/African (30.9%) respondents used the conditioning pathway more than East Asian (10.5%) or Aboriginal/Pacific Islander (3.9%) respondents (P<.001). — [Carter 2015 JOE](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2015.04.014)
- Chen, Carter, Boschen, Love, George 2020, Eur Endod J 5(1):2-5 (PMID 32342030). 146 East Asian patients: cognitive conditioning most selected (51.4% Chinese, 43.6% non-Chinese), then informative (38.3%, 38.5%), vicarious (27.1%, 33.3%) and parental (18.7%, 33.3%). — [Chen 2020](https://doi.org/10.14744/eej.2019.46338)
- Carter, AlShwaimi, Boschen, Carter, George 2019, Aust Endod J 45(1):20-25 (PMID 29336506). 324 patients of Saudi descent: 90% reported more than one pathway; those living in Australia mainly used the vicarious pathway (94.9%), those in Saudi Arabia the verbal-threat (93.5%) and parental (78.3%) pathways. Pilot study. — [Carter 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/aej.12253)
- Wong & Lytle 1991: patients who had heard negative stories about RCT had higher anxiety toward it, and patients with experience of RCT had lower anxiety than those without. — [Wong 1991](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0099-2399(07)80138-5)
- Dou 2018: in patients with no previous endodontic therapy, negative attitudes acquired from others or the media were associated with more dentist-rated anxiety during treatment (P<0.001). — [Dou 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-018-0563-x)
- van Wijk & Hoogstraten 2006, Int Endod J 39(5):384-8 (PMID 16640637). Randomized experiment, 437 subjects allocated to one of five brochure paragraphs: those given positive information about pain during endodontic treatment reported less fear of pain associated with it. Non-patient sample; outcome was questionnaire fear, not behaviour. — [van Wijk 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2591.2006.01090.x)
- van Wijk & Hoogstraten 2005, J Dent Res 84(10):947-50 (PMID 16183796). 48 highly anxious patients, 56 periodontal patients, 262 students: less fear of a specific dental pain was reported when it had been experienced personally, except among highly anxious patients. — [van Wijk 2005](https://doi.org/10.1177/154405910508401014)
- AAE 2025 release: 30% of adults (40% of Gen Z, 36% of millennials) always or often struggle to distinguish myths from facts about dental health online. — [AAE 2025 release](https://www.aae.org/specialty/new-survey-finds-gen-z-millennials-most-vulnerable-to-online-dental-misinformation/)

Catastrophizing, trait anxiety and other dispositions
- Mungia, Funkhouser, Law, Nixdorf, Lindquist, Caprio et al. 2025, J Dent 163:106147 (PMID 41075433). PBRN PREDICT, 1,722 RCT patients: 21% had severe pre-operative pain (≥7/10); a pain catastrophizing score ≥5 was associated with severe pain (OR 2.85, p<.001). — [Mungia 2025 pain](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdent.2025.106147)
- Gastmann, Xavier, Pilownic, Romano, Gomes, Goettems 2024, Braz Oral Res 38:e054 (PMID 38922214). Prospective, 60 pregnant and non-pregnant women having RCT: post-operative pain associated with high catastrophizing (OR 1.96, 95% CI 1.01–3.84) and irreversible pulpitis (OR 4.78, 95% CI 1.55–13.55). — [Gastmann 2024](https://doi.org/10.1590/1807-3107bor-2024.vol38.0054)
- Ogawa, Saito, Karukivi, Lahti 2024, Eur J Oral Sci 132(2):e12973 (PMID 38311805). 428 Japanese panel adults, structural equation model: sensory sensitivity and pain catastrophizing were independently associated with anticipatory and treatment-related dental anxiety and fully mediated the effect of alexithymia. Not endodontic. — [Ogawa 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/eos.12973)
- Çoban Öksüzer 2025: strong positive correlation between MDAS and both STAI-S and STAI-T in RCT patients. Maraş 2026: significant correlations among MDAS, DFS, STAI-S and STAI-T. — [Çoban Öksüzer 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-19374-z); [Maraş 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/jcm15124660)
- Vitali 2024: sense of coherence affected post-operative pain indirectly through dental anxiety and dental fear (coefficient 0.006, p=.01). — [Vitali 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.14137)
- Vishal, Padala, Soujanya, Kaushik 2026, J Conserv Dent Endod 29(5):514-518 (PMID 42306609). 150 patients with symptomatic pulpitis or apical periodontitis, GAD-7: contralateral mechanical allodynia (10.7% of patients) was associated with higher general anxiety (P<0.001). — [Vishal 2026](https://doi.org/10.4103/JCDE.JCDE_138_26)

### Inferences
- The predictors with the most consistent direction across endodontic studies are previous bad experience and expected pain; sex, age and education effects appear in some samples and vanish in others, suggesting they are moderated by setting and culture.
- Endodontic fear is frequently acquired without direct experience (hearsay, parental, informative pathways), which fits the observation that non-experienced treatments are feared more and that accurate positive information lowers stated fear.
- The Cognitive Vulnerability Model has population-level support for dental fear generally (uncontrollability, dangerousness) but has not been tested directly in an endodontic cohort; the endodontic "loss of control" evidence is qualitative.
- Pre-operative pain and anxiety are probably bidirectionally linked; cross-sectional designs cannot separate pain raising anxiety from anxious patients delaying care and presenting with worse pain.

### Gaps
- No endodontic study was found that measured the four CVM perceptions (uncontrollability, unpredictability, dangerousness, disgust) as predictors.
- No peer-reviewed study was found quantifying the effect of media or social-media portrayal of "root canal" on anxiety; the AAE misinformation data are not linked to anxiety scores.
- Pain catastrophizing has been related to endodontic pain outcomes, but I found no study relating the Pain Catastrophizing Scale to a validated dental anxiety score in endodontic patients.
- Carter's pathway studies report which pathways patients endorse, not how strongly each predicts anxiety severity.

## 3. Measurement: validated instruments, cut-offs, chairside practicality and physiological measures

### Takeaway
The MDAS (5 items, score 5–25, high anxiety ≥19) is the best-supported brief chairside screen and the instrument most used in recent endodontic studies; a 0–10 or 100-mm anxiety VAS is a validated one-item alternative (about 5 cm as the threshold for meaningful anxiety). Physiological measures used in endodontic studies (heart rate, blood pressure, SpO2, heart rate variability, salivary cortisol, electrodermal activity) track procedural stress but correlate inconsistently with self-reported anxiety, and I found no endodontic patient study using salivary alpha-amylase.

### Cited Findings

Self-report instruments
- Corah Dental Anxiety Scale (DAS/CDAS): introduced by Corah 1969, J Dent Res 48(4):596 (PMID 5256508) and assessed as reliable and valid by Corah, Gale, Illig 1978, J Am Dent Assoc 97(5):816-9 (PMID 31377); described by Corah 1988 as a brief four-item scale. — [Corah 1969](https://doi.org/10.1177/00220345690480041801); [Corah 1978](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.1978.0394); [Corah 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3053270/)
- DAS cut-offs as applied in epidemiology: ≥13 for high dental anxiety (Sohn & Ismail 2005); ≥15 extreme and 12–14 moderate (Moore 1993). — [Sohn 2005](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.2005.0027); [Moore 1993](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0528.1993.tb00777.x)
- DAS limitations (Humphris 1995): no item on local anesthetic injection; answer options for three of four questions are not clearly ordered by severity and mix physiological reactions with anxiety, so the CDAS "can provide meaningful measures only of extremely high or extremely low dental anxiety". — [Humphris 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7584581/)
- Modified Dental Anxiety Scale (MDAS): adds an injection item and uniform ordered response options (Humphris 1995). Five questions summed to 5–25, cut-off 19 for high dental anxiety; UK norms from 1,000 adults; described as suitable for integration into everyday practice as a screen (Humphris 2009). — [Humphris 2009](https://doi.org/10.1186/1472-6831-9-20)
- MDAS psychometrics: Newton & Edwards 2005, Community Dent Health 22(1):40-2 (PMID 15819115), 135 patients: Cronbach alpha 0.93, test-retest ICC 0.93 over 2–3 months, discriminated non-anxious, treated-anxious and highly anxious groups, correlated highly with the 6-item Spielberger trait scale. In endodontic patients, alpha 0.91 (Dou 2018). — [Newton 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15819115/); [Dou 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-018-0563-x)
- MDAS banding used in endodontic studies varies: 5–9 low, 10–18 moderate, ≥19 phobic (Dou 2018); ≤10 low vs ≥11 moderate-to-high (Mumcu 2026); 15 and 19 as moderate-to-high and high cut-offs (White 2017). — [Dou 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-018-0563-x); [Mumcu 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jds.2025.10.019); [White 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29118148/)
- MDAS predictive validity at the endodontic chair: MDAS correlated with dentist-rated anxiety during treatment (Spearman 0.308, P<0.001) and with pain during treatment (0.431, P<0.001) in Dou 2018. — [Dou 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-018-0563-x)
- Dental Fear Survey (DFS): origin in Kleinknecht, Klepac, Alexander 1973, J Am Dent Assoc 86(4):842-8 (PMID 4511174); factor analysis with cross-validation gave three stable factors: avoidance and anticipatory anxiety, fear of specific stimuli and procedures, and felt physiological arousal (Kleinknecht, Thorndike, McGlynn, Harkavy 1984, J Am Dent Assoc 108(1):59-61, PMID 6582116). Used in endodontic samples by Akhavan 2007 and Maraş 2026. — [Kleinknecht 1973](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.1973.0165); [Kleinknecht 1984](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.1984.0193)
- Index of Dental Anxiety and Fear (IDAF-4C+): Armfield 2010, Psychol Assess 22(2):279-87 (PMID 20528055). Three modules (anxiety/fear, phobia, feared stimuli); the 8-item IDAF-4C covers emotional, behavioral, physiological and cognitive components; Cronbach alpha .94, test-retest r=.82 at 4 months; convergent and predictive validity compared favourably with the Corah DAS and a single-item measure. — [Armfield 2010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018678)
- IDAF-4C norms: Armfield 2011, Aust Dent J 56(1):16-22 (PMID 21332736), percentile ranks by age and sex from 1,063 Australian adults; recommends screening all dental patients. — [Armfield 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1834-7819.2010.01279.x)
- IDAF-4C+ caveat: Svensson, Hakeberg, Wide 2020, Eur J Oral Sci 128(5):423-428 (PMID 33463781), 147 adults with severe dental anxiety: the anxiety module agreed acceptably with other scales (rs 0.69–0.75; ICC 0.90, 95% CI 0.87–0.93) but the phobia module agreed very poorly with ICD-10 specific phobia (kappa 0.02). — [Svensson 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/eos.12731)
- Short Dental Anxiety Inventory (S-DAI): Aartman 1998, Community Dent Oral Epidemiol 26(5):350-4 (PMID 9792128), nine items, 321 highly anxious Dutch patients: alpha 0.88; correlation 0.73 with DAS. Used by Murillo-Benítez 2020 in RCT patients. — [Aartman 1998](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0528.1998.tb01972.x)
- State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI): used for state anxiety in endodontic trials (Lai 2008) and alongside MDAS in RCT cohorts (Çoban Öksüzer 2025; Maraş 2026). Facco 2013 used STAI-Y1 = 40 as the reference cut-off for pre-operative anxiety. — [Lai 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2702.2008.02350.x); [Facco 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23860442/)
- Visual analogue scale for anxiety (VAS-A): Facco, Zanette, Favero, Bacci, Sivolella, Cavallin, Manani 2011, Anesth Prog 58(1):8-13 (PMID 21410359), 1,114 implant patients: r=0.57 with DAS; thresholds 5.1 cm for dental anxiety and 7.0 cm for phobia; VAS-A and DAS disagreed in 28% of cases. — [Facco 2011](https://doi.org/10.2344/0003-3006-58.1.8)
- VAS-A second validation: Facco, Stellini, Bacci, Manani, Pavan, Cavallin 2013, Minerva Anestesiol 79(12):1389-95 (PMID 23860442), 100 oral surgery patients: correlated with CDAS, STAI-Y1 and STAI-Y2; ROC threshold 46 mm against STAI-Y1 = 40; about 50 mm proposed as clinically meaningful. — [Facco 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23860442/)
- VAS-A used in endodontic studies: Alroomy 2020 (prevalence), Craveiro & Caldeira 2020 (randomized trial, n=160), Zaki 2025 (randomized trial, n=99). — [Alroomy 2020](https://doi.org/10.14744/eej.2020.17363); [Craveiro 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2020.03.024); [Zaki 2025](https://doi.org/10.47391/JPMA.21023)
- Single-item questions: single-item dental fear measures were used as comparators in IDAF-4C validation (Armfield 2010) and in Swedish and Spanish adaptations; the PBRN PREDICT study used a categorical dental treatment fear rating ("some" to "extremely afraid"; the abstract does not say whether it is a single item) that predicted anesthesia failure (Liu 2026). — [Armfield 2010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018678); [Wide Boman 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/eos.12229); [Liu 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.70171)
- Dentist judgement: dentists' recognition of patient anxiety correlated only moderately with patient report (Spearman rho 0.460, p<0.001) in 66 endodontic patients (Perković 2014). — [Perković 2014](https://doi.org/10.15644/asc47/4/3)
- Overview: Newton & Buck 2000, J Am Dent Assoc 131(10):1449-57 (PMID 11042984) reviewed 15 dental anxiety measures; reliability and validity were good for most; Corah's DAS was the most widely used but possibly less sensitive than others. — [Newton 2000](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.2000.0056)

Physiological measures in endodontic studies
- Heart rate and SpO2: Olivieri 2021 (n=96) found higher HR with higher MDAS and fear scores, no clinically relevant SpO2 change. Pandey 2025 (n=76) found a moderate positive correlation between MDAS and HR, with SpO2 stable. — [Olivieri 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2020.10.024); [Pandey 2025](https://doi.org/10.5005/jp-journals-10024-3828)
- Contradictory: de España, Olivieri, Ortega-Martinez, Morelló, Roig-Cayón, Durán-Sindreu 2022, J Clin Exp Dent 14(10):e809-e814 (PMID 36320669), 80 patients (RCT vs single implant): no significant relation between anxiety level and HR or SpO2. — [de España 2022](https://doi.org/10.4317/jced.59281)
- Blood pressure and heart rate: Georgelin-Gurgel 2009 monitored HR, SBP and DBP through surgical and non-surgical endodontic sessions; Di Nasso 2016 (randomized trial, n=100, baseline Corah DAS) used SBP, DBP and HR before, during and after treatment. — [Georgelin-Gurgel 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2008.09.019); [Di Nasso 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2016.05.015)
- Dissociation of self-report and vital signs: Craveiro & Caldeira 2020, J Endod 46(7):909-914 (PMID 32389383), randomized trial, 160 endodontic patients: an audiovisual intervention reduced VAS anxiety significantly more than control, with no between-group difference in SBP, DBP or HR change. — [Craveiro 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2020.03.024)
- Heart rate variability: Santana, Pita Neto, Martiniano et al. 2016, Braz Oral Res 30 (PMID 26910016), 50 adults having first-session treatment of mandibular molars: non-linear HRV indices changed across four intervals (before, after anesthesia, during, after), HRV being reduced during treatment. — [Santana 2016](https://doi.org/10.1590/1807-3107BOR-2016.vol30.0029)
- Finger temperature: Lai, Hwang, Chen et al. 2008, J Clin Nurs 17(19):2654-60 (PMID 18808630), randomized trial, 44 root canal treatment patients: music increased finger temperature and reduced state anxiety (effect sizes 0.14 and 0.34). — [Lai 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2702.2008.02350.x)
- Salivary cortisol: Mumcu, Gündüz, Kiraz, Gündüz 2026, J Dent Sci 21(2):799-806 (PMID 42688684), 52 patients with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis: the moderate-to-high MDAS group had higher salivary cortisol and pulse rate (P<0.05). — [Mumcu 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jds.2025.10.019)
- Contradictory for cortisol: Ercan, Ozasir, Ozasir, Yesil, Arslan, Gulsahi 2026, Med Princ Pract 35(1):71-80 (PMID 40753968), within-subject study of 44 patients having endodontic then restorative treatment on a maxillary premolar: no significant correlation between Corah DAS scores and salivary cortisol (LC-MS/MS). — [Ercan 2026](https://doi.org/10.1159/000547716)
- Salivary cortisol was also an outcome in de Andrade Silva 2024 (randomized trial of cannabidiol, J Endod) and Wazzan 2022 (randomized trial of music, 46 emergency endodontic patients; no significant differences). — [de Andrade Silva 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2024.10.010); [Wazzan 2022](https://doi.org/10.23750/abm.v92i6.11668)
- Electrodermal activity: Anatürk, Özkan, Saral, Çakar 2025, J Oral Rehabil 52(11):1933-1944 (PMID 40506422), randomized trial of video information in endodontic patients using a wrist galvanic skin response device; GSR did not differ between groups. — [Anatürk 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/joor.14054)
- Synthesis: Troian-Michel, Tietz, Mendes et al. 2023, Clin Oral Investig 27(11):6321-6332 (PMID 37728616), systematic review of 5 randomized trials of music during endodontic treatment: outcomes were state anxiety, heart rate and blood pressure; very low certainty. — [Troian-Michel 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00784-023-05247-0)

### Inferences
- For chairside screening before endodontic treatment the MDAS is the pragmatic choice: five items, an injection item (relevant given needle fear), a published cut-off with norms, and direct evidence that it predicts observed anxiety and intra-operative pain in pulpitis patients.
- Banding below 19 is not standardized (≥11, ≥15, 10–18 are all used), so "moderate anxiety" prevalence figures are not comparable between endodontic studies.
- A single VAS or a single fear question is defensible when time is short: both have validation data, and a brief categorical fear rating predicted anesthesia failure in a 1,723-patient network study.
- Physiological indices reflect procedural arousal (injection, drilling) more than trait dental anxiety, and they can stay flat when self-reported anxiety changes; they should be reported as stress markers, not as substitutes for a questionnaire.
- Dentist intuition alone misses a meaningful share of anxious patients (rho about 0.3–0.46 with self-report).

### Gaps
- Unverified from memory (not confirmed in the abstracts retrieved): the DFS has 20 items scored 20–100, with ≥60 often used for high fear; the conventional Corah DAS bands (9–12 moderate, 13–14 high, 15–20 severe); and the IDAF-4C mean-score thresholds for high fear.
- I found no study using salivary alpha-amylase to measure anxiety in adult endodontic patients; the PubMed search returned only non-endodontic or non-patient studies.
- No head-to-head comparison of MDAS, IDAF-4C+, DFS and VAS-A in an endodontic cohort was found, so there is no evidence on which discriminates best at the endodontic chair.
- Minimal clinically important differences for these scales in endodontic trials were not identified.
- A 2025 PLoS One meta-analysis on sensory art therapies for root canal anxiety (Guo & Luo, PMID 40892734) has been retracted (retraction notice PMID 42658835) and should not be cited as evidence. — [Retraction notice](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0357077)

## 4. Anxiety and pain in endodontics: anticipated vs. experienced, intra-operative, post-operative and analgesic use

### Takeaway
Patients reliably expect more pain from root canal treatment than they experience, and anxiety inflates the expectation; higher dental anxiety is also associated with more intra-operative pain (OR about 4 in the best-known study, correlations about 0.4) and with more post-operative pain in the first days. The evidence is observational, reviews rate its certainty as low to very low, and at least one endodontic study found no link between anxiety and pain actually experienced.

### Cited Findings

Anticipated vs. experienced pain
- Watkins, Logan, Kirchner 2002, J Am Dent Assoc 133(1):45-54 (PMID 11811742). 333 adults rated sensory and affective pain immediately before and after endodontic therapy by dental school faculty or residents: 43% anticipated high levels, yet only 22% experienced high pain and 18% high unpleasantness. Levels did not differ by tooth type, pulpal diagnosis, endodontic history or attendance. Younger patients anticipated and experienced more; women anticipated but did not experience more. The authors conclude patients "perceive each ET experience as new". — [Watkins 2002](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.2002.0020)
- Rousseau, Clark, Newcomb, Walker, Eleazer, Scheetz 2002, J Endod 28(2):108-10 (PMID 11833681). 250 patients (150 pulpectomy, 50 extraction, 50 restoration): 92% of root canal patients said pain during the procedure was less or much less than anticipated; 83% had less pain during treatment than before it; extraction was significantly more painful than root canal therapy. — [Rousseau 2002](https://doi.org/10.1097/00004770-200202000-00015)
- Perković 2014 (n=66 endodontic patients): expected pain exceeded actual pain (paired t=3.540, p=0.001); anxiety correlated with expected pain (rho 0.401, p=0.001) but not with actual pain (rho 0.080, p=0.524). — [Perković 2014](https://doi.org/10.15644/asc47/4/3)
- Klages 2004 (n=97, extraction and RCT): patients in general expected more pain than they experienced; high-DAS patients expected and experienced more than low-DAS patients; high-DAS patients with high pain sensitivity did not disconfirm their expectations. — [Klages 2004](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0722.2004.00167.x)
- Chandraweera, Goh, Lai-Tong, Newby, Abbott 2019, Aust Endod J 45(2):225-232 (PMID 30341798). Pilot, 69 patients in Perth, 28 (41%) with both pre and post surveys: cost (55%) and pain (51%) were the main pre-treatment concerns; 7% expected no pain, 28% experienced none. — [Chandraweera 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/aej.12312)
- Pradhan, Nepal, Chakradhar, Mahanta, Adhikari 2025, Kathmandu Univ Med J 23(91):375-380 (PMID 42028775). 227 patients: 16.7% anticipated painless RCT, 75.3% painful, 7.9% extremely painful; afterwards 54.2% reported no pain, 43.2% painful, 2.6% extremely painful. — [Pradhan 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42028775/)
- Kopec, Lindemann, Hogden, Rysavy et al. 2026, Aust Endod J (PMID 42458676). Prospective cohort, 77 mandibular molar RCT patients: 73% reported no procedural pain and 65% rated the experience better than expected. — [Kopec 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/aej.70107)
- Öksüzer, Özceylan, Hatipoğlu, Hatipoğlu 2026, BMC Oral Health 26(1) (PMID 43058612). Prospective observational, 120 patients receiving IANB or PSA block for endodontics: expected injection pain 3.9 ± 2.4 vs experienced 2.3 ± 1.3; fear correlated with experienced pain (rho 0.33) and expected pain more strongly (rho 0.48); in regression the fear effect (β=0.15) became non-significant (β=−0.03, p=0.63) once expected pain entered (β=0.27, p<0.001). — [Öksüzer 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09671-y)
- Arntz, van Eck, Heijmans 1990, Behav Res Ther 28(1):29-41 (PMID 2302147). 40 subjects undergoing extensive dental treatment twice: anxious subjects expected more pain and anxiety than they experienced, needed more experiences before predictions became accurate, and their expectations and memories later returned to the original inaccurate level; they did not experience more pain but did experience more anxiety. General dental, not endodontic. — [Arntz 1990](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-7967(90)90052-k)

Baseline pain burden for context
- Pak & White 2011, J Endod 37(4):429-38 (PMID 21419285). Systematic review, 72 studies: pain prevalence 81% (SD 28%) pre-treatment, 40% (SD 24%) at 24 h, 11% (SD 14%) at 1 week; severity on a 100-point scale 54, 24 and 5; supplemental injections "frequently required". — [Pak 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2010.12.016)
- Segura-Egea, Cisneros-Cabello, Llamas-Carreras, Velasco-Ortega 2009, Int Endod J 42(7):614-20 (PMID 19467050). 176 patients: mean pain during RCT 1.2 ± 0.8 on a 0–10 VAS; 54% had no pain; more pain with mandibular and posterior teeth, irreversible pulpitis with acute apical periodontitis, and visits over 45 minutes. — [Segura-Egea 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2591.2009.01562.x)
- Montero, Lorenzo, Barrios, Albaladejo, Mirón Canelo, López-Valverde 2015, J Endod 41(9):1456-61 (PMID 26211565). Cohort of 250 adults: 62% felt no pain during RCT, 95% were relatively comfortable; at 7 days 60.4% had some pain, mean 1.5 ± 1.6 of 10. — [Montero 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2015.06.003)

Intra-operative pain
- Murillo-Benítez 2020, Int Endod J (cross-sectional, n=180, S-DAI vs 10-cm VAS): mean intra-operative pain 2.2 ± 2.1; anxiety correlated with intra-operative pain (R=0.406); in multivariate logistic regression anxiety was associated with moderate or intense intra-operative pain (OR 4.0, 95% CI 1.7–9.3, P=0.001). Note: the abstract's conclusion says anxious patients were "more than twice as likely" to feel such pain, which understates the reported OR. — [Murillo-Benítez 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.13245)
- Dou 2018 (n=130): MDAS correlated with pain during the present endodontic visit (Spearman 0.431, P<0.001). — [Dou 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-018-0563-x)
- Farias 2023 systematic review (4 observational studies, 471 patients): of three studies on anxiety and intra-operative pain, two found a strongly significant positive association; one noted anxiety influences pain expectancy. JBI quality good; GRADE certainty low and very low. — [Farias 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00784-023-05181-1)
- Lin, Wu, Yi 2017, J Dent Res 96(2):153-162 (PMID 28106507). Systematic review and meta-regression across dental procedures: 35 articles, 47 clinical groups. Dental anxiety significantly predicted expected pain, pain during treatment and post-treatment pain; pre-treatment state anxiety predicted expected pain only. Surgical and non-surgical procedures did not differ in dental anxiety or pre-treatment state anxiety. — [Lin 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022034516678168)
- Sadr 2025 scoping review (48 studies): procedural and post-procedural endodontic pain was consistently associated with anxiety, pain expectations, depression and pain beliefs. — [Sadr 2025](https://doi.org/10.1177/20494637251408718)
- Manteca-Fernández, Meniz-García, Fernández-Cáliz et al. 2025, Dent J 13(5):199 (PMID 40422619). Randomized comparison of articaine vs bupivacaine in 140 patients with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis: high MDAS anxiety was significantly associated with higher pain scores in both groups (p=0.000). — [Manteca-Fernández 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/dj13050199)
- van Wijk & Hoogstraten 2009, J Dent 37(9):700-4 (PMID 19556053). 247 patients before invasive dental treatment: anxious patients felt more and longer injection pain; fear of dental pain, surface anesthesia and gender explained 28% of variance in pain duration; injection anxiety and dental anxiety explained 22% of variance in intensity. — [van Wijk 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdent.2009.05.023)
- Maggirias & Locker 2002 (population cohort, n=1,422): 42.5% reported pain during dental treatment over five years and 19.1% moderate-to-severe pain; the number of invasive treatment types (including root canal therapy) had the strongest independent effect, followed by previous painful experience, dental anxiety, expecting pain and low perceived control. — [Maggirias 2002](https://doi.org/10.1034/j.1600-0528.2002.300209.x)
- Contradictory or null: Perković 2014 found no association between anxiety and actual pain (rho 0.080); Eli 1997 found anxiety correlated with expected pain but not with pain reported after electric pulp stimulation. — [Perković 2014](https://doi.org/10.15644/asc47/4/3); [Eli 1997](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0099-2399(97)80404-9)

Post-operative pain, flare-ups and analgesic use
- Torabinejad, Cymerman, Frankson, Lemon, Maggio, Schilder 1994, J Endod 20(7):345-54 (PMID 7996097). Multicentre controlled trial, 588 consecutive patients, ten medication/placebo arms, VAS for 72 h: three factors determined post-instrumentation pain: pre-operative pain (p<0.0001), apprehension before treatment (0.012<p<0.047) and type of medication. — [Torabinejad 1994](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0099-2399(06)80098-1)
- Vitali 2024, Int Endod J (n=154, single-visit RCT of asymptomatic necrotic mandibular molars, NRS over 30 days, structural equation model): dental anxiety (coefficient 0.028, p<.01) and dental fear (0.007, p=.02) had direct effects on post-operative pain. — [Vitali 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.14137)
- Sağlam & Arslan 2026, BMC Oral Health 26(1) (PMID 42192373). Prospective observational, 132 patients with vital symptomatic mandibular molars, MDAS and 7-day VAS diary: post-operative pain differed by anxiety group in the first 3 days (p<0.05); analgesic use differed among anxiety groups (p<0.001); anxiety remained associated with post-operative pain after adjustment (p=0.018). — [Sağlam 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-08715-7)
- Gastmann 2024: post-operative pain associated with high catastrophizing (OR 1.96, 95% CI 1.01–3.84). — [Gastmann 2024](https://doi.org/10.1590/1807-3107bor-2024.vol38.0054)
- Wu, Lin, Yang 2022, Clin Oral Investig 26(1):275-285 (PMID 34080062). 35 patients with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis receiving emergency treatment: expected pain relief predicted post-treatment relief (rho 0.76) and relief recalled at 6 weeks (rho 0.69), and predicted lower post-treatment pain (B=−1.06, p=0.017) and anxiety (B=−1.60, p=0.009). — [Wu 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00784-021-03997-3)
- Erdogan, Park, Casey, Rubi, Alroomy, de Armas et al. 2026, J Pain 45:106273 (PMID 41933816). Prospective cohort, 93 root-end resection patients: 15% had a non-resolving 7-day pain trajectory, predicted by Pain Catastrophizing Scale score (OR 1.11 per point, 95% CI 1.05–1.20), chronic bodily pain (OR 7.56) and pre-operative pain at the site (OR 5.95). — [Erdogan 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpain.2026.106273)
- Null for persistent pain: Philpott, Gulabivala, Leeson, Ng 2019, Int Endod J 52(4):407-415 (PMID 30332512), 198 patients (264 teeth): 24% of healing teeth had persistent pain or discomfort at first review; being a catastrophizer (PCS ≥30) had no influence (P=0.5). — [Philpott 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.13029)
- Flare-ups: Walton & Fouad 1992, J Endod 18(4):172-7 (PMID 1402571), 946 visits, 3.17% flare-ups, related to severe presenting symptoms, necrosis with painful apical pathosis and analgesic use; no psychological measure was collected. — [Walton 1992](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0099-2399(06)81413-5)

Memory of pain
- Gedney, Logan, Baron 2003, J Pain 4(2):47-55 (PMID 14622715). Root canal therapy patients followed by phone: experienced pain intensity predicted 1-week memory of intensity (adjusted R² .34), whereas pre-treatment affective state predicted memory of intensity at 18 months (R² .24) and of unpleasantness at 1 week (R² .40) and 18 months (R² .62). — [Gedney 2003](https://doi.org/10.1054/jpai.2003.3)
- Kent 1985, Pain 21(2):187-194 (PMID 3982842). Pain remembered 3 months after a dental appointment was closer to expected than to experienced pain, particularly in high-DAS patients. — [Kent 1985](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-3959(85)90288-X)

Does treating anxiety reduce pain?
- Silva, Agnol Júnior, Weissheimer, Reis So et al. 2023, Eur Endod J 8(2):105-113 (PMID 37010201). Systematic review of randomized trials on pharmacological anxiety management and pain during root canal treatment: 4 trials included (3 low, 1 high risk of bias); GRADE low; "insufficient evidence" that pharmacological anxiolysis changes intra-operative pain. — [Silva 2023](https://doi.org/10.14744/eej.2022.83097)

### Inferences
- The anticipation gap is the most robust finding in this literature (43% vs 22% high pain; 92% "less than expected"), and anxious patients both widen the gap and are slower to update, which is consistent with memory being rebuilt around expectation rather than experience.
- Anxiety is more strongly tied to expected pain (rho about 0.4–0.5) than to experienced pain; where intra-operative associations are found they are moderate (r about 0.4) and come from cross-sectional designs in which pain may itself raise anxiety ratings.
- Expectation appears to mediate the fear–pain link (Öksüzer 2026), which points to expectation management as the proximal clinical target.
- Post-operative evidence now includes three independent endodontic cohorts (1994, 2024, 2026) pointing the same way, but effect sizes are small and measurement of anxiety differs in each.
- That anxiolytic drugs have not been shown to reduce intra-operative pain suggests the association may not be simply causal, or that the trials were underpowered.

### Gaps
- No study was found linking a validated dental anxiety score to endodontic flare-up incidence (unscheduled emergency visit); the flare-up literature retrieved did not measure anxiety.
- Analgesic consumption as a function of anxiety is reported in only one study (Sağlam 2026) and only as a between-group p-value in the abstract; quantities were not available.
- I could not access the full texts of Farias 2023 or Khan 2016 to list the individual included studies and their effect sizes.
- No endodontic study with a longitudinal design that measures anxiety before pain onset was found, so temporal ordering remains unresolved.

## 5. Anxiety and local anesthetic success, avoidance/delay and attendance, satisfaction and quality of life

### Takeaway
Pre-treatment dental fear independently predicted local anesthesia failure in a 1,723-patient practice-based study, and a small prospective study found far lower IANB success in anxious patients with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis, yet oral benzodiazepine premedication did not improve IANB success in two placebo-controlled trials while nitrous oxide did. Dental anxiety is consistently linked to postponement and symptom-driven attendance, but endodontic-specific data on missed appointments are lacking and evidence on satisfaction and treatment outcome is thin and low quality.

### Cited Findings

Local anesthetic success
- Liu, Ordinola-Zapata, Noblett, Funkhouser, Law, Nixdorf et al. 2026, Int Endod J 59(9):1817-1830 (PMID 42148534). National Dental PBRN PREDICT cohort, 1,723 non-surgical RCT patients; anesthesia failure defined as patient-reported intra-operative pain ≥3 on a 0–10 scale. Failure in 16%. Independent associations (GEE adjusted for clustering): dental treatment fear, "some" to "extremely afraid" (p=0.005), age <55 (p=0.05), mandibular teeth (p=0.005), more abnormal pre-treatment diagnostic findings (p=0.02); protective: treatment by an endodontist (p=0.002) and non-Hispanic White patient (p=0.007). "Pre-treatment fear was the only significant psychosocial predictor of intra-operative pain." — [Liu 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.70171)
- Mumcu, Gündüz, Kiraz, Gündüz 2026, J Dent Sci 21(2):799-806 (PMID 42688684). Prospective clinical study, 52 patients with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis in mandibular first molars, IANB with 1.8 mL 4% articaine plus buccal-lingual infiltration, success = VAS ≤3 during access. Abstract reports success "84.2%" in the low-anxiety (MDAS ≤10) group vs "15.8%" in the moderate-to-high (MDAS ≥11) group (P<0.001); in logistic regression salivary cortisol was the only independent predictor of failure (P=0.010). The authors caution about operator and technique effects. — [Mumcu 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jds.2025.10.019)
- Manteca-Fernández 2025 (n=140, articaine vs bupivacaine, MDAS): the authors conclude anesthetic efficacy "did not appear to be influenced by the degree of anxiety", although high anxiety was associated with higher pain scores; reinforcement was needed more often with bupivacaine (p=0.004). — [Manteca-Fernández 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/dj13050199)
- Lindemann, Reader, Nusstein, Drum, Beck 2008, J Endod 34(10):1167-70 (PMID 18793913). Randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial, 58 emergency patients with irreversible pulpitis of a mandibular posterior tooth: IANB success 43% with sublingual triazolam 0.25 mg vs 57% with placebo (P=.43). — [Lindemann 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2008.07.013)
- Khademi, Saatchi, Minaiyan, Rostamizadeh, Sharafi 2012, J Endod 38(10):1337-9 (PMID 22980173). Randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial, 60 patients: IANB success 53% with oral alprazolam 0.5 mg vs 40% with placebo (P=.301). — [Khademi 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2012.06.007)
- Stanley, Drum, Nusstein, Reader, Beck 2012, J Endod 38(5):565-9 (PMID 22515880). Randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial, 100 emergency patients with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis: IANB success 50% with 30–50% nitrous oxide vs 28% with room air/oxygen (P=.024). — [Stanley 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2012.02.010)
- Later nitrous-oxide-based trials from the same group reported IANB success of 58% in both arms (MacDonald 2021, n=62) and no added benefit of intranasal ketorolac over nitrous oxide alone (Stentz 2018, n=102). — [MacDonald 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2020.12.014); [Stentz 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2017.09.011)
- Shetkar, Jadhav, Mittal et al. 2016, J Conserv Dent 19(5):390-5 (PMID 27656053). 198 patients, three block techniques: premedication with alprazolam plus diclofenac improved pain control vs control in every technique. The anxiolytic was not tested separately from the analgesic. — [Shetkar 2016](https://doi.org/10.4103/0972-0707.190013)
- Context: IANB fails in approximately 30–45% of cases even with proper technique (Potocnik & Bajrović 1999, review). — [Potocnik 1999](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-9657.1999.tb00782.x)
- Injection pain: higher anxiety predicts more injection pain in endodontic and surgical-endodontic patients (Cho 2017; Öksüzer 2026; van Wijk 2009). — [Cho 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2016.10.036); [Öksüzer 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09671-y)

Avoidance, delay and attendance
- Dou 2018: 53.1% of irreversible-pulpitis patients reported postponing dental visits; MDAS 15.00 ± 4.72 in postponers vs 13.23 ± 4.67 in non-postponers (P<0.05). — [Dou 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-018-0563-x)
- Santos-Puerta 2023 (n=100 endodontic patients): dental fear, pain anticipation and dental avoidance positively intercorrelated (p<0.001). — [Santos-Puerta 2023](https://doi.org/10.1922/CDH_00157Santos-Puerta07)
- Armfield, Stewart, Spencer 2007, BMC Oral Health 7:1 (PMID 17222356). Telephone survey, random sample of 6,112 Australians aged 16+: higher fear associated with less frequent visiting, symptom-driven visits and worse self-rated oral health; 29.2% of the very afraid vs 11.6% of those with no fear showed the full pattern of delayed visiting, poor oral health and symptom-driven treatment seeking ("vicious cycle"). — [Armfield 2007](https://doi.org/10.1186/1472-6831-7-1)
- Hill, Chadwick, Freeman, O'Sullivan, Murray 2013, Br Dent J 214(1):25-32 (PMID 23306496). UK Adult Dental Health Survey 2009: cost and anxiety were important barriers; adults with extreme dental anxiety were more likely to attend only when having trouble with their teeth. — [Hill 2013](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.bdj.2012.1176)
- Missed appointments: 8.4% (26/308) of private-practice patients reported missing an appointment because of dental anxiety (White 2017); 10.5% of clinic patients avoided calling for an appointment and 4.9% cancelled or did not appear (Erten 2006); almost 1 in 20 avoided, cancelled or failed appointments because of injection fear (Milgrom 1997). None are endodontic samples. — [White 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29118148/); [Erten 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16594362/); [Milgrom 1997](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.1997.0301)
- Sohn & Ismail 2005 (n=630): dentally anxious adults with private insurance were significantly less likely to visit regularly. Schuller 2003: adults with high dental fear had more decayed and missing teeth and fewer filled and functional teeth. — [Sohn 2005](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.2005.0027); [Schuller 2003](https://doi.org/10.1034/j.1600-0528.2003.00026.x)
- Demand for sedation: 51% of graduate endodontic clinic patients were interested in sedation if available; anxiety level and cost were the two factors influencing demand (P<.05) (Huh 2015). Nationally, preference for anesthesia/sedation was threefold higher than reported use (Dionne 1998). — [Huh 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2014.10.009); [Dionne 1998](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.1998.0173)
- Intention to avoid treatments never experienced, RCT among the most feared, rises with trait dental anxiety (Lin 2021). — [Lin 2021](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-021-01682-1)

Satisfaction, quality of life and outcome
- Hamedy, Shakiba, Fayazi, Pak, White 2013, Iran Endod J 8(4):197-204 (PMID 24171029). Narrative review of patient-centred RCT outcomes: pulpal disease affects quality of life moderately, mainly via physical pain and psychological discomfort; RCT broadly improves OHIP scores; satisfaction is extremely high with cost the main reason for dissatisfaction; anxiety and fear "profoundly" influence avoidance and pain experience. — [Hamedy 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24171029/)
- Montero 2015 (n=250): the main quality-of-life impact of pulpal pathology was in the pain and psychological discomfort dimensions. — [Montero 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2015.06.003)
- Natarajan, Kurian, Megalaa et al. 2025, J Pharm Bioallied Sci 17(Suppl 4):S3033-S3035 (PMID 41522944). Prospective cohort, 150 single-rooted RCT patients: in regression (R²=0.61) satisfaction was negatively associated with pre-operative anxiety (β=−0.38, p<0.001) and 48-h pain (β=−0.29); chewing improvement was the strongest positive predictor (β=0.42). Supplement-issue paper with limited methodological detail. — [Natarajan 2025](https://doi.org/10.4103/jpbs.jpbs_1431_25)
- Ahmed, Sharma, Mahaprasad et al. 2024, J Pharm Bioallied Sci 16(Suppl 3):S2685-S2687 (PMID 39346408). Prospective cohort, 100 patients: reported lower post-treatment pain (2.1 vs 3.8), higher "success rate" (85% vs 65%, P=0.015) and higher satisfaction in lower-anxiety patients. Low reliability: the anxiety scale, success criteria and follow-up are not specified in the abstract. — [Ahmed 2024](https://doi.org/10.4103/jpbs.jpbs_371_24)
- Melgaço-Costa, Martins, Ferreira, Sobrinho 2016, Int J Environ Res Public Health 13(5):450 (PMID 27128932). Qualitative study in Brazilian public services: perceived service quality was related to pain and anxiety experienced, number and length of sessions, and how patients were treated, more than to technical competence. — [Melgaço-Costa 2016](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph13050450)
- Pradhan 2025: 100% satisfaction with RCT and most patients willing to undergo it again. de España 2026: RCT and retreatment gave satisfaction comparable to single implants with lower perceived fear and pain. — [Pradhan 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42028775/); [de España 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10266-025-01199-x)

### Inferences
- Fear predicts "anesthesia failure" when failure is defined by patient-reported pain, so part of the association may reflect lower pain thresholds or reporting in fearful patients rather than failed nerve blockade; no study used an objective pulpal anesthesia endpoint stratified by anxiety.
- The benzodiazepine trials argue against a simple model in which reducing anxiety restores block success in inflamed pulps; nitrous oxide's benefit may be due to its analgesic as much as its anxiolytic action. Both benzodiazepine trials were small (58 and 60 patients) and did not select anxious patients.
- The Mumcu percentages (84.2% and 15.8%) sum to 100%, which suggests they may describe the share of successful cases falling in each anxiety group rather than within-group success rates; the full text should be checked before quoting them as success rates.
- Avoidance evidence in endodontic patients is limited to self-reported postponement; the vicious-cycle model (fear, delay, symptomatic presentation, more invasive treatment such as RCT) is supported at population level and is plausible for endodontics but not directly demonstrated.
- Claims that anxiety lowers endodontic "success" rest on one weak study and should not be presented as established.

### Gaps
- No study was found reporting appointment non-attendance, cancellation or treatment non-completion rates in endodontic practice as a function of measured dental anxiety.
- No study was found relating dental anxiety to periapical healing or tooth survival with adequate methods.
- No validated OHRQoL instrument (e.g. OHIP-14) has, in the studies retrieved, been analysed against a dental anxiety score in an endodontic cohort before and after treatment.
- I did not review the abstract of a 2025 trial comparing etizolam with music distraction on IANB success in symptomatic irreversible pulpitis (Singla et al., Anesth Prog, PMID 41360139); it may add to the anxiolytic evidence.
- No AAE position statement on dental fear or anxiety was located; only AAE consumer survey releases were found.

## 6. Change in anxiety across the endodontic visit and across multiple visits

### Takeaway
Self-reported anxiety is highest before treatment and falls by roughly 30% afterwards; physiological arousal peaks at local anesthetic injection and at access/drilling and declines toward the end of the visit. Patients returning to continue treatment are less often anxious than new patients, but prior endodontic experience does not reliably protect against anxiety at the next course of treatment, and anxious patients' expectations tend to revert.

### Cited Findings

Before vs. after treatment
- Khan 2016 (J Endod systematic review): pre-treatment anxiety 39 (SD 9) from 18 articles and 1,989 subjects vs post-treatment 27 (SD 5) from 4 articles and 232 subjects, on a normalized 100-point scale, a 30% reduction; a L'Abbé plot of 5 studies also showed anxiety decreased after NSRCT. The pre and post pools are largely different studies. — [Khan 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2016.08.007)
- Ercan 2026 (n=44, within-subject): Corah DAS fell from before to after endodontic treatment (p=0.004) and from before to after restorative treatment (p<0.001); patients reported higher anxiety before endodontic than before restorative treatment, with the authors noting no control condition and a time gap between sessions. — [Ercan 2026](https://doi.org/10.1159/000547716)
- Chandraweera 2019 (pilot, 28 paired responses): after treatment, anxiety about future RCT was significantly reduced. — [Chandraweera 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/aej.12312)
- Natarajan 2025 (n=150): MDAS fell from 14.7 to 8.3 by 30 days after RCT (mean change −6.4, P<0.001, d=1.8). Supplement-issue paper. — [Natarajan 2025](https://doi.org/10.4103/jpbs.jpbs_1431_25)
- Lin 2017 (meta-regression, 47 clinical groups): dental anxiety explained variance in state anxiety before and during treatment but not after treatment. — [Lin 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022034516678168)
- Wu 2022 (n=35, emergency treatment for symptomatic irreversible pulpitis): expected pain relief predicted lower post-treatment anxiety (B=−1.60, p=0.009). — [Wu 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00784-021-03997-3)

Within the visit
- Olivieri 2021 (n=96, HR and SpO2 at multiple time points of primary RCT): heart rate decreased at radiograph-taking (T6) and post-treatment (T7) compared with earlier time points (P<.05); the authors conclude RCT by endodontists "does not result in a significant alteration in patients". — [Olivieri 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2020.10.024)
- Pandey 2025 (n=76, six stages): heart rate was significantly elevated during local anesthesia administration (93.9 ± 8.2) and access drilling (94.7 ± 8.07), especially with moderate anxiety and severe pain (p<0.05); SpO2 stable. — [Pandey 2025](https://doi.org/10.5005/jp-journals-10024-3828)
- Georgelin-Gurgel 2009 (30 surgical, 30 non-surgical): intergroup differences in HR and SBP increased during active steps: local anesthesia, rotary instrumentation, periradicular curettage, root filling and field removal. — [Georgelin-Gurgel 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2008.09.019)
- Santana 2016 (n=50, HRV at four intervals): HRV was reduced during treatment and the parasympathetic component increased after local anesthetic application. — [Santana 2016](https://doi.org/10.1590/1807-3107BOR-2016.vol30.0029)
- de España 2022 (n=80, RCT vs implant, five time points): heart rate was higher at the initial stages and decreased as both procedures finished. — [de España 2022](https://doi.org/10.4317/jced.59281)
- Dou 2018: 36.2% of irreversible-pulpitis patients displayed moderate or severe anxiety during treatment by dentist rating. — [Dou 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-018-0563-x)

Across visits and with experience
- Alroomy 2020 (n=95): 62% of patients awaiting new treatment were anxious vs 39% of those returning to continue treatment (P=0.049); 43% of new vs 20% of returning patients were in pain (P=0.027). The authors conclude pain and anxiety "are usually reduced after the first endodontic session". Cross-sectional comparison of different patients. — [Alroomy 2020](https://doi.org/10.14744/eej.2020.17363)
- Zhang, Wang, Wang 2007, Hua Xi Kou Qiang Yi Xue Za Zhi 25(4):375-7 (PMID 17896496, Chinese). 360 patients in three information/communication/anesthesia groups: dental anxiety decreased as the number of treatment visits increased, most with psychological communication plus local anesthesia. — [Zhang 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17896496/)
- Saicharan, Mathew, Madhavan et al. 2025, Bioinformation 21(6):1563-1566 (PMID 40978627). 60 patients with irreversible pulpitis allocated to single- or multiple-visit RCT: anxiety fell from 18.6 to 11.2 with single visit vs 19.1 to 13.7 with multiple visits. Low reliability: scale, randomization and statistics are not described in the abstract. — [Saicharan 2025](https://doi.org/10.6026/973206300211563)
- Wong & Lytle 1991: patients with experience of root canal therapy had lower anxiety toward it than those without. — [Wong 1991](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0099-2399(07)80138-5)
- Contradictory: Peretz & Moshonov 1998 found no DAS difference between patients experienced in endodontic treatment and those who were not; Watkins 2002 found anticipated and experienced pain did not differ by endodontic history; Dou 2018 found no MDAS difference by prior endodontic therapy (14.69 vs 13.95, P>0.05), though those recalling it as "very bad" averaged 16.56 vs 12.67 for "good" (not significant). — [Peretz 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0099-2399(98)80028-9); [Watkins 2002](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.2002.0020); [Dou 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-018-0563-x)
- Persistence mechanisms: Arntz 1990 showed anxious patients' expectations and memories returned to their original inaccurate level over time; Kent 1985 showed remembered pain tracked expected rather than experienced pain in anxious patients; Gedney 2003 showed pre-treatment affect predicted memory of RCT pain unpleasantness at 18 months (adjusted R² .62). — [Arntz 1990](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-7967(90)90052-k); [Kent 1985](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-3959(85)90288-X); [Gedney 2003](https://doi.org/10.1054/jpai.2003.3)

### Inferences
- Two arousal peaks are clinically actionable: the injection and the start of access. These are the points where distraction, topical anesthesia, slow injection and explicit signalling are most likely to matter.
- The post-treatment drop in anxiety is real but probably temporary for trait-anxious patients: experience lowers anxiety on average, yet a good experience does not durably overwrite expectation in the most anxious, which explains why prior RCT does not predict lower anxiety or pain at the next RCT.
- The quality of the previous endodontic experience, not merely having had one, appears to be what matters (Dou's "very bad" vs "good" gradient), though that comparison was underpowered.
- Lower anxiety in returning patients is confounded by pain relief after the first visit and by drop-out of the most fearful.

### Gaps
- No prospective study was found that tracks the same endodontic patients with a validated anxiety scale at each appointment of a multi-visit treatment.
- No adequately reported randomized comparison of single- vs multiple-visit treatment with anxiety as an outcome was found; the one study identified is low quality.
- How long the post-treatment reduction in anxiety lasts, and whether it lowers avoidance of future endodontic care, has not been measured beyond 30 days in the studies retrieved.
- No data were found on anxiety trajectories across retreatment or surgical endodontic visits.
