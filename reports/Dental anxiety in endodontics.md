# Expected Pain Drives Root Canal Fear

Dental anxiety at the endodontic chair is common but, on average, moderate, and it is driven far more by what patients expect to feel than by what root canal treatment actually feels like: pooled pre-treatment anxiety sits at 39 on a 100-point scale, 43% of patients anticipate high pain while only 22% report it, and anxiety falls by roughly 30% once treatment is over. Phobic-range anxiety (MDAS ≥19) affects roughly 6–18% of adults depending on the sample, including 16% of one emergency pulpitis cohort, and the reproducible drivers are a previous bad dental experience, expected pain, fear of the needle and of not getting numb, and loss of control. Anxious patients report more intraoperative pain, more pain-defined anesthetic failure and more early postoperative pain, but that evidence is observational and graded low to very low, and nothing reliable links anxiety to periapical healing or tooth survival. The five-item MDAS is the best-supported chairside screen; clinician intuition is a poor substitute. On the mitigation side, the strongest endodontic-specific evidence is in pain control: NSAID or dexamethasone premedication roughly doubles block success in the hot tooth, and planned supplemental anesthesia covers most of the remainder. Cognitive behavioural therapy is the only intervention shown to reduce the fear itself, while music, aromatherapy, virtual reality and information videos rest on low or very-low-certainty evidence. An oral benzodiazepine lowers anxiety during the visit but does not improve inferior alveolar nerve block success; nitrous oxide did in a single trial. No ADA or AAE guideline on managing the anxious adult was located, so the stepped chairside approach at the end of this report is a synthesis rather than a published algorithm.

> **How to read the evidence.** Almost every study below was verified at abstract level only; the exceptions read in full were Dou 2018, Shih 2024, Wolf 2022, Appukuttan 2016, Raja 2014, the ADA 2024 pain guideline, the ADA 2025 sedation guideline, SDCEP 2017, the triazolam label and two AAE newsletters. Statements marked "researchers' inference" are the note-takers' own reasoning, not sourced findings. Retracted papers, unverified figures and low-detail reports are named as such where they arise and are not used as evidence.

## Endodontic patients are moderately anxious on average, and most of the fear is anticipatory

Population surveys put high dental fear at roughly one adult in eight. A meta-analysis of 31 population studies (72,577 adults) pooled dental fear and anxiety at **15.3%**, high fear at **12.4%** and severe fear at **3.3%**, although 28 of the 31 studies carried a high risk of bias ([Silveira 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdent.2021.103632)). Using the MDAS ≥19 cut-off, 11.6% of a representative UK sample scored as highly anxious ([Humphris 2009](https://doi.org/10.1186/1472-6831-9-20)); about 12% of Detroit-area adults had high anxiety on the Corah scale ([Sohn 2005](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.2005.0027)); and 6.8% of patients in three US private offices reached MDAS ≥19 ([White 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29118148/)). A 2026 meta-analysis restricted to that cut-off pooled 18% (95% CI 10–30%), but it mixes population surveys with clinical samples ([Leśna 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjc.70049)). The number moves with the instrument: the same New Zealand sample produced 18.6% on one scale and 13.0% on another ([Ibrahim 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/adj.12541)).

Endodontic patients as a group are not dramatically worse. The key systematic review pooled pre-treatment anxiety at **39 (SD 9) on a normalized 100-point scale** across 1,989 patients, called it "generally moderate", and noted that root canal anxiety ranks "often close to oral surgery" among dental procedures ([Khan 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2016.08.007)). That figure is a mean intensity, not a prevalence. Setting matters enormously: patients treated by endodontists in Spain had a median MDAS of 6–8 ([Olivieri 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2020.10.024)), whereas Chinese adults seeking emergency care for irreversible pulpitis averaged 14.2, with **16.2% at MDAS ≥19** ([Dou 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-018-0563-x)). The often-repeated "83% moderate or high anxiety" from that cohort is produced by a very wide 10–18 "moderate" band and overstates the problem; the phobic-range share sits close to general-population estimates. In the US National Dental PBRN cohort of 1,723 root canal patients, those treated by endodontists and by general dentists had similar dental fear ([Mungia 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdent.2025.105723)), and at one US endodontic clinic anxiety was detected in 60% of women versus 33% of men and in 62% of new patients versus 39% of those returning to continue treatment ([Alroomy 2020](https://doi.org/10.14744/eej.2020.17363)). The researchers' inference is that acute pain, emergency presentation and referral status explain more of the variance than the words "root canal" do.

Reputation outruns experience. In a US survey, 35% of respondents had heard that root canal therapy was the most unpleasant dental treatment, yet among people who had actually had each procedure only 17% rated root canal the worst, against 53% for oral surgery ([Wong 1991](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0099-2399%2807%2980138-5)). Fear of treatments never experienced exceeds fear of treatments experienced, with root canal and wisdom-tooth extraction at the top of the never-experienced list ([Lin 2021](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-021-01682-1)). A meta-analysis found root canal treatment more psychologically stressful than restorative or hygiene care, with very low to low certainty and an inconclusive comparison against extraction ([Huynh 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/eos.12941)). AAE consumer surveys report that 59% of US adults feared a root canal in 2019 and 38% in 2025 (48% of Gen Z), but these are non-peer-reviewed press releases with changing wording and undisclosed methods, and they cannot be set beside psychometric prevalence figures ([AAE 2019 via News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20191022/AAE-survey-More-people-afraid-of-getting-a-root-canal-than-spiders-snakes-sharks.aspx); [AAE 2025](https://www.aae.org/specialty/new-survey-finds-gen-z-millennials-most-vulnerable-to-online-dental-misinformation/)). No study reports the prevalence of clinically significant anxiety, with a validated instrument and cut-off, in retreatment or apical surgery cohorts; the available data are qualitative, with surgery patients describing loss of control and apprehension at seeing the wound ([Khoo 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.13691)).

### Bad experiences, expected pain, needles and lost control are the reproducible drivers

A previous negative dental experience is the most consistent correlate in endodontic samples. MDAS averaged 15.8 in pulpitis patients whose last dental visit was bad versus 12.1 when it was good ([Dou 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-018-0563-x)), and the association recurs in Spanish and Indian root canal cohorts ([Olivieri 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2020.10.024); [Pandey 2025](https://doi.org/10.5005/jp-journals-10024-3828)). In dental patients generally, experiences of helplessness during treatment carried the strongest association with high dental anxiety (**OR 8.2**) and a positive phobia screen (OR 16.2) ([Oosterink 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.janxdis.2008.09.002)). Expected pain is the second pillar: anxiety correlated with expected pain at rho 0.40 in endodontic patients ([Perković 2014](https://doi.org/10.15644/asc47/4/3)), and fear, pain anticipation and avoidance were tightly intercorrelated in another endodontic sample ([Santos-Puerta 2023](https://doi.org/10.1922/CDH_00157Santos-Puerta07)).

The injection deserves separate attention. Patients at a US graduate endodontic clinic named fear of pain (35%), fear of needles (16%) and difficulty getting numb (10%) as their main concerns ([Huh 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2014.10.009)). More than a quarter of adults in a university survey had at least one clinically significant injection fear, and almost 1 in 20 had avoided or cancelled dental care because of it ([Milgrom 1997](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.1997.0301)). In 12,400 University of Iowa records, needle anxiety was by far the strongest correlate of patients describing themselves as difficult to anesthetize (**adjusted OR 29**), ahead of general dental fear (OR 3.6) ([Bowers 2025](https://doi.org/10.17245/jdapm.2025.25.2.97)). Perceived uncontrollability and dangerousness predict dental fear at population level ([Armfield 2008](https://doi.org/10.1186/1472-6831-8-2)), and patients who want control but feel they have none are the most distressed before and after treatment ([Logan 1991](https://doi.org/10.1037//0278-6133.10.5.352)); this model has not been tested directly in an endodontic cohort. Root canal fear is also frequently learned second-hand, with cognitive conditioning and parental pathways the main self-reported sources in 594 patients ([Carter 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.12343)).

Demographic predictors are weaker than their reputation. Women and younger adults are more anxious in several endodontic samples, but other cohorts show no sex or age effect, and women anticipated more pain than men without experiencing more ([Watkins 2002](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.2002.0020)). Preoperative pain travels with anxiety in most but not all studies, at low to very low certainty, and cross-sectional designs cannot separate pain raising anxiety from anxious patients delaying care ([Farias 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00784-023-05181-1)). Pain catastrophizing predicts severe preoperative endodontic pain (OR 2.85) ([Mungia 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdent.2025.106147)), but no study has related it to a validated dental anxiety score in endodontic patients. Two special groups merit a resident's awareness. Trauma history is common and usually undisclosed ([Raja 2014](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.2013.30)), and among women with a history of childhood sexual abuse 28% reported memories of the abuse during dental treatment ([Leeners 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychores.2006.11.009)). Carriers of MC1R red-hair variants report more dental anxiety and are more than twice as likely to avoid care ([Binkley 2009](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.2009.0283)), yet the only block trial, in women with healthy pulps, found no difference in anesthetic success ([Droll 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2012.08.014)).

### Five MDAS questions outperform clinical intuition

The Modified Dental Anxiety Scale is the pragmatic chairside screen. It has five items scored 5–25, a published cut-off of **19 for high anxiety** with population norms, and, unlike the original Corah scale, an item on the local anesthetic injection ([Humphris 2009](https://doi.org/10.1186/1472-6831-9-20); [Humphris 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7584581/)). Internal consistency and test-retest reliability are both 0.93 ([Newton 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15819115/)), and in pulpitis patients the score correlated with dentist-rated anxiety during treatment (0.31) and with pain during treatment (0.43) ([Dou 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-018-0563-x)). Bands below 19 are not standardized (≥11, ≥15 and 10–18 are all in use), so "moderate anxiety" rates are not comparable between studies. When time is short, a 100-mm anxiety VAS is a validated one-item alternative, with roughly 50 mm marking clinically meaningful anxiety ([Facco 2011](https://doi.org/10.2344/0003-3006-58.1.8); [Facco 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23860442/)), and a brief categorical fear rating predicted anesthetic failure in the PBRN cohort ([Liu 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.70171)). The IDAF-4C+ has strong psychometrics, but its phobia module agreed very poorly with a clinical phobia diagnosis (kappa 0.02) ([Svensson 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/eos.12731)). Conventional score bands for the Dental Fear Survey, the Corah scale and the IDAF-4C circulate widely, but the notes could not verify them against a source, so they are omitted here.

Formal screening is worth the two minutes. Dentists' recognition of anxiety in endodontic patients correlated only moderately with patient report (rho 0.46) ([Perković 2014](https://doi.org/10.15644/asc47/4/3)), completing the MDAS in the waiting room did not raise state anxiety in a 1,004-patient trial ([Humphris 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16829885/)), and handing the score to the dentist reduced state anxiety in highly anxious general-practice patients ([Dailey 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12097444/); [Hally 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28619274/)). Physiological measures are stress markers, not substitutes: a pre-treatment video lowered self-reported anxiety in 160 endodontic patients without changing blood pressure or heart rate ([Craveiro 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2020.03.024)), and salivary cortisol tracked anxiety in one endodontic study and not in another ([Mumcu 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jds.2025.10.019); [Ercan 2026](https://doi.org/10.1159/000547716)). Heart rate peaks at the injection and at access, which identifies the two moments where management matters most ([Pandey 2025](https://doi.org/10.5005/jp-journals-10024-3828)).

## Anxiety tracks with pain and anesthetic failure, but causation remains unproven

The anticipation gap is the most robust finding in this literature. Among 333 US endodontic patients, **43% anticipated high pain but only 22% experienced it**, and the authors concluded that patients "perceive each ET experience as new" regardless of endodontic history ([Watkins 2002](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.2002.0020)). Ninety-two percent of pulpectomy patients said procedural pain was less than they anticipated ([Rousseau 2002](https://doi.org/10.1097/00004770-200202000-00015)); mean pain during treatment was 1.2 on a 0–10 scale with 54% feeling none ([Segura-Egea 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2591.2009.01562.x)); and in a 2026 Iowa cohort of mandibular molars treated with a planned supplemental protocol, 73% reported no procedural pain and 65% rated the experience better than expected ([Kopec 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/aej.70107)). Anxiety is tied more closely to expected pain than to experienced pain, and in one study the fear effect on injection pain disappeared once expected pain entered the model ([Öksüzer 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09671-y)). That makes expectation the proximal clinical target, which is the researchers' inference from a single mediation analysis.

Anxious patients nonetheless report more pain during treatment. In 180 Spanish patients, anxiety correlated with intraoperative pain (R = 0.41) and was associated with moderate or intense pain at **OR 4.0 (95% CI 1.7–9.3)** ([Murillo-Benítez 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.13245)); the notes flag that the abstract's own summary ("more than twice as likely") understates its reported odds ratio. A meta-regression across dental procedures found dental anxiety predicted expected, procedural and post-treatment pain ([Lin 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022034516678168)). The endodontic systematic review, however, contains only four observational studies and rates certainty low to very low ([Farias 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00784-023-05181-1)), and at least one endodontic study found anxiety correlated with expected but not actual pain ([Perković 2014](https://doi.org/10.15644/asc47/4/3)). Postoperatively, apprehension before treatment was one of three determinants of post-instrumentation pain in a 588-patient multicentre trial ([Torabinejad 1994](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0099-2399%2806%2980098-1)), and two recent cohorts point the same way with small effects ([Vitali 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.14137); [Sağlam 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-08715-7)). After root-end surgery, each point on the Pain Catastrophizing Scale raised the odds of a non-resolving 7-day pain trajectory (OR 1.11) ([Erdogan 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpain.2026.106273)).

Fear also predicts anesthetic failure, with an important caveat about definitions. In the PBRN cohort, 16% of 1,723 root canal patients reported intraoperative pain ≥3/10, and pre-treatment fear was the only significant psychosocial predictor, while treatment by an endodontist was protective ([Liu 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.70171)). In the active arms of an ibuprofen premedication trial, patients with successful blocks had a median MDAS of 8 versus 15 in those whose blocks failed ([de Oliveira 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00784-024-05765-5)). Because failure in these studies is defined by patient-reported pain, part of the association reflects pain reporting rather than failed nerve blockade, and no study used an objective pulpal endpoint stratified by anxiety. One 52-patient study reports success of "84.2%" in low-anxiety and "15.8%" in moderate-to-high-anxiety patients ([Mumcu 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jds.2025.10.019)); those figures sum to 100% and may describe how successful cases were distributed across groups, so they should not be quoted as success rates until the full text is checked. A randomized comparison of articaine and bupivacaine concluded that efficacy "did not appear to be influenced by the degree of anxiety" ([Manteca-Fernández 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/dj13050199)). The causal question stays open: a systematic review of four trials found "insufficient evidence" that pharmacological anxiolysis changes intraoperative endodontic pain ([Silva 2023](https://doi.org/10.14744/eej.2022.83097)).

Memory explains why a good visit does not cure the fear. Pre-treatment emotional state predicted how unpleasant patients remembered their root canal to be 18 months later (adjusted R² 0.62), whereas the pain actually felt was the predictor of what they remembered at one week ([Gedney 2003](https://doi.org/10.1054/jpai.2003.3)). Remembered dental pain drifts back toward expected pain, most strongly in anxious patients ([Kent 1985](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-3959%2885%2990288-X)), and anxious patients' predictions return to their original inaccurate level when disconfirming experiences are "few and far between" ([Arntz 1990](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-7967%2890%2990052-k)). Self-reported anxiety does fall after treatment, by about 30% in pooled data that mostly compare different studies before and after ([Khan 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2016.08.007)), yet prior endodontic experience does not reliably predict lower anxiety next time.

On behaviour and outcomes the evidence thins rapidly. Just over half of pulpitis patients reported postponing dental visits, and postponers were more anxious ([Dou 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-018-0563-x)). At population level, 29.2% of very fearful Australians showed the full cycle of delayed visiting, poor oral health and symptom-driven attendance, against 11.6% of the non-fearful ([Armfield 2007](https://doi.org/10.1186/1472-6831-7-1)). No study reports missed appointments or non-completion in endodontic practice as a function of measured anxiety, and none relates anxiety to periapical healing or tooth survival with adequate methods. The one paper reporting a lower "success rate" in anxious patients (85% vs 65%) does not specify its anxiety scale, success criteria or follow-up, and the claim that anxiety lowers endodontic success should not be treated as established ([Ahmed 2024](https://doi.org/10.4103/jpbs.jpbs_371_24)).

## Profound anesthesia is the most endodontic-specific anxiety intervention

Patients rate the dentist's "explicit dedication to prevent pain" as the single most anxiety-reducing behaviour ([Corah 1988](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.1988.0162)), and painful or helpless treatment experiences are the best-documented precipitants of dental fear: people reporting painful, frightening and embarrassing dental experiences were 22 times more likely to be dentally anxious ([Locker 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8763138/)). The link from intraoperative endodontic pain to later anxiety rests on retrospective association, not longitudinal proof, but it is the mechanistic case for treating anesthesia as anxiety management.

### Slow, warm injections help; buffering and thinner needles do not

No device abolishes injection pain, and every adjunct has a modest effect. Slow deposition has the best support: a 60-second block was more comfortable than a 15-second one and produced more pulpal anesthesia in a double-blind crossover of 38 volunteers ([Kanaa 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2006.04.004)), a point the AAE endorses ([AAE Winter 2009](https://www.aae.org/specialty/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2017/07/winter09ecfe.pdf)). Warming the cartridge cut infiltration pain from 35 to 15 mm on a 100-mm scale in a split-mouth trial ([Aravena 2018](https://doi.org/10.2147/LRA.S147288)), and three of four studies in a systematic review agree ([Tirupathi 2020](https://doi.org/10.17245/jdapm.2020.20.4.187)). Topical benzocaine reduced needle-insertion pain only for maxillary anterior injections in 2,336 injections, not for the block ([Nusstein & Beck 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14959903/)), although in apical surgery patients a lidocaine–prilocaine topical blunted the extra pain that high-MDAS patients otherwise reported ([Cho 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2016.10.036)). Vibration devices lowered injection pain in several small, often unblinded trials and failed in one ([Salma 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijom.2020.07.010); [Erdogan 2018](https://doi.org/10.2344/anpr-65-02-01)), and computer-controlled delivery lowers pain by about 9 points on a 100-point scale with inconsistent effects on anxiety ([Libonati 2018](https://doi.org/10.23804/ejpd.2018.19.04.14)). Buffering does not reduce intraoral injection pain in adults ([Aulestia-Viera 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.12899)), and needle gauge made no difference across 930 injections ([Flanagan 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17511363/)), with one exception: a 31-gauge needle markedly reduces the pain of the intrapulpal injection ([Suresh 2020](https://doi.org/10.14744/eej.2020.38358)). Combining topical or precooling, a warmed cartridge and slow deposition into a bundle is the researchers' inference; the bundle itself is untested.

### Half of blocks fail in the hot tooth, so plan the supplement and premedicate

The AAE states that the inferior alveolar nerve block alone succeeds only **19–56% of the time** in irreversible pulpitis, that lip numbness does not guarantee pulpal anesthesia, and that a patient who reports previous difficulty getting numb is more likely to fail again ([AAE Winter 2009](https://www.aae.org/specialty/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2017/07/winter09ecfe.pdf)). Ohio State data put block success at 25–28% for molars, with a supplemental articaine buccal infiltration rescuing only 42–48% of those failures ([Fowler 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2015.12.025)). A positive cold test after the block strongly indicates failure, and a negative one should be confirmed with electric pulp testing ([Afkhami 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2024.10.013)). Articaine's advantage over lidocaine lies in the supplemental infiltration (OR 3.55) rather than in the block itself ([Kung 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2015.07.001)). Intraosseous injection succeeds in roughly 86–91% of cases and intraligamentary injection in about 75%, rising to about 95% with re-injection, according to the AAE summary ([AAE Winter 2009](https://www.aae.org/specialty/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2017/07/winter09ecfe.pdf)); a network meta-analysis ranks supplemental intraosseous injection first, followed by buccal plus lingual articaine with preoperative NSAIDs, at very low to moderate confidence ([Zanjir 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2019.09.002)).

Oral premedication is the cheapest gain available. NSAIDs raise block success with a risk ratio of 1.92 in a trial-sequential meta-analysis the authors call conclusive ([Nagendrababu 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2018.02.017)); the newest synthesis in adults with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis reports pain-free blocks in **64.6% versus 28.4%** (RR 2.06), rates certainty low, and warns that "clinicians should still anticipate supplemental anesthesia" ([Sabeti 2026](https://doi.org/10.17245/jdapm.2026.26.122)). Oral dexamethasone performs equivalently (RR 1.80, moderate certainty against placebo) ([Suresh 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.70162)), and adding acetaminophen to an NSAID adds nothing for block success ([Pulikkotil 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/iej.12912)). Benzodiazepines belong in a different category: the two benzodiazepine trials pooled to a risk ratio of 0.99 for block success ([Lapidus 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.adaj.2016.01.006)). The ordering most consistent with this evidence is premedication, a slow block, a 15-minute wait, pulp testing, articaine infiltration, then intraosseous or intraligamentary injection, with intrapulpal injection as a warned last resort. That sequence is the researchers' integration of AAE guidance and the network rankings; no single trial tests the whole pathway. Telling the patient beforehand that extra numbing is routine in an inflamed tooth turns the supplemental injection from an alarming failure into an expected step, which is an inference from the helplessness and unpredictability literature rather than a tested script.

### Accurate postoperative expectations are cheap, and the numbers support optimism

Pain prevalence falls from 81% before treatment to **40% at 24 hours and 11% at one week**, and mean severity from 54 to 24 to 5 on a 100-point scale ([Pak & White 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2010.12.016)). About 5% of patients have persistent pain of any cause after endodontic procedures ([Nixdorf 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2009.11.007)), and in a PBRN cohort optimism about the procedure cut the odds of pain at six months (OR 0.39) ([Nixdorf 2016](https://doi.org/10.1097/j.pain.0000000000000343)). An endodontic network meta-analysis found NSAIDs alone or with acetaminophen reduce pain by about 21–22 points at 6–8 hours, while opioids and corticosteroids did not significantly help ([Zanjir 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2020.07.002)). The ADA 2024 guideline recommends non-opioids first-line, but its formal scope is extraction pain and temporary toothache management, so applying it after root canal treatment is an extension ([ADA 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.adaj.2023.10.009)). A 0.5% bupivacaine block reduced pain at 6 and 12 hours and analgesic use against lidocaine in a 60-patient endodontic trial ([Parirokh 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2012.04.012)). Whether counselling patients with these numbers lowers fear has not been tested in endodontic patients; the supporting trial is an analogue experiment in which 437 subjects who read positive, accurate information about endodontic pain reported less fear of it ([van Wijk & Hoogstraten 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2591.2006.01090.x)).

## Among behavioural options, only CBT changes the fear itself

The most comprehensive synthesis of adult trials (173 randomized trials) separates two purposes that clinicians often blur. For trait anxiety, **CBT reduces dental anxiety with moderate certainty (SMD −0.65)**, and for dental phobia the pooled effects are −0.43 to −0.48. For state anxiety during treatment, only hypnosis reaches moderate certainty, with a small effect (SMD −0.31) measured in oral surgery, and the evidence "does not support" virtual reality distraction, music, aromatherapy, video information or acupuncture ([Steenen 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38945067/)). The per-intervention estimates behind those negative verdicts could not be retrieved from the abstract, and it is unknown whether any endodontic trial entered the pooled analyses. An earlier meta-analysis of 29 trials found non-pharmacological interventions reduced mental distress (g = 0.58) but not pain (g = 0.00) ([Burghardt 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29154798/)).

CBT works in brief and scalable formats, though none has been tested in endodontic patients. One-session and five-session exposure were equally effective at reducing avoidance, and 77% of treated phobic patients sought care in the following year ([Haukebø 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18005932/)). In a UK service evaluation, **79% of patients went on to have dental treatment without sedation** after a mean of five CBT appointments ([Kani 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26611310/)). Online CBT supported by dental staff matched psychology-staff delivery in a 499-patient, 12-month trial ([Tellez 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40514778/)). Most CBT trials use wait-list or information controls, which inflates effect sizes, and the lead time CBT needs is a poor fit for symptomatic pulpitis.

Communication evidence favours what is said about pain over how much procedural detail is delivered. A sensory-focus instruction before root canal therapy reduced pain only in patients with high desire for control and low felt control, and adding procedural information contributed nothing ([Logan 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8552739/)). Information videos and leaflets have not beaten an ordinary verbal explanation in endodontic or mixed-procedure trials ([Anatürk 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40506422/); [Plachý 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40151554/)), a nine-trial review in third-molar surgery agrees ([Souza 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36525143/)), and graphic audiovisual information increased anxiety before third-molar and implant surgery ([Akomolafe 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36493800/); [Camacho-Alonso 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31639265/)). Stop signals are ethically expected and low-risk, but their evidence is inconsistent: a 1978 study found more arousal with a signalling device, and the positive endodontic studies are small, with one device patented by an author ([Corah 1978](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/277503/); [Singh 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22368336/)). No adult trial of tell-show-do or of clinician empathy training with patient anxiety outcomes was found.

Music is the most studied endodontic adjunct and illustrates the quality problem. A systematic review of five endodontic trials favoured music for state anxiety and vital signs at **very low certainty** ([Troian-Michel 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37728616/)); across the individual endodontic trials, roughly half show a benefit on at least one outcome and half are null, with cortisol unchanged in both trials that measured it. Across invasive dental procedures the pooled effect in adults is SMD −0.51 at low certainty, and **patient-selected music (SMD −1.01) outperforms researcher-selected music (−0.24)** ([Shih 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11546414/)). In dentally anxious patients, brief relaxation beat music head-to-head and music had no clinically relevant effect in the highly anxious ([Lahmann 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18310736/)). In the only endodontic comparison, an immersive VR headset performed no better than an inexpensive audio-guided breathing and body-scan track, with no untreated control to show whether either did anything ([Mintz 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41334019/)). Adult VR meta-analyses conflict, from a non-significant SMD −0.35 to −0.69, and a corrigendum to the larger estimate was not reviewed ([Nezhad 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39535423/); [Cao 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42801915/)). Lavender or rose scent lowered anxiety in two endodontic trials from one research group and did nothing in a third from another ([Jadhav 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32901242/); [Mittal 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39113957/); [Mousavi 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40497523/)), and the dedicated systematic review grades aromatherapy low to very low ([Cai 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32267044/)). Auricular acupuncture beat sham by about 3.6 state-anxiety points in the largest trial ([Michalek-Sauberer 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22219023/)), has no endodontic data, and requires needles in patients whose dominant fear is the needle.

Two integrity warnings apply. The only meta-analysis pooling non-pharmacological trials for root canal anxiety (Guo and colleagues, PLoS One 2025) **has been retracted** and is not used here ([retraction notice](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0357077)), and the first dental VR meta-analysis was retracted and republished with significant effects in children only ([López-Valverde 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32987885/)). More broadly, pre-to-post designs in this field capitalize on the natural fall in anxiety once treatment ends, so only between-group differences are interpretable, and most trials enrol unselected, mildly anxious patients. They show whether an intervention makes an ordinary visit more comfortable, not whether it enables treatment of a phobic patient.

## Drugs carry the patient through one visit, not past the fear

Oral benzodiazepines reduce state anxiety with low-certainty pooled evidence (SMD −0.43) ([Steenen 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.janxdis.2024.102891)). The only endodontic-specific anxiolysis trial randomized 79 anxious endodontic patients and found **triazolam 0.25 mg superior to diazepam 5 mg and placebo** for anxiety, with more amnesia and more cognitive impairment; the low diazepam dose confounds the drug comparison ([Ehrich 1997](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0099-2399%2897%2980272-5)). Alprazolam reduced anxiety during oral surgery only at 0.75 mg, a dose that also impaired memory ([Coldwell 1997](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0278-2391%2897%2990280-9)). The benzodiazepine does not fix the block: success was 43% with sublingual triazolam versus 57% with placebo ([Lindemann 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2008.07.013)) and 53% versus 40% with alprazolam ([Khademi 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2012.06.007)), neither difference significant. Both trials were small and did not select anxious patients, so a modest effect in either direction is not excluded, but there is no signal of benefit.

The triazolam label sets the adult dose at 0.25 mg with a maximum of 0.5 mg, a geriatric start of 0.125 mg, peak levels within two hours, contraindication with strong CYP3A inhibitors such as ketoconazole, itraconazole and ritonavir, caution with macrolides and grapefruit juice, and a boxed warning for concomitant opioids ([Halcion label](https://labeling.pfizer.com/ShowLabeling.aspx?id=586)). Equivalent label figures for alprazolam, diazepam, lorazepam and oral midazolam were not verified. Stacked dosing is hazardous: incremental sublingual triazolam totalling 1.0 mg produced sedation scores consistent with deep sedation in 8 of 10 volunteers ([Jackson 2006](https://doi.org/10.1097/01.jcp.0000186742.07148.da)). Because effective doses are amnestic, obtain consent before dosing and give written instructions to the escort; this is a practice inference, and no US guideline text stating an explicit escort rule for single-dose oral anxiolysis was found.

Nitrous oxide is the one anxiolytic with randomized evidence of improving block success in symptomatic irreversible pulpitis: **50% versus 28%** in 100 emergency patients ([Stanley 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2012.02.010)). It is a single trial with a low placebo rate, and later studies from the same group used nitrous oxide in every arm; they show block success of only 44–58% even with it, rising to 80% after two sets of supplemental intraligamentary injections ([Chen 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2021.08.013)). That its analgesic action rather than anxiolysis explains the difference from benzodiazepines is the researchers' inference. Adult anxiolysis data are thin, with one 60-patient endodontic trial reporting lower MDAS and injection pain without effect sizes ([Gupta 2019](https://doi.org/10.4103/JCD.JCD_332_18)). Recovery is rapid and psychomotor function is unimpaired afterwards ([Thompson 1999](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.bdj.4800330)). Contraindications were verified only from a pediatric AAPD document, which lists bowel obstruction, recent retinal surgery and untreated vitamin B12 deficiency ([AAPD](https://www.aapd.org/globalassets/media/policies_guidelines/bp_useofnitrous26.pdf)); commonly taught adult cautions such as first-trimester pregnancy and severe COPD were not confirmed against a source.

IV moderate sedation is in demand but rarely delivered by endodontists. About half of patients at a graduate endodontic clinic wanted sedation if available ([Huh 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2014.10.009)), yet in a 2018 survey of 616 AAE members only **4% provided IV sedation themselves**, 26% used another provider and 10% felt adequately trained ([Yehia 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2018.07.004)); 13 of 31 responding US program directors offered IV-sedation education ([Yehia 2018, J Dent Educ](https://doi.org/10.21815/JDE.018.077)). Efficacy data come from third-molar surgery, including a 997-patient multicentre trial in which all midazolam-based regimens beat placebo ([Dionne 2001](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.archive.2001.0271)); no randomized trial of IV sedation exists in adult endodontic patients. In one graduate program 15% of moderate sedations were prompted by local anesthetic failure ([Setty 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2014.05.015)), a scenario in which sedation manages distress without correcting the anesthesia. Alternatives to benzodiazepines have not earned a place: oral CBD-rich extract before endodontic treatment did not reduce anxiety ([de Andrade Silva 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2024.10.010)), propranolol failed in a quadruple-blind trial ([Steenen 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2022.842353)), melatonin results are inconsistent to negative ([Rosa 2026](https://doi.org/10.4317/medoral.27846)), and the positive signals for pregabalin and herbal agents come from small oral-surgery trials ([Nutt 2009](https://doi.org/10.1177/0269881108094722)).

Sedation does not treat the fear. In 50 dental phobics, a single benzodiazepine dose and one session of psychological treatment both reduced anxiety during surgery, but the benzodiazepine group relapsed and only **20% continued dental care versus 70%** after psychological treatment ([Thom 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037//0022-006x.68.3.378)). Ten-year attendance was 91.6% after behavioural therapy against 33.3% after general anesthesia in a small cohort ([Hakeberg 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7645792/)). Repeated nitrous oxide within a supportive programme matched cognitive therapy at five years in one trial ([Willumsen 2003](https://doi.org/10.1080/00016350310001442)). These studies are small, European, and several used patient-preference allocation.

### State rules, not ADA guidance, define what a resident may do

The ADA sedation guidelines were revised (adopted October 2025, released April 2026) and state that "dentists must comply with their state laws" and that "level of sedation is entirely independent of the route of administration". Enteral minimal sedation may not exceed the FDA maximum recommended dose for unmonitored home use; exceeding it in a single appointment "is considered to be moderate sedation". Minimal sedation monitoring must include pulse oximetry, and moderate sedation requires ACLS, supplemental oxygen, continuous pulse oximetry, end-tidal CO2 and continuous ECG unless precluded ([ADA 2025](https://www.ada.org/-/media/project/ada-organization/ada/ada-org/files/resources/library/oral-health-topics/ada_sedation_use_guidelines.pdf); [ADA News](https://adanews.ada.org/ada-news/2026/april/ada-releases-updated-sedation-and-anesthesia-guidelines/)). Applying that definition to the triazolam label, a total above 0.5 mg in an adult is moderate sedation however awake the patient appears; this is the researchers' application of the two documents, not a statement found in either. A 2025 review of state rules found 43 states require a moderate sedation permit, 12 require ACLS even for minimal sedation and 27 require capnography, with "substantial variation" from ADA guidance ([Simon 2025](https://doi.org/10.1097/PTS.0000000000001320)); that review most likely analysed the 2016 ADA edition, and its 60-hour, 20-patient training figure may reflect the earlier teaching guidelines. Individual state rules were not checked. No AAE position statement on sedation or on managing the anxious patient was located; AAE material consists of practice articles and educational newsletters.

## A stepped chairside approach, synthesized rather than validated

The table below condenses the strength of evidence. Certainty labels reproduce a source's GRADE rating where one exists and are otherwise the note-takers' judgement.

| Oral dexamethasone premedication | RR 1.80 vs placebo | — | Moderate | Alternative to NSAID |
| Planned supplemental anesthesia (articaine infiltration, intraosseous, intraligamentary) | Network meta-analysis; AAE guidance | — | Very low to moderate | Core of the visit; test before access |
| Slow, warmed injection; vibration or computer-controlled delivery | Mostly volunteer trials | Small effects (≈5–20 mm/100) | Low | Slow and warm routinely; devices optional |
| Buffering, thinner needles for comfort | Null in pulpitis trials | Null pooled effect | Low–moderate | Do not rely on |
| NSAID ± acetaminophen after treatment; bupivacaine | Network meta-analysis; small bupivacaine trials | ADA 2024 guideline (different scope) | Very low to moderate | First-line; no opioids |

| Accurate positive pain information; sensory focus | Two older trials (fear-of-pain and pain outcomes) | — | Low | Use; more detail is not better |

Published frameworks converge on one architecture: assess every patient, give everyone a base layer of behavioural measures, add adjuncts for moderate anxiety, and reserve CBT and sedation for severe or phobic patients. Scottish guidance calls anxiety control "both a right for the patient and a duty placed on the dentist", requires "the simplest and safest effective technique", and advises choosing the right technique at the outset "rather than progress through a range of techniques likely to fail" ([SDCEP 2017](https://www.sdcep.org.uk/media/iegenn4c/sdcep-conscious-sedation-guidance.pdf)). The reviews agree that mild and moderate anxiety is usually managed psychologically with occasional anxiolysis, and that phobic patients need combined approaches ([Appukuttan 2016](https://doi.org/10.2147/CCIDE.S63626); [Newton 2012](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.bdj.2012.830)). Trauma-informed care adds "universal precautions" that need no disclosure: preview the appointment, narrate sensations before they happen, agree a hand-raise stop signal, and never treat reassurance as assessment ([Raja 2014](https://doi.org/10.14219/jada.2013.30)). The Indicator of Sedation Need combines anxiety, medical and behavioural factors and treatment complexity ([Coulthard 2011](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.bdj.2011.725)), but its scoring thresholds could not be verified and the notes hold conflicting versions. The same caution applies to the MDAS bands in NHS England's standards, which were read through a text extractor and overlap at 10, to the tier details of Newton 2012, and to the German S3 guideline, known here only through trade-press summaries.

The protocol below is the researchers' integration of those frameworks with the endodontic anesthesia evidence. It is not a published algorithm, no trial has tested it as a bundle, and its MDAS bands below 19 are approximate.

| Tier | Who | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Every patient | All new patients | MDAS plus three questions: any trouble getting numb before, what worries you most (injection, numbness, pain, loss of control), and what has helped. State explicitly that you will test the tooth before starting and stop to add anesthetic if anything is felt. Agree and honour a stop signal. Slow, warmed injection with topical where it works. Give accurate intraoperative and postoperative expectations. Offer the patient's own music. Close with a predicted-versus-actual pain debrief and a written postoperative plan. |
| Moderate anxiety or situational risk | MDAS roughly 11–18, hot mandibular molar, prior anesthetic failure, marked needle fear, high catastrophizing or chronic pain history | Add a longer or staged appointment with breaks; a 5–10-minute breathing or relaxation exercise before the injection; NSAID or dexamethasone premedication; announced, planned supplemental anesthesia; bupivacaine for the first 6–12 hours; nitrous oxide or a single-dose oral benzodiazepine within state rules. |
| Severe or phobic | MDAS ≥19, or the tier above has failed | If urgent, stabilize with pulpotomy or pulpectomy under the best achievable anesthesia, with sedation if needed, and defer definitive care. Document the justification for sedation. Refer for, or assign, CBT with the goal of treatment under local anesthesia. Arrange moderate sedation through a permit holder or anesthesia provider. Reserve general anesthesia for when behavioural and sedation routes are insufficient. |

Several elements of this table are theory-driven suggestions without trial support, and should be presented to patients and supervisors as such. The end-of-visit debrief follows from the memory-reconstruction studies but has never been tested. Staging an emergency hot tooth as a short pain-relieving visit followed by scheduled definitive care fits the finding that procedures under 45 minutes are more often pain-free ([Segura-Egea 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2591.2009.01562.x)), yet the only study of single- versus multiple-visit treatment with an anxiety outcome is too poorly reported to use. Rubber dam is generally well accepted, with negative opinions linked to prolonged use and slower placement by students ([Stewardson & McHugh 2002](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2591.2002.00571.x)); no study addresses dam-related claustrophobia or gagging in adult endodontic patients. A brief invitation to voice worries before starting removed the excess pain reported by catastrophizers in a hygiene-treatment trial in undergraduates ([Sullivan & Neish 1999](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0304-3959%2898%2900163-8)), which is suggestive rather than endodontic evidence.

## Conclusion

The anesthesia protocol and the communication protocol turn out to be the same script. Saying "I will not start until we have tested that the tooth is numb, and I will add more if you feel anything" is both the behaviour patients rate as most anxiety-reducing and an accurate description of the workflow the hot-tooth evidence demands. For a resident this reframes block failure in irreversible pulpitis as an expected event to be planned for and announced, rather than an operator error to be pushed through; a single very painful visit can undo the benefit of many uneventful ones, so the threshold to stop and re-anesthetize should be low. The second shift is to match the tool to the purpose. Anesthesia, expectation-setting, relaxation, nitrous oxide and benzodiazepines manage today's visit; only CBT and repeated, predictable, low-anxiety exposure change whether the patient returns, and anxious patients' memories revert toward what they expected unless good visits are frequent and explicitly reinforced.

The evidence gaps are specific and addressable from within a residency program. No trial has tested CBT, a multi-component bundle, or a post-visit debrief in endodontic patients; no study follows the same patients' anxiety across the visits of a multi-visit treatment; none uses an objective pulpal anesthesia endpoint stratified by anxiety; and none links anxiety to healing or tooth survival. With IV sedation training absent from most programs and no US guideline to cite, the practical levers available to a resident today are a two-minute screen, a rehearsed anesthesia pathway and nitrous oxide, with state rules as the binding constraint on anything pharmacological beyond that.
